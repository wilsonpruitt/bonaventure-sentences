# Commentarius in librum Sapientiae — Prooemium
## *Introductio generalis · De quadruplici causa*

---

## Latin
<!-- page 107 -->

### Prooemium commentarii in Sapientiam.

#### Introductio generalis.

1. *Diligite lumen sapientiae, omnes, qui praeestis populis*, Sapientiae sexto[^p107-1]. Quoniam eodem habet res formari, et deformata reformari; et omnia leguntur per sapientiam esse facta, secundum illud Psalmi: *Omnia in sapientia fecisti*; necesse est, omnia per sapientiam reformari. Imago ergo rationalis animae, quae « ad totius sapientiae similitudinem legitur esse facta » in libro de Spiritu et anima, per peccatum vero postmodum deformata in tantum, ut merito possit quaeri: *Cuius est imago et superscriptio haec*[^p107-2]? per sapientiam est necessario reformanda. Item[^p107-3], quia ad eius reformationem duo sunt necessaria, scilicet *malitiae expulsio* et perfecti *boni introductio*, ut eo informetur et ei cuius est imago, expresse conformetur; sapientia autem est *malitiae expulsiva*, unde Sapientiae septimo[^p107-4]: *Sapientia vincit malitiam*; item perfecti *boni introductiva*, quia « sapientia est, in qua perfecti boni forma consistit », ut dicit Hugo in libro de Origine artium[^p107-5]. Et quoniam imago divina, per peccatum in anima deformata, reformatione indiget secundum omnes tres suas partes, quae secundum Augustinum[^p107-6] sunt intelligentia, memoria et voluntas; idcirco Sapiens ad sapientiam tripliciter allicit et invitat, scilicet ad sapientiae *studiosam acquisitionem*, et hoc quantum ad intelligentiam; ad acquisitae sapientiae *firmam retentionem*, et hoc quantum ad memoriam; ad acquisitae sapientiae et memoriter retentae *dilectionem*, et hoc quantum ad voluntatem.

2. Ad sapientiae *studiosam acquisitionem*; Proverbiorum vigesimo septimo[^p107-7]: *Stude sapientiae, fili mi. Filium* vocat, quemcumque paterna affectione informat, sive sit iuvenis, sive senex; Augustinus: « Ad discendum quod opus est nulla mihi aetas sera videri potest; quia, etsi senes magis docere deceat quam doceri, magis tamen decet eos discere, quam quid doceant ignorare ». *Stude*, inquit, et hoc innuit vehementem diligentiam circa acquisitionem sapientiae adhibendam. « Studium enim, ut dicit Tullius[^p107-8], est vehemens applicatio animi cum summa voluptate ad aliquid agendum ». Haec autem vehemens animi applicatio requirit vacationem ab exterioribus occupationibus. « Impar enim efficitur ad singula qui confusa mente dividitur ad multa », ut dicit Gregorius. Propter quod Ecclesiastici trigesimo octavo[^p107-9]: *Sapientiam scribe in tempore vacuitatis*, id est vacationis ab opere exteriori; *qui enim minoratur actu*, scilicet exteriori, *percipiet sapientiam*, ut dicitur in eodem.

3. Ad sapientiae *firmam retentionem*; Ecclesiastici decimo quarto[^p107-10], ubi dicit: *Beatus vir, qui in sapientia morabitur*; item Proverbiorum tertio:
<!-- page 108 -->
*Describe eam in tabulis cordis tui*, id est in tribus partibus divinae imaginis, et specialiter in memoria, eam firmiter retinendo. Propter quod dicit: *Describe*, quod enim scribitur firmius et diutius retinetur; Proverbiorum vigesimo secundo: *Ecce, descripsi eam tibi tripliciter*, id est in *intelligentia* per studiosam acquisitionem, in *memoria* per firmam retentionem, in *voluntate* per *dilectionem*; ad quod monet in verbis propositis dicens: *Diligite lumen sapientiae* etc.

#### De quadruplici causa huius libri.

4. In quibus verbis nobis quatuor causae huius operis innuuntur, scilicet causa *efficiens* ex persona, quae loquitur dicens: *Diligite*; *formalis*, id est modus agendi, per *lumen* innuitur; *materialis* nomine *sapientiae* exprimitur; *finalis* ex ordine seu *comparatione loquentis ad personam*, ad quam sermo dirigitur, intelligi potest, cum dicitur: *Omnes, qui praeestis populis.*

5. De primo notandum, quod triplex est *causa efficiens* huius libri: prima per modum *inspirantis*, scilicet Deus; unde Iob trigesimo secundo[^p108-1]: *Inspiratio Omnipotentis dat intelligentiam*; item Ecclesiastici primo: *Omnis sapientia a Domino Deo est*, sicut omnis *essentia* ab eius essentia, et ab eius veritate omnis *veritas*, et ab eius bonitate omnis *bonitas*. « Primum enim in unoquoque genere eorum quae post ipsum sunt, causa est[^p108-2] »; et omne perfectum a perfecto causatur, secundum Boethium.

6. Secunda causa efficiens per modum *invenientis* est ipse Salomon; unde in ecclesiastica historia[^p108-3] « omnis antiquorum chorus librum, qui Sapientia intitulatur, Salomonis esse dixerunt ». Unde liber ipse *Sapientia Salomonis* inscribitur, et more ecclesiastico lectionibus de hoc libro sumtis praemittitur: « Dixit Salomon filiis Israel », quia scilicet de eius sententiis liber iste compilatus fuit. — Proxima causa efficiens per modum *compilantis* fuit Philo, sapientissimus Iudaeorum, qui temporibus Apostolorum fuit, ut refert ecclesiastica historia[^p108-4]; qui ab amore sapientiae *Philo* non immerito est appellatus. Rabanus etiam librum istum potius asserit non a Salomone, ut putatur, sed a Philone, sapientissimo Iudaeo, fuisse conscriptum, id est compilatum.

7. Causa autem *formalis*, id est modus agendi, ut dictum est, per *lumen* innuitur, quia, sicut lumen habet radicalis suae originis occultationem, in egressu claram manifestationem, in fluxu continuationem; sic et iste liber Sapientiae habet in sententiis profundam difficultatem et originis *occultationem*, secundum illud Iob vigesimo octavo[^p108-5]: *Trahitur sapientia de occultis*; in sermonibus claram *manifestationem*, Sapientiae sexto: *Clara est et quae nunquam marcescit sapientia, clara*, inquam, per eloquentiae venustatem; « stilus enim huius libri, secundum Hieronymum, Graecam redolet eloquentiam »; in processu vero rationum *consequentiam et ordinatam continuationem*, Ecclesiastici vigesimo primo[^p108-6]: *Scientia sapientis tanquam inundatio abundabit*, continue fluendo, non stillando; *et consilium illius sicut fons vitae permanet.*

8. Causa vero *materialis* nomine *sapientiae* exprimitur. « Sapientia autem, secundum Augustinum[^p108-7], est divinarum rerum cognitio »; res autem divinae sunt Dei *potentia*, *sapientia* et *bonitas*; de quibus agitur in hoc libro, quamvis principalius de *sapientia* intendatur. *Potentiam* Dei notificat in punitione
<!-- page 109 -->
rebellium, ut patet primo, secundo, tertio, quarto, quinto capitulis; *sapientiam* in illuminatione humilium, ut patet sexto, septimo, octavo, nono capitulis; *bonitatem* in collatione beneficiorum, quantum ad utrosque, ut patet a decimo capitulo et inde. Agitur autem hic non tantum de sapientia *increata*, eius laudibus insistendo, sed etiam de *creata*, eius utilitates ostendendo; unde infra sexto[^p109-1]: *Quid autem*, id est qualis sit, *sapientia*, scilicet increata; *et quemadmodum facta sit*, scilicet creata, *referam* etc.; quamvis illud totum possit intelligi de sapientia *increata* tanquam de subiecto. Merito ergo *liber Sapientiae* intitulatur, quia in eo de sapientia tanquam de subiecto vel de materia principaliter tractatur, sicut *liber de Anima*, quia in eo principaliter de anima determinatur.

9. Causa vero *finalis* intelligi potest ex ordine personae loquentis ad illos, ad quos dirigitur sermo; qui tanguntur ibi[^p109-2]: *Omnes, qui praeestis populis.* Sicut enim est in illuminationibus *angelicis*, quod divinae lucis radium, secundum beatum Dionysium, primo recipiunt Angeli de prima hierarchia, secundo illi de media, ultimo illi de postrema — « lex enim, ut ipse dicit[^p109-3], divinitus promulgata est, per prima media et per media postrema reducere », unde in Psalmo: *Illuminans tu mirabiliter a montibus aeternis*, id est superioribus Angelis inferiores — sicut etiam videmus in illuminationibus *corporeis*, quia primo derivatur solis illuminatio ad loca superiora et caelo viciniora, deinde in media, ultimo in postrema, Ecclesiastici quadragesimo tertio: *Sol enim, tripliciter exurens montes, radios exsufflat*, scilicet in valles: sic etiam debet esse in illuminationibus *humanis*, ut primo in supremos, id est in praelatos, deinde in medios, id est in eorum ministros, tertio in postremos, id est eorum subditos, lumen sapientiae diffundatur, secundum illud Psalmi[^p109-4]: *Suscipiant montes pacem populo* etc. Hic enim est processus sapientiae. *Attingit enim a fine*, scilicet supremo, *usque ad finem* ultimum, *et disponit omnia*, scilicet media, *suaviter*, infra capitulo octavo. Propter hoc dicebat Plato, « beatas vel bonas fore respublicas, si eas sapientes regerent, aut earum rectores sapientiae studerent », sicut ait Boethius libro de Consolatione[^p109-5]. *Secundum* enim *iudicem populi, sic ministri eius; et qualis rector civitatis, tales et inhabitantes in ea*; ubi tangit tres ordines, scilicet iudicum, ministrorum et subditorum. Propter praedicta auctor iste primo et principaliter intendit diffundere lumen sapientiae in loca excelsa, id est in superiores et per eos in inferiores. Unde patet, causam finalem huius doctrinae esse exhortationem seu provocationem principum et praelatorum, et per consequens omnium, ad studium et amorem sapientiae.

## English
<!-- page 107 -->

### Prooemium of the commentary on Wisdom.

#### General introduction.

1. *Love the lumen*[^tr-lumen] *of wisdom, all ye that rule over peoples*, Wisdom, the sixth chapter[^p107-1]. Since a thing has its being formed and, once deformed, its being re-formed from the same source; and all things are read to have been made through wisdom, according to that word of the Psalm: *Thou hast made all things in wisdom*; it is necessary that all things be re-formed through wisdom. The image of the rational soul, therefore, which « is read to have been made to the likeness of the whole of wisdom » in the book *On the Spirit and the Soul*, but afterwards through sin so deformed that it may rightly be asked: *Whose is this image and inscription*[^p107-2]? must of necessity be re-formed through wisdom. Likewise[^p107-3], because for its re-formation two things are necessary, namely the *expulsion of malice* and the *introduction* of perfect *good*, so that it may be informed by this and be expressly conformed to him whose image it is; and wisdom is *expulsive of malice*, whence Wisdom, the seventh chapter[^p107-4]: *Wisdom overcometh malice*; likewise *introductive* of perfect *good*, because « wisdom is that in which the form of the perfect good consists », as Hugh says in the book *On the Origin of the Arts*[^p107-5]. And since the divine image, deformed in the soul through sin, needs re-formation according to all three of its parts, which according to Augustine[^p107-6] are intelligence, memory and will; therefore the Wise Man entices and invites to wisdom in three ways, namely to the *studious*[^tr-studium] *acquisition* of wisdom, and this as regards intelligence; to the *firm retention* of wisdom acquired, and this as regards memory; to the *love* of wisdom acquired and retained in memory, and this as regards the will.

2. To the *studious acquisition* of wisdom; Proverbs, the twenty-seventh chapter[^p107-7]: *Be studious of wisdom, my son. Son* he calls whomever he informs with fatherly affection, whether he be young or old; Augustine: « For learning what is needful no age can seem to me too late; because, although it befits old men rather to teach than to be taught, yet it befits them more to learn than to be ignorant of what they teach ». *Be studious*, he says, and by this he suggests that vehement diligence is to be applied to the acquisition of wisdom. « For study, as Tully says[^p107-8], is the vehement application of the mind, with the highest pleasure, to doing something ». But this vehement application of the mind requires freedom[^tr-vacare] from exterior occupations. « For he is made unequal to single things who is divided in a confused mind among many », as Gregory says. On which account Ecclesiasticus, the thirty-eighth chapter[^p107-9]: *Write wisdom in the time of leisure*, that is, of freedom from exterior work; *for he that is less in action*, namely exterior action, *shall receive wisdom*, as is said in the same place.

3. To the *firm retention* of wisdom; Ecclesiasticus, the fourteenth chapter[^p107-10], where he says: *Blessed is the man that shall continue in wisdom*; likewise Proverbs, the third chapter:
<!-- page 108 -->
*Write it out in the tables of thy heart*, that is, in the three parts of the divine image, and especially in the memory, by retaining it firmly. On which account he says: *Write it out*, for what is written is retained more firmly and longer; Proverbs, the twenty-second chapter: *Behold, I have written it out for thee in three ways*, that is, in the *intelligence* through studious acquisition, in the *memory* through firm retention, in the *will* through *love*; to which he admonishes in the words proposed, saying: *Love the lumen of wisdom* etc.

#### On the fourfold cause of this book.

4. In which words four causes of this work are suggested to us, namely the *efficient* cause from the person who speaks, saying: *Love*; the *formal*, that is, the manner of proceeding, is suggested by *lumen*; the *material* is expressed by the name of *wisdom*; the *final* can be understood from the order or *relation of the one speaking to the person* to whom the discourse is directed, when it is said: *All ye that rule over peoples.*

5. Concerning the first it must be noted that the *efficient cause* of this book is threefold: the first by way of *one inspiring*, namely God; whence Job, the thirty-second chapter[^p108-1]: *The inspiration of the Almighty giveth understanding*; likewise Ecclesiasticus, the first chapter: *All wisdom is from the Lord God*, as every *essence* is from his essence, and from his truth every *truth*, and from his goodness every *goodness*. « For the first in each genus is the cause of those things that come after it[^p108-2] »; and everything perfect is caused by the perfect, according to Boethius.

6. The second efficient cause, by way of *one finding*, is Solomon himself; whence in the ecclesiastical history[^p108-3] « the whole chorus of the ancients said that the book which is entitled Wisdom is Solomon's ». Whence the book itself is inscribed *The Wisdom of Solomon*, and by ecclesiastical custom there is set before the lessons taken from this book: « Solomon said to the children of Israel », because, namely, this book was compiled from his sentences. — The proximate efficient cause, by way of *one compiling*, was Philo, the wisest of the Jews, who lived in the times of the Apostles, as the ecclesiastical history relates[^p108-4]; who from the love of wisdom is not undeservedly called *Philo*. Rabanus too asserts that this book was written, that is, compiled, not by Solomon, as is supposed, but rather by Philo, a most wise Jew.

7. But the *formal* cause, that is, the manner of proceeding, is suggested, as has been said, by *lumen*, because, as lumen has the hiddenness of its radical origin, in its going forth a clear manifestation, in its flowing a continuation; so also this book of Wisdom has in its sentences a profound difficulty and *hiddenness* of origin, according to that word of Job, the twenty-eighth chapter[^p108-5]: *Wisdom is drawn out of secret places*; in its discourses a clear *manifestation*, Wisdom, the sixth chapter: *Clear is wisdom, and such as never fadeth away, clear*, I say, through the charm of eloquence; « for the style of this book, according to Jerome, savours of Greek eloquence »; but in the procession of its reasons a *consequence and ordered continuation*, Ecclesiasticus, the twenty-first chapter[^p108-6]: *The knowledge of a wise man shall abound like a flood*, flowing continuously, not in drops; *and his counsel continueth like a fountain of life.*

8. But the *material* cause is expressed by the name of *wisdom*. « Now wisdom, according to Augustine[^p108-7], is the cognition of divine things »; and the divine things are the *power*, *wisdom* and *goodness* of God; of which this book treats, although *wisdom* is intended more principally. It makes known the *power* of God in the punishing
<!-- page 109 -->
of the rebellious, as is clear in the first, second, third, fourth and fifth chapters; his *wisdom* in the illumination of the humble, as is clear in the sixth, seventh, eighth and ninth chapters; his *goodness* in the conferring of benefits, as regards both, as is clear from the tenth chapter onward. But here it is treated not only of *uncreated* wisdom, by dwelling on its praises, but also of *created* wisdom, by showing its usefulness; whence below, in the sixth chapter[^p109-1]: *Now what*, that is, of what kind, *wisdom is*, namely uncreated wisdom, *and how she was made*, namely created wisdom, *I will declare* etc.; although that whole can be understood of *uncreated* wisdom as of the subject. Rightly therefore is it entitled *the book of Wisdom*, because in it wisdom is principally treated as the subject or the matter, as *the book On the Soul* is so called because in it the soul is principally determined.

9. But the *final* cause can be understood from the order of the person speaking to those to whom the discourse is directed; who are touched on there[^p109-2]: *All ye that rule over peoples.* For as it is in the *angelic* illuminations, that the ray of divine light, according to blessed Dionysius, is received first by the Angels of the first hierarchy, secondly by those of the middle, lastly by those of the last — « for the law, as he himself says[^p109-3], has been divinely promulgated, to lead back the middle things through the first and the last through the middle », whence in the Psalm: *Thou enlightenest wonderfully from the everlasting mountains*, that is, the inferior Angels from the superior — as we also see in *bodily* illuminations, because the illumination of the sun is derived first to the higher places and those nearer to heaven, then to the middle, lastly to the lowest, Ecclesiasticus, the forty-third chapter: *For the sun, burning the mountains threefold, breathes out its rays*, namely into the valleys: so also it ought to be in *human* illuminations, that the lumen of wisdom be diffused first into the highest, that is, into prelates, then into the middle, that is, into their ministers, thirdly into the lowest, that is, their subjects, according to that word of the Psalm[^p109-4]: *Let the mountains receive peace for the people* etc. For this is the procession of wisdom. *For she reacheth from end*, namely the supreme, *to end*, the last, *and ordereth all things*, namely the middle ones, *sweetly*, below in the eighth chapter. On account of this Plato said that « commonwealths would be blessed or good if wise men ruled them, or if their rulers were studious of wisdom », as Boethius says in the book *On Consolation*[^p109-5]. *For as* is *the judge of the people, so are his ministers; and what manner of man the ruler of the city is, such also are they that dwell therein*; where he touches on three orders, namely of judges, of ministers and of subjects. On account of the foregoing this author first and principally intends to diffuse the lumen of wisdom into lofty places, that is, into superiors, and through them into inferiors. Whence it is clear that the final cause of this teaching[^tr-doctrina] is the exhortation or provocation of princes and prelates, and consequently of all, to the study and love of wisdom.

[^tr-lumen]: **En.** Translator's note: Latin has two words here where English has one. *Lux* is light in its source — this chunk has it once, in the *divinae lucis radium* that the Angels receive — while *lumen* is light as given off and received, which is what wisdom is when Wisdom 6:23 bids rulers love it. The received English of the verse reads "Love the light of wisdom", but the whole Introduction leans on the word: it returns as the *formal* cause of the book, where *lumen* is said to have a hidden origin, a clear going forth and a continuous flowing, and again as the *lumen* of wisdom diffused from prelates to their subjects. Collapsing it into "light" would hide the argument, so *lux* is "light" throughout and *lumen* is left standing, here and at its six further sites in this chunk, as in the rest of this translation.

[^tr-studium]: **En.** Translator's note: the Latin *studium* — with *studere* and *studiosus* — holds together zeal, eager pursuit, application and study. Here the Introduction builds its first invitation on one root: the *studiosa acquisitio* of wisdom, Proverbs 27:11's *Stude sapientiae*, Tully's definition *Studium est vehemens applicatio animi*, and, at the close, the *studium et amor sapientiae* to which princes are exhorted. This translation therefore keeps one English root throughout — "studious", "be studious", "study" — so that the definition is seen to be defining the very word of the verse, at the cost of "study" reading more narrowly in English than Tully's "vehement application of the mind".

[^tr-vacare]: **En.** Translator's note: the Latin *vacare* holds together several senses that English has to split: being at leisure, being free, being free *for* something and being free *from* something (with *ab*). Here *vacatio* governs *ab* with the ablative twice — *vacationem ab exterioribus occupationibus* and *vacationis ab opere exteriori*, the second glossing Ecclesiasticus' *tempore vacuitatis* — so the English takes the side of being free *from*: the point is what the mind must be released from in order to apply itself to wisdom. The verse's own *vacuitas* keeps the Douay's "leisure". No *vaca-* word is rendered "rest" in this translation, because *quies* and *requies* take that word.

[^tr-doctrina]: **En.** Translator's note: the Latin *doctrina* holds together what English divides among doctrine, teaching and learning — the body of what is taught, the act of teaching it, and what a learner takes away. Here *causam finalem huius doctrinae* names the book itself as an act of instruction directed at princes and prelates, whose end is their exhortation to the study and love of wisdom, so the English takes the side of "teaching". Where this translation renders the same word "doctrine" or "learning", it is because the Latin there names the body of what is taught or the thing learned.
