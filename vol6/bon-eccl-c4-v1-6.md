## Latin
<!-- page 37 -->

### Capitulum IV.

#### Secundo, de vanitate malitiae in subditis secundum triplicem differentiam.

*Verti me ad alia* etc. Supra actum est de vanitate malitiae in praelatis; hic in *subditis.* Vanitas autem malitiae tangitur hic quantum ad triplicem differentiam: primo *calumniae* in malitiose nocendo; secundo *invidiae* in[^p37-4] malitiose dolendo, ibi: *Rursus contemplatus sum;* tertio *accidiae,* a bono opere discedendo; et hoc tangitur ibi: *Stultus complicat.*

(Vers. 1. 2. 3.). Describit igitur *calumniae* vanitatem; et quantum ad *considerationem calumniae* dicit: *Verti me ad alia,* supple: consideranda; *et vidi calumnias, quae sub sole geruntur,* id est in hoc mundo; Ezechielis vigesimo secundo[^p37-5]: « Populi terrae calumniabantur calumniam et rapiebant violenter, egenum et pauperem affligebant et advenam opprimebant calumnia absque iudicio ». Et dicitur *calumnia* violenta bonorum extorsio, quasi per iustitiam, sed per malitiam. — Et subiungit *aggravationem miseriae,* propter oppressorum calamitatem, quia opprimuntur *iniuste, inconsolabiliter, irremediabiliter.* — Quia *iniuste,* dicit: *Et lacrymas innocentium,* scilicet pro huiusmodi calumniis; Iob trigesimo quinto[^p37-6]: « Propter multitudinem calumniatorum clamabunt, et eiulabunt propter vim brachii tyrannorum ». — Quia *inconsolabiliter: et neminem consolatorem,* quod quidem aggravat crudelitatem; unde Threnorum primo: « Non est qui consoletur eam ex omnibus caris eius ». Econtra Ecclesiastici septimo: « Non desis plorantibus in consolatione, et cum lugentibus ambula »; — Nec tantum consolatione caret, immo *remedio;* unde et subdit: *Nec posse resistere violentiae eorum, cunctorum auxilio destitutos,* supple: vidi. De hac malitia dicitur Proverbiorum vigesimo secundo[^p37-7]: « Non facias violentiam pauperi, quia pauper est, neque conteras egenum in porta ». Ideo secundi Paralipomenon vigesimo: « In nobis non est tanta fortitudo, ut possimus huic multitudini resistere, quae irruit super nos ». — Quantum autem ad *detestationem culpae* subiungit: *Et laudavi magis mortuos quam viventes,* eo scilicet, quod ista mala et crudelitates non vident, quas nemo bonus potest videre et non dolere; tertii Regum decimo nono[^p37-8] dixit Elias: « Sufficit mihi, Domine, tolle animam meam; non enim melior sum quam patres mei ». — *Et feliciorem utroque iudicavi,* scilicet mortuo et vivente, *qui necdum natus est nec vidit mala, quae sub sole sunt;* quasi videre ista mala vel vidisse magna sit miseria; primi Machabaeorum secundo: « Vae mihi, dixit Mathathias, utquid natus sum videre contritionem populi mei et contritionem

<!-- page 38 -->
civitatis sanctae, et sedere illic »? — Sic igitur notatur hic *consideratio calumniae, aggravatio miseriae,* quia illi sunt oppressi, desolati, auxilio destituti, et tunc *detestatio culpae.*

(Vers. 4.). *Rursus contemplatus sum* etc. Hic secundo tangitur vanitas *invidiae* in dolendo de bono et prosperitate proximi malitiose, quam etiam vidit; unde subdit: *Rursus contemplatus sum.* Haec contemplatio est liber ac perspicax et defixus intuitus[^p38-1]. *Contemplatus sum,* inquam, *omnes labores et industrias hominum; labores,* quantum ad corpus; *et industrias hominum,* quantum ad animam. — Vel: *labores et industrias,* id est virtutes; unde alia translatio[^p38-2]: *Omnem virtutem operis;* supra secundo: « Detestatus sum omnem industriam meam, qua sub sole studiosissime laboravi ». *Et animadverti,* id est, percepi in animo; *patere,* id est, exposita esse; *invidiae proximi,* quia videns bona invidus statim dolet; Ecclesiastici decimo quarto: « Nequam est oculus lividi et avertens faciem et despiciens animam suam ». *Et in hoc ergo vanitas,* scilicet malitiae; Proverbiorum vigesimo octavo: « Vir, qui festinat ditari et aliis invidet » etc. *Et cura superflua,* scilicet poenae, quia, cum deberet de bonis suis cogitare, cogitat semper de alienis; unde Proverbiorum vigesimo tertio: « Ne comedas cum homine invido, et ne desideres cibos eius, quoniam in similitudinem arioli aestimat quod ignorat ».

(Vers. 5. 6.). *Stultus complicat* etc. Hic tertio tangitur vanitas *accidiae,* propter quam homo redditur taediosus et piger ad omne bonum et per hoc stultus. Ideo dicit: *Stultus complicat manus suas;* ideo *stultus,* quia *piger;* Proverbiorum duodecimo[^p38-3]: « Qui sectatur otium stultissimus est »; Proverbiorum sexto: « Vade ad formicam, o piger, et disce sapientiam ». Hic *stultus complicat manus,* quia renuit operari; Proverbiorum vigesimo sexto: « Abscondit piger manus suas sub ascella sua et laborat, si ad os suum eas converterit ». Et ex hoc, quod operari renuit, pauper efficitur et macer propter defectum inopiae; unde dicit: *Et comedit carnes suas, dicens;* hyperbolice dictum est, eo quod macer efficitur, non habens cibum; Proverbiorum vigesimo primo[^p38-4]: « Desideria occidunt pigrum, noluerunt enim quidquam manus eius operari ». — Sed in hac pigritia accidiae habere sibi videtur excusationem sapientiae; unde et subdit dicens: *Melior est pugillus cum requie quam plena utraque manus cum labore et afflictione animi.* Haec est sententia pigri, per quam se excusat; unde dicitur Proverbiorum vigesimo sexto: « Sapientior sibi videtur piger septem viris loquentibus sententias ». — Et sic insinuatur in pigro *torpor accidiae,* quia *complicat manus; sustinentia penuriae,* quia *comedit carnes; excusatio ignorantiae,* quia laudat requiem.

### Quaestiones

I. Quaeritur hic de hoc quod dicit: *Laudavi magis mortuos quam viventes.*

**Contra:** 1. Infra nono[^p38-5]: *Melior est canis vivus leone mortuo.*

2. Praeterea, hoc videtur *ratione:* quia melior est habitus quam privatio, ergo vita quam mors: ergo, *a coniugatis*[^p38-6], meliores sunt viventes quam mortui.

Respondeo: dicendum, quod laudare aliquid magis alio vel praeferre dupliciter potest esse: vel *simpliciter,* vel *quoad aliquam conditionem. Simpliciter* loquendo, praefertur vivens mortuo, nisi addatur conditio beatitudinis; sed *quoad aliquid* praefertur mortuus vivo: primo, quia non habet tot occasiones peccandi; unde dicebat Apostolus ad Romanos septimo[^p38-7]: *Infelix ego homo, quis me liberabit a servitute corporis huius?* Secundo, quia non habet tot occasiones tristandi, quia non videt tot mala; Ionae ultimo: *Melius est mihi mori quam vivere.* Et quantum ad istam conditionem praefertur mortuus vivo in exaggerationem praesentium malitiarum et miseriarum.

II. Item quaeritur de hoc quod dicitur: *Feliciorem utroque iudicavi, qui necdum natus est.*

**Contra:** 1. Ille nihil boni habet: ergo non est felicior.

2. Praeterea, omne, quod habet aliquo modo rationem felicitatis, est appetendum; sed nullus potest appetere *non-esse,* sicut dicit Augustinus[^p38-8]: ergo qui non est natus nullo modo felix est.

III. Item quaeritur, utrum aliquis possit hoc velle, scilicet magis *non-esse* quam poenam, ordinato

<!-- page 39 -->
appetitu. — Quod *sic:* Matthaei vigesimo sexto[^p39-1]: « Melius erat ei, si natus non fuisset »: ergo si omne melius appetendum et praeeligendum, etiam et hoc.

Quod *non,* videtur, quia totum bonum tollit: ergo nihil illo peius.

Respondeo: dicendum, quod felicitas duobus modis est in aliquo: *vere* et *aestimative. Vere* est in uno solo, *aestimative* in multis secundum opiniones et considerationes diversas et eius conditiones in diversis repertas. Quia enim est bonum iucundissimum, ideo Epicurus eam ponit in voluptatibus, et sic de aliis. Quia igitur *felix* nulla malitia vel miseria molestatur; et ista conditio reperitur in eo qui nondum natus est: ratione istius conditionis dicit: *Feliciorem aestimavi* etc. Dico etiam, quod ratione illius conditionis appetitur a multis afflictis.

Quod quaeritur, utrum debeat appeti vel praeeligi; dicendum, quod duplex est poena: *temporalis* et *aeterna;* neutra poena *totum* adimit, sed tamen aeterna *semper* adimit; corruptio vero in *non-esse totum* adimit, sed *semel.* Si igitur talis corruptio in *non-esse* comparetur poenae *temporali,* simpliciter est peior; si autem *aeternae,* se habent sicut excedentia et excessa, quia haec in ademptione, illa in continuatione excedit. Et quia illa est infinita duratione, ista vero momentanea; in hoc excedit et potest magis rationabiliter fugi et sperni[^p39-2].

IV. Item quaeritur de hoc quod dicit: *Animadverti, omnia patere invidiae proximi.* Quaero, utrum homo debeat cessare propter eius invidiam a bono opere. — Quod sic, videtur: quia magis diligendus est proximus quam quaelibet utilitas praesens: ergo si proximus perit invidendo, illud debet omitti.

**Contra:** Opus bonum bonum praestat exemplum et excitat ad bonum: ergo si bonum aperiendum est ad laudem, non debet homo occultare propter invidiam proximi.

Respondeo: aut est opus illud de *necessitate,* aut de *utilitate.* Si de *necessitate,* nequaquam dimittitur; si de *utilitate*[^p39-3], aut *potest* aeque bonum fieri in eius absentia, ut in praesentia, aut *non;* si *sic,* debet homo opus illud ab eius oculis absentare, ut tollat occasionem, quando probabiliter coniicit, quod ille invidet; si autem *non potest,* facere debet nec dat occasionem, sed ille accipit.

## English

### Chapter IV.

#### Secondly, on the vanity of malice in subjects according to a threefold difference.

*I turned myself to other things* etc. Above he treated of the vanity of malice in prelates; here in *subjects.* Now the vanity of malice is touched on here as to a threefold difference: first of *calumny,* in harming maliciously; secondly of *envy,* in[^p37-4] grieving maliciously, at: *Again I considered;* thirdly of *acedia,* in departing from good work; and this is touched on at: *The fool foldeth.*

(Vers. 1. 2. 3.). He describes, then, the vanity of *calumny;* and as to the *consideration of calumny* he says: *I turned myself to other things,* supply: to be considered; *and I saw the calumnies that are done under the sun,* that is, in this world; Ezechiel, the twenty-second chapter[^p37-5]: « The people of the land practised calumny and committed robbery with violence; they afflicted the needy and the poor, and they oppressed the stranger by calumny without judgement ». And *calumny* is called the violent extortion of goods, as though by justice, but by malice. — And he adds the *aggravation of misery,* on account of the calamity of the oppressed, since they are oppressed *unjustly, without consolation, without remedy.* — Because *unjustly,* he says: *And the tears of the innocent,* namely for calumnies of this kind; Job, the thirty-fifth chapter[^p37-6]: « By reason of the multitude of calumniators they shall cry out, and they shall wail for the violence of the arm of tyrants ». — Because *without consolation: and they had no comforter,* which indeed aggravates the cruelty; hence Lamentations, the first chapter: « There is none to comfort her among all them that were dear to her ». On the contrary, Ecclesiasticus, the seventh chapter: « Be not wanting to them that weep in comforting, and walk with them that mourn »; — And not only does he lack consolation, but also *remedy;* hence he also adds: *And they were not able to resist their violence, being destitute of help from any,* supply: I saw. Of this malice it is said in Proverbs, the twenty-second chapter[^p37-7]: « Do no violence to the poor, because he is poor, neither crush the needy in the gate ». Therefore, II Paralipomenon, the twentieth chapter: « In us there is not so great strength that we can resist this multitude which rusheth upon us ». — But as to the *detestation of fault* he adds: *And I praised the dead rather than the living,* namely in this, that they do not see these evils and cruelties, which no good man can see and not grieve; III Kings, the nineteenth chapter[^p37-8], Elias said: « It is enough for me, Lord, take away my soul; for I am no better than my fathers ». — *And I judged him happier than them both,* namely than the dead and the living, *that is not yet born, nor hath seen the evils that are under the sun;* as though to see these evils, or to have seen them, were a great misery; I Machabees, the second chapter: « Woe is me, said Mathathias, wherefore was I born to see the ruin of my people and the ruin

<!-- page 38 -->
of the holy city, and to sit there »? — Thus, then, there is noted here the *consideration of calumny,* the *aggravation of misery,* since they are oppressed, desolate, destitute of help, and then the *detestation of fault.*

(Vers. 4.). *Again I considered* etc. Here secondly is touched the vanity of *envy,* in grieving maliciously over the good and prosperity of one's neighbour, which he also saw; hence he adds: *Again I considered.* This contemplation is a free and clear-sighted and fixed gaze[^p38-1]. *I considered,* I say, *all the labours and industries of men; labours,* as to the body; *and industries of men,* as to the soul. — Or: *labours and industries,* that is, virtues; hence another translation[^p38-2]: *All the virtue of the work;* above, the second chapter: « I detested all my industry wherewith I laboured most studiously under the sun ». *And I remarked,* that is, I perceived in my mind; *that they lie open,* that is, are exposed; *to the envy of the neighbour,* because on seeing good things the envious man grieves at once; Ecclesiasticus, the fourteenth chapter: « The eye of the envious is wicked, and he turneth away his face, and despiseth his own soul ». *And in this therefore vanity,* namely of malice; Proverbs, the twenty-eighth chapter: « A man that maketh haste to be rich, and envieth others » etc. *And superfluous care,* namely of punishment, since, when he ought to think of his own goods, he thinks always of other men's; hence Proverbs, the twenty-third chapter: « Eat not with an envious man, and desire not his meats, because like a soothsayer he esteemeth that which he knoweth not ».

(Vers. 5. 6.). *The fool foldeth* etc. Here thirdly is touched the vanity of *acedia,* on account of which a man is rendered weary and sluggish towards every good, and through this a fool. Therefore he says: *The fool foldeth his hands together;* therefore a *fool,* because *sluggish;* Proverbs, the twelfth chapter[^p38-3]: « He that pursueth idleness is very foolish »; Proverbs, the sixth chapter: « Go to the ant, O sluggard, and learn wisdom ». This *fool foldeth his hands,* because he refuses to work; Proverbs, the twenty-sixth chapter: « The sluggard hideth his hands under his armpit, and it is a labour to him if he turn them to his mouth ». And from this, that he refuses to work, he is made poor and lean through the want of need; hence he says: *And eateth his own flesh, saying;* it is said hyperbolically, in that he is made lean, not having food; Proverbs, the twenty-first chapter[^p38-4]: « Desires kill the sluggard, for his hands have refused to work at all ». — But in this sluggishness of acedia he seems to himself to have the excuse of wisdom; hence he also adds, saying: *Better is a handful with rest than both hands full with labour and vexation of mind.* This is the sentence of the sluggard, by which he excuses himself; hence it is said in Proverbs, the twenty-sixth chapter: « The sluggard is wiser in his own conceit than seven men that speak sentences ». — And thus there is intimated in the sluggard the *torpor of acedia,* since *he foldeth his hands;* the *endurance of penury,* since *he eateth his flesh;* the *excuse of ignorance,* since he praises rest.

### Questions

I. Here it is asked concerning that which he says: *I praised the dead rather than the living.*

**Contra:** 1. Below, the ninth chapter[^p38-5]: *Better is a living dog than a dead lion.*

2. Likewise, this seems so *by reason:* since a habit is better than a privation, therefore life than death: therefore, *from conjugates*[^p38-6], the living are better than the dead.

I respond: it must be said that to praise one thing more than another, or to prefer it, can be in two ways: either *simply,* or *as to some condition.* Speaking *simply,* the living is preferred to the dead, unless the condition of beatitude is added; but *as to something* the dead is preferred to the living: first, because he has not so many occasions of sinning; hence the Apostle said, to the Romans, the seventh chapter[^p38-7]: *Unhappy man that I am, who shall deliver me from the servitude of this body?* Secondly, because he has not so many occasions of sorrowing, since he does not see so many evils; Jonas, the last chapter: *It is better for me to die than to live.* And as to that condition the dead is preferred to the living, in exaggeration of present malices and miseries.

II. Likewise it is asked concerning that which is said: *I judged him happier than them both, that is not yet born.*

**Contra:** 1. He has nothing of good: therefore he is not happier.

2. Likewise, everything that has in any way the character of happiness is to be desired; but no one can desire *non-being,* as Augustine says[^p38-8]: therefore he who is not born is in no way happy.

III. Likewise it is asked whether anyone can will this, namely *non-being* rather than punishment, with an ordered

<!-- page 39 -->
appetite. — That *he can:* Matthew, the twenty-sixth chapter[^p39-1]: « It were better for him, if he had not been born »: therefore if everything better is to be desired and chosen before, this also.

That *he cannot,* it seems, since it takes away all good: therefore nothing is worse than that.

I respond: it must be said that happiness is in a thing in two ways: *truly* and *in estimation.* *Truly* it is in one alone; *in estimation* in many, according to divers opinions and considerations, and according as its conditions are found in divers things. For because it is a most delightful good, therefore Epicurus places it in pleasures, and so of the others. Since, then, the *happy man* is troubled by no malice or misery, and this condition is found in him who is not yet born: by reason of this condition he says: *I esteemed him happier* etc. I say also that by reason of that condition it is desired by many who are afflicted.

As to what is asked, whether it ought to be desired or chosen before; it must be said that punishment is twofold: *temporal* and *eternal;* neither punishment takes away the *whole,* but yet the eternal takes away *always;* whereas corruption into *non-being* takes away the *whole,* but *once.* If, then, such corruption into *non-being* is compared to *temporal* punishment, it is simply worse; but if to *eternal,* they stand to one another as exceeding and exceeded, since the one exceeds in the taking away, the other in the continuation. And since the one is infinite in duration, but the other momentary, in this it exceeds, and can more reasonably be fled and spurned[^p39-2].

IV. Likewise it is asked concerning that which he says: *I remarked that all things lie open to the envy of the neighbour.* I ask whether a man ought to cease from a good work on account of his envy. — That he ought, it seems: since one's neighbour is more to be loved than any present usefulness: therefore if one's neighbour perishes by envying, that work ought to be left off.

**Contra:** A good work gives a good example and stirs up to good: therefore if the good is to be disclosed for praise, a man ought not to hide it on account of his neighbour's envy.

I respond: either that work is one of *necessity,* or of *usefulness.* If of *necessity,* it is by no means left off; if of *usefulness*[^p39-3], either it *can* be done equally well in his absence as in his presence, or *not;* if *so,* a man ought to remove that work from his eyes, so as to take away the occasion, when he probably conjectures that the other envies; but if it *cannot,* he ought to do it, and he does not give the occasion, but the other takes it.
