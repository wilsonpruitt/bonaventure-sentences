## *Utrum spiritualia habeant permanentem, vel successivam mensuram*

---

## Latin
<!-- page 61 -->

### Quaestio III. *Utrum spiritualia habeant permanentem, vel successivam mensuram.*

Tertio quaeritur, utrum spiritualia habeant mensuram *permanentem*, aut *successivam*. Et quod *permanentem*, carentem priori et posteriori, videtur primo *auctoritate*, deinde *ratione*.

1. Et *auctoritate* sic: Augustinus duodecimo de Civitate Dei[^1]: «Immortalitas Angelorum non transit in tempore nec est praeterita, quasi iam non sit, nec futura, quasi nondum sit»: ergo tota est praesens, ergo permanens, non succedens.

2. Item, Augustinus undecimo Confessionum[^2]: «Si praesens tempus staret et non transiret in praeteritum, esset aeternitas»; sed non propter hoc esset aeternitas increata: ergo esset aeternitas creata; et hoc est aevum: ergo aevum habet *nunc* non transiens: ergo etc.

3. Item, Philosophus[^3]: «In perpetuis non differt *esse* et *posse*»: ergo potentia est in toto suo actu, ergo nihil exspectat, ergo totum simul: ergo nulla est ibi successio vel prioritas.

4. Item *ratione*: proprietas debet respondere substantiae, ergo substantiae simplicis mensura debet esse simplex; sed substantia spiritualis est simplex: ergo et eius mensura. Sed eius mensura est aevum: ergo simplex est: ergo non habet prius et posterius.

5. Item, omne successivum reducitur ad permanens, ergo prius et posterius successivum ad prius et posterius permanens[^4]; sed prius et posterius permanens est in sola magnitudine: ergo quod caret priori et posteriori permanente caret et successivo. Sed talis est Angelus: ergo etc.

6. Item, ubi est prius et posterius, ibi necessario cadit aliquid novum, quod de posteriori fit prius, et per hoc lapsus in *praeteritum*, et aliquid novum; sed in perpetuis non est aliqua mutatio[^5]: ergo nec successio.

7. Item, ubi est prius, ibi est aliqua exspectatio respectu eius quod posterius est et nondum habitum est; sed in Beatis perfecte nulla cadit exspectatio, quia evacuatur omnino fides et spes, et habetur omne quod habituri sunt[^6]: ergo nullo modo est ibi prius et posterius.

**Contra:**

1. Hieronymus ad Marcellam[^7]: «Tantum est, qui non novit *fuisse* vel *futurum*»: omnis ergo creatura in sui *esse* habet praeteritionem sive *fuisse* et *fore*: ergo habet in sui *duratione* successionem.

2. Item, Anselmus in Proslogio[^8] tractans illud verbum: *Dominus regnabit in aeternum et ultra*, propter hoc dictum est, non quia post aeternitatem sit aliquid, sed quia Dei aeternitas, quae tota simul est, excedit aeternitatem creaturae, quae non omnino est tota simul; ista est sententia: ergo idem quod prius.

<!-- page 62 -->

3. Item, *ratione* videtur: unitas angelica deficit ab unitate divina, ergo simplicitas aeternitatis creatae a simplicitate aeternitatis increatae: si ergo hoc verum est, ergo habet compositionem aliquo modo. Sed compositio in duratione ponit prius et posterius: ergo etc.

4. Item, hoc ipsum videtur, quia mensura durationis angelicae non est ipse Angelus, immo differt ab Angelo, sicut mensura a mensurato; sed mensura secundum veritatem est in genere quantitatis[^9], et talis est divisibilis, ergo habens partes: ergo etc. *Si tu dicas*, quod est quantitatis *principium* sicut punctus; *contra*: punctus non habet complete rationem mensurae, sed est *principium* mensurae; aevum autem vere et proprie est mensura: ergo non potest esse sicut *principium*.

5. Item, Angeli duratio est infinita: si ergo est tota simul in actu, ergo aliquod creatum est infinitum actu. Sed hoc est impossibile: ergo etc.

6. Item, in eo quod habet totum esse simul, idem est *fuisse* et *esse* et *futurum esse*; sed quod fuit, impossibile est cogitari non fuisse, si intelligatur fuisse: ergo si in aeviterno idem est *fuisse* et *fore*, ergo impossibile est cogitari non fore. Hoc autem falsum est, quia hoc est proprium solius Dei: ergo etc.

7. Item, si totum *esse* praesens est, ergo non differt *esse* et *fuisse*: ergo quod nunc non est, nunquam fuit, et si fuit, est. Sed Deus non potest facere, quod fuit non fuisse[^10]: ergo non potest facere, aeviternum non esse. Sed hoc est manifeste falsum: ergo etc.

8. Item, si totum esse aeviterni simul est, et tota duratio sine priori et posteriori, ergo non est ibi magis longum et minus longum; ergo anima beati Petri nec prius nec diutius fuit in gloria quam anima beati Francisci. Si ergo hoc manifeste falsum est, patet etc.

### Conclusio

> In aevo est aliqua successio, quae non importat prius et posterius cum variatione et innovatione, ut in tempore, sed tantum durationis extensionem.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod circa hoc duplex fuit opinio.

Quidam enim dixerunt, quod aevum est totum simplex, sicut et illud quod mensurat; et *esse* substantiae aeviternae et eius *duratio* est tota simul, non habens successionem prioris et posterioris. Unde dixerunt, aevum non esse proprie quantitatem, sed in genere quantitatis solum esse per reductionem; unde *simplex* est extensione, sed tamen est *quantum* virtute, et adeo quantum, quod sua virtute excedat omne *esse* temporale ad similitudinem aeternitatis increatae. Nam sicut anima simplex est quantum ad quantitatem *molis*, tamen propter suam *virtutem* et simplicitatem est in qualibet parte sui corporis; sic in proposito intelligendum est. — Sed, sicut in opponendo tactum est, si quis ponat aevum omnino simplex, ponet ex hoc non esse veram mensuram; ponet etiam durationem creatam actu infinitam; ponet etiam durationem creatam adeo entem[^11], quod nec Deus destruere, nec intellectus possit cogitare non esse; quae omnia, cum non sint intelligibilia, faciunt, praedictam positionem nec esse rationabilem nec intelligibilem.

Et ideo, cum Sanctorum auctoritates aliud videantur sonare, est aliorum positio probabilior et intelligibilior, quod in aevo est ponere *prius* et *posterius*, et est ponere aliquam *successionem*, aliam tamen successionem quam in tempore. In tempore enim est *successio* cum variatione, et *prius et posterius* cum inveteratione et renovatione. In aevo vero est *prius et posterius*, quod dicit durationis extensionem, quod tamen nullam dicit variationem nec innovationem. Et per hanc distinctionem ad omnia obiecta in contrarium facile est solvere, si quis eam intelligat.

Quod si forte quaeratur: *quomodo* potest esse prius et posterius sine novitate circa esse; dicendum, quod sicut videmus, quod aliter egreditur rivulus[^12] a fonte, aliter radius a sole, sic in proposito videmus. Nam rivulus sic egreditur a fonte, quod nova aqua semper exit, non eadem; radius a sole continue egreditur, non quia semper novum aliquid emittatur, sed quia quod emissum est continuatur; unde solis influentia non est aliud quam continuatio dati. Similiter in *motu*, et in *esse* rei *mobilis* aliqua proprietas habita amittitur, vel non habita acquiritur; sed in *esse* rei *aeviternae* quod primo datum est per continuam Dei influentiam *continuatur*. Nulla enim aevi creatura est omnino actus, nec aliqua eius virtus, unde continue indiget divina virtute cooperante. Ideo, etsi *esse* totum habeat, tamen *continuationem esse* non habet totam simul, et ideo est ibi successio sine aliqua innovatione circa *esse* vel proprietatem absolutam; tamen ibi est vera continuatio, respectu cuius creatura habet *esse* quodam modo in potentia, ac per hoc habet successionem.

<!-- page 63 -->

Solus igitur Deus, qui est actus purus, est actu infinitus, et totum *esse* et possessionem sui *esse* simul habet[^13]. His visis, satis de facili solvuntur obiecta.

1. 2. Quod enim obiicitur de praeterito et futuro et de transitu, dicendum breviter, quod intelligitur de istis, ut dicunt variationem.

3. Quod obiicitur, quod non differt *esse* et *posse*; dicendum, quod verum est de *posse* et *esse*, quod non differt, id est, quod non distat; motus enim et tempus facit distare; in solo autem aeterno verum est, quod potentia omnino sit actus, non in aliqua creatura[^14].

4. Quod obiicitur, quod substantiae simplicis proprietas non potest esse composita; dicendum, quod verum est, si illa proprietas habeat compositionem partium simul entium[^15]; nunc autem non est sic, immo de aevo nunquam est nisi *nunc*, prius, sicut et de tempore dicitur. Et ita bene potest esse in simplici, ut in composito; unde tantam extensionem habet *duratio* unius grani milii, sicut et unius montis.

5. Quod obiicitur, quod *prius* et *posterius* reducitur ad permanens; dicendum, quod verum est, quod ad permanens reducitur; sed non oportet, quod ad *prius* et *posterius* permanens. Empyreum enim durationem habet aeviternam et aeque simplex est sicut duratio Angeli: ergo *prius* et *posterius* in partibus permanentibus non facit aliquid ad hoc.

6. Quod obiicitur de novo et veteri, iam patet: quia nihil est ibi alterationis, est tamen ibi crementum durationis absque omni alteratione absolutae proprietatis. Verum enim est dicere, quod diutius fuit anima Petri, quam anima Francisci[^16], quando in gloriam introivit, sed non est ita circa Deum; non enim diutius duravit hodie quam ante heri.

7. Quod obiicitur de exspectatione, dicendum, quod, sicut est *successio* non per *novi acquisitionem*, sed per *prius dati continuationem*, ita etiam est *exspectatio* non novi habendi, sed continuationis prius habiti, quod quia iam habent, et certi sunt se habituros, ideo potius dicitur *tentio* et *comprehensio* quam *exspectatio*. Et sic patet illud.

### Scholion

**I.** Haec quaestio etiam sub his terminis proponitur: utrum aevum sit mensura simplex sive indivisibilis. S. Thom. in solutione eius cum communiore sententia contra S. Bonaventuram tenet, quod, sicut essentia et *existentia* Angeli simplex est et tota simul, sic etiam *mensura* ipsius excludat omnem successionem, nisi forte quatenus connotat coexistentiam ad tempus. — Sententia S. Bonaventurae duabus innititur distinctionibus. 1. Aliter sentiendum est de *esse substantiali* Angeli, aliter de *duratione* eius: illud *esse* in instanti creationis accipit ac indivisibile et totum simul, non autem *durationem* eius, quae, quia creatura continuo dependet a Deo conservante, non est indivisibilis, sed habet aliquam suo modo successionem. 2. Duplex est successio: *propria*, quae competit tempori secundum prius et posterius et habet variationem et renovationem, quia semper aliquid amittitur, et aliquid acquiritur; et *impropria*, quae competit aevo et non importat nisi durationis extensionem sine variatione vel innovatione. Per creationem res proprie *fit* et acquirit *initium* existentiae et durationis; per conservationem vero non *fit*, sed *continuatur in existentia* et acquirit maiorem durationem, quin in *esse* eius fiat innovatio vel additio. Unde aeviternum in instanti suae creationis totum suum *esse substantiale* habuit, sed non totam suam *durationem*, immo non nisi durationis *initium*. — Admissis his distinctionibus, quas contrariae sententiae fautores non approbant, facile intelligitur, in solo Deo idem esse *essentiam, existentiam, durationem*; in omnibus creaturis autem haec tria distinguenda esse.

**II.** Sententiam S. Thomae sequuntur inter antiquos Scholasticos Alex. Hal., Petr. a Tar., Henr. Gand., Durand., Dionys. Carth. aliique. — B. Albertus consentit S. Bonaventurae. Richard. a Med. utriusque sententiae argg. ponit et solvit, magis tamen inclinat in sententiam S. Thomae. Aegid. R. in re parum a S. Bonav. discedit. Scotus vero (hic q. 1.) in utramque partem disputat et anceps haeret, tamen, testibus Lycheto pluribusque aliis Scotistis, magis inclinat in sententiam S. Bonaventurae. Idem etiam de Rerum princip. q. 22. n. 8. ait: «Vel forte habet [aevum] aliquam naturam successionis, scil. quae est *esse* et *modus* essendi et durandi illorum substantiarum». Nec inter recentiores desunt viri doctissimi, qui sententiam S. Bonaventurae praeferant, vel valde probabilem censent, ut cl. P. Kleutgen S. I., Philosophie der Vorzeit tom. I. Abhandl. 4. c. 1. § 2, qui ut huius opinionis sequaces laudat Conimbricenses Phys. IV. c. 14. q. 2, et P. Silv. Maurum, Quaest. philos. tom. II. q. 32.

## English

### Question III. *Whether spiritual things have a permanent or a successive measure.*

Thirdly it is asked whether spiritual things have a *permanent* or a *successive* measure. And that [they have] a *permanent* one, lacking before and after, seems true first *by authority*, then *by reason*.

1. And *by authority* thus: Augustine, in the twelfth book of *On the City of God*[^1]: «The immortality of the Angels does not pass in time, nor is it past, as though it now were not, nor future, as though it were not yet»: therefore it is wholly present, therefore permanent, not successive.

2. Likewise, Augustine, eleventh book of the *Confessions*[^2]: «If the present time were to stand and not pass into the past, it would be eternity»; but not on that account would it be uncreated eternity: therefore it would be created eternity; and this is the age: therefore the age has a *now* that does not pass: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the Philosopher[^3]: «In perpetual things *being* and *being able* do not differ»: therefore the potency is in its whole act, therefore it awaits nothing, therefore [it is] all at once: therefore there is no succession or priority there.

4. Likewise *by reason*: a property must correspond to the substance, therefore the measure of a simple substance must be simple; but a spiritual substance is simple: therefore also its measure. But its measure is the age: therefore it is simple: therefore it does not have before and after.

5. Likewise, every successive thing is reduced to a permanent one, therefore the successive before-and-after to a permanent before-and-after[^4]; but the permanent before-and-after is in magnitude alone: therefore what lacks a permanent before-and-after lacks also the successive one. But such is the Angel: therefore, etc.

6. Likewise, where there is before and after, there necessarily falls something new, which from being later becomes earlier, and through this a lapse into the *past*, and something new; but in perpetual things there is no change[^5]: therefore neither succession.

7. Likewise, where there is a before, there is some expectation with respect to that which is later and not yet possessed; but in the Blessed no expectation at all falls, because faith and hope are wholly emptied out, and all that they will have is had[^6]: therefore in no way is there before and after there.

**On the contrary:**

1. Jerome to Marcella[^7]: «He alone is, who does not know *to have been* or *to be about to be*»: every creature, therefore, in its own *being* has a pastness, that is, a *having been* and a *going to be*: therefore it has in its own *duration* a succession.

2. Likewise, Anselm in the *Proslogion*[^8], treating that word: *The Lord shall reign forever and beyond*, [says] this is said not because there is something after eternity, but because God's eternity, which is wholly at once, exceeds the eternity of the creature, which is not wholly at once; this is the sense: therefore the same as before.

<!-- page 62 -->

3. Likewise, it seems so *by reason*: angelic unity falls short of the divine unity, therefore the simplicity of created eternity [falls short] of the simplicity of uncreated eternity: if then this is true, then it has composition in some way. But composition in duration posits before and after: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, this same thing seems so, because the measure of angelic duration is not the Angel itself, but rather differs from the Angel, as a measure from the measured; but a measure, in truth, is in the genus of quantity[^9], and such a thing is divisible, therefore having parts: therefore, etc. *If you say* that it is the *principle* of quantity, like a point; *on the contrary*: a point does not have completely the account of a measure, but is the *principle* of a measure; but the age is truly and properly a measure: therefore it cannot be like a *principle*.

5. Likewise, the duration of an Angel is infinite: if then it is wholly at once in act, then some created thing is infinite in act. But this is impossible: therefore, etc.

6. Likewise, in that which has its whole being at once, *to have been* and *to be* and *to be about to be* are the same; but what has been, it is impossible to think not to have been, if it is understood to have been: therefore if in the aeviternal *to have been* and *to be going to be* are the same, then it is impossible to think it not going to be. But this is false, because this is proper to God alone: therefore, etc.

7. Likewise, if the whole *being* is present, then *being* and *having been* do not differ: therefore what now is not, never was, and if it was, [now] is. But God cannot bring it about that what has been should not have been[^10]: therefore He cannot bring it about that the aeviternal not be. But this is manifestly false: therefore, etc.

8. Likewise, if the whole being of the aeviternal is at once, and the whole duration without before and after, then there is not there a longer and a shorter; therefore the soul of blessed Peter was neither earlier nor longer in glory than the soul of blessed Francis. If, then, this is manifestly false, it is plain, etc.

### Conclusio

> In the age there is some succession, which does not imply before and after with variation and innovation, as in time, but only an extension of duration.

**I respond:** It must be said that concerning this there was a twofold opinion.

For some have said that the age is wholly simple, just as also that which it measures; and the *being* of the aeviternal substance and its *duration* is wholly at once, not having a succession of before and after. Whence they said that the age is not properly a quantity, but is in the genus of quantity only by reduction; whence it is *simple* as to extension, but is nevertheless *quantitative* in power, and so quantitative that by its power it exceeds all temporal *being* in likeness to uncreated eternity. For just as the soul is simple as to the quantity of *mass*, yet on account of its *power* and simplicity is in every part of its body; so it must be understood in the matter at hand. — But, as was touched on in objecting, if anyone posits the age as wholly simple, he will posit from this that it is not a true measure; he will also posit a created duration actually infinite; he will also posit a created duration so much a being[^11] that neither could God destroy it, nor could the intellect think it not to be; all of which, since they are not intelligible, make that aforesaid position to be neither reasonable nor intelligible.

And therefore, since the authorities of the Saints seem to sound otherwise, the position of the others is more probable and more intelligible, that in the age one must posit *before* and *after*, and one must posit some *succession*, yet a succession other than that in time. For in time there is *succession* with variation, and *before and after* with aging and renewal. But in the age there is *before and after*, which states an extension of duration, which nevertheless states no variation nor innovation. And by this distinction it is easy to solve all the objections to the contrary, if one understands it.

But if perchance it be asked: *how* can there be before and after without newness about being; it must be said that, as we see that a brook flows forth from a spring in one way[^12], a ray from the sun in another, so we see in the matter at hand. For the brook flows forth from the spring in such a way that new water always goes out, not the same; the ray flows forth continuously from the sun, not because something new is always emitted, but because what has been emitted is continued; whence the sun's influence is nothing other than the continuation of what is given. Likewise in *motion*, and in the *being* of a *movable* thing, some property possessed is lost, or one not possessed is acquired; but in the *being* of an *aeviternal* thing what was first given is *continued* through God's continual influence. For no creature of the age is wholly act, nor any power of it, whence it continually needs the divine power cooperating. Therefore, even though it has its whole *being*, it nevertheless does not have the *continuation of being* wholly at once, and therefore there is succession there without any innovation about *being* or absolute property; yet there is there a true continuation, with respect to which the creature has *being* in a certain way in potency, and through this it has succession.

<!-- page 63 -->

God alone, therefore, who is pure act, is infinite in act, and has the whole *being* and possession of His *being* at once[^13]. These things being seen, the objections are quite easily solved.

1, 2. As to what is objected, then, about the past and the future and about transit, it must be said briefly that these are understood as they state a variation.

3. As to what is objected, that *being* and *being able* do not differ; it must be said that it is true of *being able* and *being* that they do not differ, that is, that they are not apart; for motion and time make [them] apart; but in the eternal alone is it true that the potency is wholly act, not in any creature[^14].

4. As to what is objected, that the property of a simple substance cannot be composite; it must be said that it is true, if that property has a composition of parts existing at once[^15]; but now it is not so, indeed of the age there is never anything but *now*, before, just as is said also of time. And so it can well be in the simple as in the composite; whence the *duration* of a single grain of millet has as great an extension as that of a single mountain.

5. As to what is objected, that *before* and *after* is reduced to a permanent thing; it must be said that it is true that it is reduced to a permanent thing; but it is not necessary that [it be reduced] to a permanent before-and-after. For the empyrean has an aeviternal duration and is equally simple as the duration of an Angel: therefore before and after in permanent parts does nothing toward this.

6. As to what is objected about the new and the old, it is already clear: because there is nothing of alteration there, yet there is there an increment of duration without any alteration of an absolute property. For it is true to say that the soul of Peter was [in glory] longer than the soul of Francis[^16], when it entered into glory, but it is not so with regard to God; for He has not endured longer today than before yesterday.

7. As to what is objected about expectation, it must be said that, just as there is *succession* not through *the acquisition of something new*, but through *the continuation of what was given before*, so too there is *expectation* not of having something new, but of the continuation of what was had before, which, because they already have it, and are certain they will have it, is therefore rather called a *holding* and *comprehension* than an *expectation*. And thus that [objection] is plain.

### Scholion

**I.** This question is also proposed under these terms: whether the age is a simple or indivisible measure. St. Thomas, in his solution of it, holds, with the more common opinion, against St. Bonaventure, that, just as the essence and *existence* of the Angel is simple and wholly at once, so too its *measure* excludes all succession, except perhaps insofar as it connotes a coexistence with time. — The opinion of St. Bonaventure rests on two distinctions. 1. One must judge otherwise about the *substantial being* of the Angel, otherwise about its *duration*: that *being* it receives in the instant of creation as indivisible and wholly at once, but not its *duration*, which, because the creature continuously depends on God conserving [it], is not indivisible, but has in its own mode some succession. 2. Succession is twofold: *proper*, which belongs to time according to before and after and has variation and renewal, because something is always lost and something acquired; and *improper*, which belongs to the age and implies only an extension of duration without variation or innovation. By creation a thing properly *comes to be* and acquires the *beginning* of existence and duration; but by conservation it does not *come to be*, but is *continued in existence* and acquires a greater duration, without any innovation or addition occurring in its *being*. Whence the aeviternal in the instant of its creation had its whole *substantial being*, but not its whole *duration*, indeed nothing but the *beginning* of duration. — These distinctions being admitted (which the supporters of the contrary opinion do not approve), it is easily understood that in God alone *essence, existence, duration* are the same; but in all creatures these three are to be distinguished.

**II.** The opinion of St. Thomas is followed, among the ancient Scholastics, by Alexander of Hales, Peter of Tarentaise, Henry of Ghent, Durandus, Dionysius the Carthusian, and others. — Bl. Albert agrees with St. Bonaventure. Richard of Middleton sets out and solves the arguments of both opinions, yet inclines more to the opinion of St. Thomas. Giles of Rome departs little in substance from St. Bonaventure. But Scotus (here q. 1) disputes on both sides and remains in doubt, yet, on the testimony of Lychetus and several other Scotists, inclines more to the opinion of St. Bonaventure. The same [Scotus] also says, *On the Principle of Things*, q. 22, n. 8: «Or perhaps [the age] has some nature of succession, namely that which is the *being* and the *mode* of being and of enduring of those substances». Nor among more recent [writers] are there lacking most learned men who prefer the opinion of St. Bonaventure, or judge it very probable, as the distinguished P. Kleutgen S.J., *Philosophie der Vorzeit*, vol. I, Treatise 4, c. 1, § 2, who as followers of this opinion cites the Conimbricenses, *Physics* IV, c. 14, q. 2, and P. Silvester Maurus, *Quaestiones philosophicae*, vol. II, q. 32.
