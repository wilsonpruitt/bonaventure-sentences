---

## Latin
<!-- page 58 -->

### Quaestio II. *Utrum omnium aeviternorum sit unum aevum.*

Secundo quaeritur, utrum spiritualia habeant mensuram *unam* per essentiam; et hoc est quaerere, utrum sit *unum aevum* omnium aeviternorum. Et quod non, videtur.

1. Dionysius de Divinis Nominibus[^1]: «Ipse est aevum aevorum»: ergo plura sunt aeva: non per interruptionem, ita quod unum post aliud: ergo per diversitatem simul existentium.

2. Item, unitas accidentis venit ab unitate subiecti[^2]; sed non est ponere unum subiectum omnium aeviternorum, cum quaedam spiritualia non habeant aliquod commune, ad cuius aspectum mensurentur: ergo non est ponere, aevum esse unum. Quod autem nullum habeant tale subiectum, quod sit contentivum spiritualium, hoc apparet, quia illud haberet excellentiam et influentiam et continentiam respectu aeviternorum, sicut primum mobile respectu temporalium; sed tale respectu substantiarum spiritualium inveniri non potest, ut patet discurrenti per singula: ergo etc.

3. Item, omnibus aliis[^3] corruptis, possibile est unicum Angelum remanere, cum non dependeat ab aliis; et si remanet in *esse* completo, habet mensuram consequentem illud *esse*, et hoc est aevum: ergo remanet aevum, quolibet Angelo remanente, et totum remanet et perfectum: ergo quot sunt Angeli, tot sunt aeva.

4. Item, aevum est mensura rei perfectae et multum habentis de specie sive de forma: si ergo[^4] aevum maxime consequitur *esse* completum, cum illud non sit unum numero, sed tantum specie, videtur, quod aevum sit unum tantum secundum speciem.

**Contra hoc:**

1. Arguitur *a simili*. Sicut se habet tempus ad temporalia, ita aevum ad aeviterna; sed tempus est unum, quod mensurat omnia temporalia, sicut Anselmus[^5] dicit et Philosophus: ergo similiter unum est aevum, quod mensurat omnia aeviterna.

2. Item, hoc videtur *a minori*. Maior est varietas et difformitas in rebus mutabilibus quam in rebus immutabilibus: si ergo mutabilia omnia possunt unica mensura per essentiam mensurari, ergo multo fortius aeviterna.

3. Item, hoc videtur *a causa*. Positio enim multorum est, quare *tempus* unum potest esse: quia, quamvis sit accidens, tamen est mensura extrinseca, sicut locus, et quia non est accidens situale; sed hoc totum invenitur in *aevo*: ergo etc.

<!-- page 59 -->

4. Item, si plura sunt aeva, aut ergo *simul*, aut *consequenter*. Non *consequenter*, quia quodlibet sit infinitum a parte post; non *simul*, quia quae sunt simul, sunt in eodem instanti, ergo si aeviterna sunt simul, sunt in eodem *nunc* aevi: ergo si omnium aeviternorum est unum *nunc*, ergo unum *aevum* per essentiam.

### Conclusio

> Licet omnium temporalium sit unum tempus, tamen quot sunt aeviterna, tot sunt aeva.

**Respondeo:** Ad huius quaestionis intelligentiam praenotandum est, qualiter intelligitur unitas in *tempore*. Sciendum autem, quod tripliciter tentaverunt aliqui unitatem *temporis* accipere, secundum quod accidens habet tripliciter comparari ad subiectum. Habet enim ibi *causam*, *existentiam* et *apparentiam*.

Dixerunt ergo aliqui, quod tempus est unum ratione subiecti, in quo primo *est* et per se, quo remoto, removetur et tempus. Unde dixerunt, quod tempus est unum, quia est in primo mobili, cuius motu cessante, cessat et tempus[^6]. — Sed illud non sufficit dicere, quia, sicut dicit Augustinus, si cessaret motus primi mobilis, adhuc posset moveri rota figuli; et constat, quod mensuraretur ille motus: ergo non tantum est ibi, immo et in aliis rebus mobilibus. *Item*, tempus est in affectionibus et cogitationibus liberi arbitrii, quod non subest motui caelesti, sicut vult Augustinus[^7].

Et ideo dixerunt alii, quod tempus est unum ratione subiecti, in quo primo *apparet*. Cum enim tempus sit numerus sive mensura motuum, et numerus iste secundum *essentiam* et *habitum* sit in re mobili vel mota, in qua est prius et posterius, secundum *actualem* vero *numerationem* sit a parte animae, sicut vult Philosophus[^8] et Augustinus, cum anima omnes motus et mutationes numeret aspiciendo ad mensuram motus primi mobilis, scilicet per diem, annum et horam; voluerunt dicere, quod unum est tempus ratione subiecti, in quo primo apparet; quia, etsi omnia habeant proprias periodos, tamen omnia numerantur et mensurantur per mensuram motus regularis et certi et nobis notissimi, scilicet motus mobilis primi. — Sed illud non sufficit, quia dicit Philosophus de Caelo et mundo[^9], quod si essent duo mobilia prima, adhuc unum esset tempus. Tunc non posset dici unum ratione *subiecti*, in quo primo *esset*, nec in quo primo *appareret*, cum utrumque esset aeque primum et evidens; nec ratione *animae*, quia tempus non est numerus numerans, sed numeratus, ut Philosophus[^10] vult, et tempus est dispositio rei extra, non fictio animae.

Et ideo dixerunt tertii profundius, quod unitas temporis sumitur ab unitate subiecti, a quo *causatur*; subiectum autem, a quo causatur, est materia ut mutabilis, et ita ut ens in potentia. Nam materia, ut est in acquisitione formae, mutatur, et sic est ens in potentia; et ideo tempus maxime inter omnia accidentia se tenet plus cum materia. Sicut igitur materia una est per *essentiam*, differens per *esse*, una non unitate *universalitatis* nec *singularitatis*, sed *medio* modo, sic et tempus in omnibus temporalibus[^11].

*Et si quaeras*, unde est hoc, quod *tempus* est unum, cum tamen *motus* sint varii; dicendum, quod *motus* definitur per id ad quod est[^12], unde motus respicit motorem et mobile et terminum; *tempus* autem respicit ipsam durationem variam et successivam, quae venit ex potentia rei mobilis. Et quia motus naturae durantis consimilis est[^13], et natura una, supra quam fundatur, est una numero; ideo tempus non tantum est unum *specie* in omnibus temporalibus, immo etiam quantum ad *essentiam* unum *numero*, differens per *esse*, sicut posuerunt omnes sapientes, qui circa hanc materiam locuti sunt.

*Si autem quaeras*, quare non sic est in aliis *quantitatibus*[^14]; omnes respiciunt materiam; dicendum, quod omnes aliae, etsi *esse incompletum* habeant in materia — nam sunt in materia dimensiones infinitae — tamen *esse completum* ipsarum est a materia existente sub forma. Tempus autem habet *esse* ex hoc, quod materia tendit ad formam, propter hoc quod causatur a motu, qui est «entelechia existentis in potentia[^15]», et ideo tenet se maxime ex parte materiae, et ideo minime distinctum.

<!-- page 60 -->

Nec dico, quod tempus sit in ipsa materia, omni forma circumscripta, quia hoc esset contra Augustinum, duodecimo Confessionum[^16], quia non est vicissitudo aliqua in materia, nisi cum in ea est aliqua forma; sed quamvis sit in materia, quae est sub forma et ab ipsa causatur, magis tamen causatur a materia, ut tendit ad formam, et hoc est in materia ratione suae potentiae.

Secundum hunc triplicem modum nisi sunt aliqui qui unitatem in *aevo* ponere, primo ratione subiecti, in quo primo *est*. Posuerunt enim ordinem in intelligentiis secundum continentiam virtualem, sicut in visibilibus est reperire continentiam localem. Et sicut tempus in primo mobili fundatur, sic aevum[^17] in prima intelligentia, quae virtualiter alias continet, in alias influit. — Sed ista positio non potest stare. Primo, quia omnes intelligentiae in incorruptione habent aequalitatem, sicut in sequenti distinctione dicitur[^18]. Secundo, quia conservatio intelligentiarum immediate pendet ex Deo. Tertio, quia, si illa intelligentia destrueretur, tunc fundamentum aevi periret; et possibile fuit, illam peccasse, immo forte peccavit, cum diabolus credatur fuisse excellentissimus Angelorum[^19].

Et ideo alii conati sunt assignare unitatem ratione subiecti, in quo primo *apparet*, quod quidem est caelum empyreum, in quo non cadit variatio; immo omnimode quietum est[^20]. — Sed nec illud sufficit, quoniam immortalitas et immutabilitas per prius est in substantiis spiritualibus quam corporalibus, cum sint digniores; et dignius non habeat mensurari per aspectum ad minus dignum.

Et ideo tentaverunt alii accipere unitatem ex parte *causae*, qui dixerunt, quod duratio intelligentiarum continuatur et conservatur per *divinam* influentiam, quae quoniam una est, unum est aevum mensurans ipsam. — Sed nec illud potest stare, quoniam influentia illa aut *active*, aut *passive* intelligitur. Si de *activa*, non est aliud quam Deus, qui non habet aevum mensuram. Si de *passiva*, tot sunt aeva, quot sunt suscipientes.

Et ideo non restat alius modus nisi per unitatem *materiae*, sicut prius dictum est de tempore. Sed multi negant, intelligentias habere materiam, et ideo minime distinctum. Nec dico, quod tempus sit in ipsa materia, omni forma circumscripta, quia hoc esset contra Augustinum, duodecimo Confessionum, quia non est vicissitudo aliqua in materia, nisi cum in ea est aliqua forma; sed quidquid sit de hoc, tamen *aevum* respicit *esse actuale* et *esse stabile*, sed *tempus* materiam ut in *potentia*. Et ideo sicut unitas temporis conformatur materiae, sic unitas aevi conformatur formae, non inquam formae, in quantum haec vel illa, sed in quantum immutabilis.

Unde sicut linea in corporibus quantumcumque diversis ponitur eadem *specie*, sic aevum habet unitatem *universalis*, non unitatem, quam habet tempus.

Et sic concedendae sunt rationes probantes, non esse unum aevum aeviternorum, sed multa[^21].

1. Quod ergo obiicitur: sicut se habet tempus ad temporalia; dicendum, quod non est simile, quia cum *tempus*, quantum est de se, respiciat variationem et motum, consequitur ipsa, quorum est mensura, ratione illius principii[^22], per quod sunt entia in potentia; et quia illud unicum dicitur esse per essentiam, ideo et tempus. *Aevum* autem de sui ratione propria respicit *esse completum* et *stabile*, et ideo magis respicit formam: et ideo non habet unitatem nisi *universalitatis* sive conformitatis, sicut et alia accidentia.

2. Quod arguitur *a minori*, quod temporalia habent unam mensuram; iam patet[^23], quod non est locus *a minori*, quia illa variatio, ratione qua dicitur esse in potentia, respicit ipsum materiale principium, et ideo facit recedere a distinctione; non sic est in aevo.

3. Quod obiicitur, quod tempus est unum, quia est accidens extrinsecum, non situatum vel situale; dicendum, quod falsum est: ista enim non est causa, sed magis illa quae praedicta est.

4. Quod obiicitur: si sunt plura aeva, aut simul, aut non simul; dicendum, quod *simul*. — Quod ergo obiicitur: ergo in eodem *nunc*; dicendum, quod esse simul in duratione est dupliciter: aut per mensurae *concomitantiam*, aut per mensurae *unitatem* et indifferentiam. Cum autem dicitur Deus, homo et Angelus esse simul; dicendum, quod hoc dicitur per mensurae *concomitantiam*. Cum autem dicitur: Petrus et Paulus simul sunt vel currunt, hoc potest dici per mensurae *unitatem* et indifferentiam; et ideo non sequitur, quod si aeva aeviternorum sunt simul, quod sint unum.

### Scholion

**I.** Hic agitur de unitate tum temporis, tum aevi, et non de unitate *specifica*, sed de unitate *secundum numerum*, ut pluries notatur in corp. — Loquendo de *tempore*, quatenus est *mensura* rei successivae et non eiusdem *intrinseca* duratio, communior sententia unitatem temporis affirmabat. In quo autem huiusmodi unitas *rationem* suam habeat, disputabatur.

<!-- page 61 -->

Tres opiniones hic recensitas affert etiam S. Thom. (hic q. 1. a. 2; S. I. q. 10. a. 6.), et ipse cum Richard. a Med. primam opinionem approbat, quae rationem unitatis ponit in unitate primi mobilis; tertia vero (quam praefert S. Bonav.), quae hanc unitatem repetit ab unitate subiecti, a quo *causatur*, quod est materia, iuxta eundem «non sufficiens videtur». Sententiae S. Thomae adhaeret B. Albert. (S. p. I. tr. 5. q. 23. m. 3. a. 2.), et quoad *perfectam* rationem temporis etiam Scotus, IV. Sent. d. 48. q. 2, et praecipue de Rerum princ. q. 20, in quo opere a quaestione 18. usque ad q. 24. de tempore et annexis quaestionibus diffuse et subtiliter disputat.

**II.** Quoad unitatem *aevi*, utrum sit admittenda, et quae sit eius ratio, maior fuit opinionum dissensus. Et praenotandum est, opinionem Aristotelis, quod etiam corpora caelestia, saltem primum mobile, inter incorruptibilia et aeviterna locum obtineant, illa aetate communiter receptam fuisse. S. Thom. in Commentar. et cum quadam restrictione etiam in Summa («concedimus ad praesens») docet, quod aevum «est unum ab unitate *esse* simplicissimi aeviternorum, quod est primus Angelus». Hanc sententiam aliquatenus approbat Richard. a Med. («quodam modo habet rationem mensurae, quamvis non plenam»). Sed Petr. a Tar. praefert opinionem secundam, «quod primum subiectum aevi est primum immobile, id est caelum empyreum». Etiam Alex. Hal. (S. p. I. q. 12. m. 9. a. 2.) unitatem aevi sustinet, et quoad *rationem* eius tertiae opinioni innititur. — E contrario S. Bonav. negat unitatem aevi et aevum ut mensuram *extrinsecam*. Si autem accipitur aevum pro mensura *intrinseca*, patet quod tot sunt admittenda aeva, quot aeviterna. Huic sententiae assentit etiam Aegid. R., et favet etiam Scotus, quia praefert opinionem, non esse unum aevum omnium aeviternorum ut mensura *extrinseca*, sicut est unum tempus omnium temporalium (hic q. 3. n. 5, q. 2. n. 10; Report. hic q. 1. n. 2, ubi dicit: «Mensurare Angelum inferiorem per *esse* Angeli superioris est aequivocare vocabulum ad mensuram perfectionis»). Tamen idem censet, etiam opinionem S. Thomae esse probabilem, si intelligitur de mensura *extrinseca*.

**III.** Praeter laudatos: B. Albert., S. p. I. tr. 5. q. 23. m. 2. a. 2. partic. 2. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 2. a. 2. — Richard. a Med., hic a. 1. q. 3. — Aegid. R., hic q. 1. a. 1. — Durand., hic q. 3. — Dionys. Carth., hic q. 2.

## English

### Question II. *Whether of all aeviternal things there is one age.*

Secondly it is asked whether spiritual things have *one* measure by essence; and this is to ask whether there is *one age* of all aeviternal things. And that there is not seems true.

1. Dionysius, *On the Divine Names*[^1]: «He is the age of ages»: therefore there are several ages: not by interruption, so that one [is] after another: therefore by the diversity of things existing simultaneously.

2. Likewise, the unity of an accident comes from the unity of the subject[^2]; but one cannot posit one subject of all aeviternal things, since certain spiritual things do not have anything in common at the regard of which they would be measured: therefore one cannot posit that the age is one. And that they have no such subject which would be containing of spiritual things, this appears, because that [subject] would have an excellence and an influence and a containment with respect to aeviternal things, just as the first movable [has] with respect to temporal things; but no such thing can be found with respect to spiritual substances, as is plain to one running through them singly: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, all other things being corrupted, it is possible for a single Angel to remain, since it does not depend on the others[^3]; and if it remains in complete *being*, it has a measure consequent upon that *being*, and this is the age: therefore the age remains, with any Angel whatever remaining, and it remains whole and perfect: therefore as many as the Angels are, so many are the ages.

4. Likewise, the age is the measure of a perfect thing and of one having much of species or of form: if therefore[^4] the age most of all follows complete *being*, since that is not one in number, but only in species, it seems that the age is one only according to species.

**Against this:**

1. It is argued *from a likeness*. As time stands to temporal things, so the age to aeviternal things; but time is one, which measures all temporal things, as Anselm[^5] says, and the Philosopher: therefore likewise the age is one, which measures all aeviternal things.

2. Likewise, this seems so *from the lesser*. There is greater variety and disformity in changeable things than in unchangeable things: if therefore all changeable things can be measured by a single measure by essence, then much more so aeviternal things.

3. Likewise, this seems so *from the cause*. For it is the position of many why *time* can be one: because, although it is an accident, it is nevertheless an extrinsic measure, like place, and because it is not a positional accident; but all this is found in *the age*: therefore, etc.

<!-- page 59 -->

4. Likewise, if there are several ages, then either *simultaneously* or *successively*. Not *successively*, because each would be infinite on the side of the after; not *simultaneously*, because things that are simultaneous are in the same instant, therefore if aeviternal things are simultaneous, they are in the same *now* of the age: therefore if of all aeviternal things there is one *now*, then one *age* by essence.

### Conclusio

> Although of all temporal things there is one time, nevertheless as many as the aeviternal things are, so many are the ages.

**I respond:** For the understanding of this question it must be premised how unity is understood in *time*. Now it must be known that in three ways some have attempted to take the unity of *time*, according as an accident can be compared in three ways to its subject. For it has there a *cause*, an *existence*, and an *appearance*.

Some, then, have said that time is one by reason of the subject in which it primarily and *per se is*, on the removal of which time too is removed. Whence they said that time is one because it is in the first movable, on whose motion ceasing, time too ceases[^6]. — But it does not suffice to say that, because, as Augustine says, if the motion of the first movable were to cease, a potter's wheel could still be moved; and it is evident that that motion would be measured: therefore it is not only there, but also in other movable things. *Likewise*, time is in the affections and thoughts of free choice, which is not subject to the celestial motion, as Augustine holds[^7].

And therefore others have said that time is one by reason of the subject in which it primarily *appears*. For since time is the number or measure of motions, and that number, according to *essence* and *habit*, is in the movable or moved thing, in which there is before and after, but according to *actual numbering* is on the part of the soul, as the Philosopher holds[^8] and Augustine — since the soul numbers all motions and changes by looking to the measure of the motion of the first movable, namely by day, year, and hour — they wished to say that time is one by reason of the subject in which it primarily appears; because, even if all things have their own periods, nevertheless all are numbered and measured by the measure of the regular and certain motion best known to us, namely the motion of the first movable. — But that does not suffice, because the Philosopher says, *On the Heavens and the World*[^9], that if there were two first movables, there would still be one time. Then it could not be called one by reason of the *subject* in which it primarily *was*, nor in which it primarily *appeared*, since each would be equally primary and evident; nor by reason of the *soul*, because time is not numbering number, but numbered, as the Philosopher[^10] holds, and time is a disposition of the thing outside, not a fiction of the soul.

And therefore a third group said more profoundly that the unity of time is taken from the unity of the subject by which it is *caused*; but the subject by which it is caused is matter as changeable, and so as a being in potency. For matter, as it is in the acquisition of form, is changed, and so is a being in potency; and therefore time, among all accidents, holds itself most with matter. As, therefore, matter is one by *essence*, differing by *being*, one not by the unity of *universality* nor of *singularity*, but in a *middle* way, so too time in all temporal things[^11].

*And if you ask* whence this is, that *time* is one, while yet *the motions* are various; it must be said that *motion* is defined by that to which it tends[^12], whence motion regards the mover and the movable and the terminus; but *time* regards the very duration, varied and successive, which comes from the potency of the movable thing. And because the motion of an enduring nature is alike[^13], and the one nature on which it is founded is one in number; therefore time is not only one in *species* in all temporal things, but even as to *essence* one in *number*, differing by *being*, as all the wise have held who have spoken about this matter.

*But if you ask* why it is not so in the other *quantities*[^14] — they all regard matter — it must be said that all the others, even if they have *incomplete being* in matter — for there are infinite dimensions in matter — nevertheless their *complete being* is from matter existing under form. But time has *being* from this, that matter tends toward form, on account of the fact that it is caused by motion, which is «the entelechy of what exists in potency[^15]», and therefore it holds itself most on the side of matter, and therefore is least distinct.

<!-- page 60 -->

Nor do I say that time is in matter itself, all form set aside, because this would be against Augustine, in the twelfth book of the *Confessions*[^16], since there is no vicissitude in matter except when there is some form in it; but although it is in matter, which is under form and is caused by it, it is nevertheless caused more by matter, as it tends toward form, and this is in matter by reason of its potency.

According to this threefold mode there were some who [tried] to posit the unity in *the age*, first by reason of the subject in which it primarily *is*. For they posited an order in the intelligences according to virtual containment, just as among visible things one finds local containment. And as time is founded in the first movable, so the age[^17] in the first intelligence, which virtually contains the others [and] flows into the others. — But this position cannot stand. First, because all the intelligences in incorruption have equality, as is said in the following distinction[^18]. Second, because the conservation of the intelligences depends immediately on God. Third, because, if that intelligence were destroyed, then the foundation of the age would perish; and it was possible for it to have sinned, indeed perhaps it did sin, since the devil is believed to have been the most excellent of the Angels[^19].

And therefore others tried to assign the unity by reason of the subject in which it primarily *appears*, which indeed is the empyrean heaven, in which no variation falls; rather it is wholly at rest[^20]. — But neither does that suffice, since immortality and immutability is prior in spiritual substances than in corporeal ones, since they are worthier; and the worthier should not be measured by regard to the less worthy.

And therefore others attempted to take the unity on the side of the *cause*, who said that the duration of the intelligences is continued and conserved by the *divine* influence, which, since it is one, the age measuring it is one. — But neither can that stand, since that influence is understood either *actively* or *passively*. If of the *active*, it is nothing other than God, who does not have the age as a measure. If of the *passive*, there are as many ages as there are receivers.

And therefore no other mode remains except by the unity of *matter*, as was said before about time. But many deny that the intelligences have matter, and therefore [it is] least distinct. Nor do I say that time is in matter itself, all form set aside, because this would be against Augustine, in the twelfth book of the *Confessions*, since there is no vicissitude in matter except when there is some form in it; but whatever may be the case concerning this, *the age* nevertheless regards *actual being* and *stable being*, but *time* [regards] matter as in *potency*. And therefore, just as the unity of time is conformed to matter, so the unity of the age is conformed to form — not, I say, to form insofar as it is this or that, but insofar as it is immutable.

Whence just as a line in bodies however diverse is posited as the same in *species*, so the age has the unity of a *universal*, not the unity which time has.

And thus the reasons proving that there is not one age of aeviternal things, but many, are to be conceded[^21].

1. As to what is objected, then: as time stands to temporal things; it must be said that it is not similar, because, since *time*, so far as it is of itself, regards variation and motion, it follows the very things of which it is the measure, by reason of that principle[^22] by which they are beings in potency; and because that single [principle] is said to be by essence, therefore time too [is one]. But *the age*, from its own proper account, regards *complete* and *stable being*, and therefore regards form more: and therefore it does not have unity except of *universality* or of conformity, like the other accidents.

2. As to what is argued *from the lesser*, that temporal things have one measure; it is already clear[^23] that there is no place for [an argument] *from the lesser*, because that variation, by reason of which it is said to be in potency, regards the material principle itself, and therefore makes [it] recede from distinction; it is not so in the age.

3. As to what is objected, that time is one because it is an extrinsic accident, not situated or positional; it must be said that it is false: for this is not the cause, but rather that which has been said before.

4. As to what is objected: if there are several ages, then either simultaneously or not simultaneously; it must be said *simultaneously*. — As to what is then objected: therefore in the same *now*; it must be said that to be simultaneous in duration is twofold: either by *concomitance* of measure, or by *unity* and indifference of measure. When it is said that God, man, and Angel are simultaneous, it must be said that this is said by *concomitance* of measure. But when it is said: Peter and Paul are or run simultaneously, this can be said by *unity* and indifference of measure; and therefore it does not follow that, if the ages of aeviternal things are simultaneous, they are one.

### Scholion

**I.** Here the subject is the unity both of time and of the age, and not the *specific* unity, but the unity *according to number*, as is noted several times in the body. — Speaking of *time*, insofar as it is a *measure* of a successive thing and not the *intrinsic* duration of that thing, the more common opinion affirmed the unity of time. But in what this unity has its *ground* was disputed.

<!-- page 61 -->

The three opinions here reviewed are also reported by St. Thomas (here q. 1, a. 2; S. I, q. 10, a. 6), and he himself, with Richard of Middleton, approves the first opinion, which places the ground of the unity in the unity of the first movable; but the third (which St. Bonaventure prefers), which traces this unity back to the unity of the subject by which it is *caused*, which is matter, according to the same [Thomas] «seems insufficient». To the opinion of St. Thomas adheres Bl. Albert (S. p. I, tr. 5, q. 23, m. 3, a. 2), and as to the *perfect* account of time also Scotus, IV Sent., d. 48, q. 2, and especially *On the Principle of Things*, q. 20, in which work from question 18 to q. 24 he disputes about time and annexed questions diffusely and subtly.

**II.** As to the unity of *the age*, whether it is to be admitted, and what its ground is, there was greater dissension of opinions. And it must be premised that the opinion of Aristotle, that even the celestial bodies, at least the first movable, hold a place among incorruptible and aeviternal things, was commonly received in that age. St. Thomas, in the Commentary and, with a certain restriction, also in the Summa («we concede for the present»), teaches that the age «is one by the unity of the *being* of the simplest of aeviternal things, which is the first Angel». This opinion Richard of Middleton in some measure approves («it has in a certain way the account of a measure, though not a full one»). But Peter of Tarentaise prefers the second opinion, «that the first subject of the age is the first immobile [thing], that is, the empyrean heaven». Alexander of Hales too (S. p. I, q. 12, m. 9, a. 2) maintains the unity of the age, and as to its *ground* relies on the third opinion. — On the contrary, St. Bonaventure denies the unity of the age and the age as an *extrinsic* measure. But if the age is taken for an *intrinsic* measure, it is plain that as many ages are to be admitted as there are aeviternal things. To this opinion Giles of Rome also assents, and Scotus too favors it, because he prefers the opinion that there is not one age of all aeviternal things as an *extrinsic* measure, just as there is one time of all temporal things (here q. 3, n. 5, q. 2, n. 10; Reportatio, here q. 1, n. 2, where he says: «To measure a lower Angel by the *being* of a higher Angel is to equivocate the term as to a measure of perfection»). Yet the same [Scotus] judges that the opinion of St. Thomas also is probable, if it is understood of an *extrinsic* measure.

**III.** Besides those praised: Bl. Albert, S. p. I, tr. 5, q. 23, m. 2, a. 2, partic. 2. — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 2, a. 2. — Richard of Middleton, here a. 1, q. 3. — Giles of Rome, here q. 1, a. 1. — Durandus, here q. 3. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 2.
