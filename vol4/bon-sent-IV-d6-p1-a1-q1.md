# IV Sent., d. 6, a. un., q. 1
## *Quid sit character secundum essentiam*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 136 -->

### ARTICULUS UNICUS. *De charactere.*
### QUAESTIO I. *Quid sit character secundum essentiam.*

Circa primum sic proceditur et ostenditur primo, quod character sit habitus:

1. « Quia tria sunt in anima, scilicet *potentiae*, *passiones* et *habitus*[^1] »; sed character non est *potentia*, quia non est in omnibus; nec *passio*, quia secundum ipsum anima non patitur: ergo est *habitus*.

2. Item, constat, characterem esse aliquam qualitatem spiritualem animae; sed « nihil est in genere aliquo determinato, quod non sit in aliqua eius specie[^2] »: cum ergo qualitas spiritualis sit in prima specie qualitatis, et illa est *habitus*, vel *dispositio*: ergo character est *habitus*, vel *dispositio*. Sed non est *dispositio*, quia non est facile mobilis: ergo *habitus*.

3. Item, si character est *habitus*, aut est *naturalis* sive innatus, aut *acquisitus*, aut *infusus*. Non innatus, hoc constat: ergo *acquisitus*, vel *infusus*; sed omnis talis habitus, cum sit in anima per modum perfectionis, est *scientia*, vel *virtus*[^4]; character autem nihil horum est: ergo non est habitus.

**Contra:** 1. Augustinus *de Bono coniugali*[^3]: Habitus est, per quem aliquis aliquid agit; sed charactere non agit homo aliquid: ergo character non est habitus.

> ### Conclusio.
> Character est habitus large sumtus, scilicet quaedam qualitas spiritualis, animam non omnino perficiens, sed disponens ad ulteriorem perfectionem.

**Respondeo:** Ad hoc dixerunt aliqui, quod characteristicatio non est aliud quam *consignatio*, et character idem est quod *signum*; et hoc signum nihil addit supra potentias animae, quod sit quid absolutum, sicut nec signatio in circulo[^5]; sed fundatur supra potentias animae nudas et est ibi secundum institutionem et actum consignantis, nulla facta mutatione; unde est pura *relatio*, ut paternitas. Et dicunt, quod verbum Philosophi[^6] intelligitur de illis quae dicunt aliquid *absolutum*. Et ponunt exemplum, quod character est in anima sicut consecratio in Ecclesia, in qua consecratione Ecclesiae non datur nova qualitas, sed solum ordinatio ab eo qui potest, ad sacra ibidem celebranda; sic dicunt, quod character est quoddam animae signaculum, in quo dicitur anima sanctificari, ut Deus in ea habitet ut in templo. Et hoc dicunt sensisse Dionysium

<!-- page 137 -->

in libro *de Ecclesiastica Hierarchia*[^7], ubi dicit: Character est signum sanctum cognitionis vel communicationis fidei et sacrae ordinationis, datum accedenti ab hierarcha. — Sed illud non potest stare: quia character dicit aliquam *assimilationem* et configurationem ad Christum, sicut character bestiae ad diabolum, de quo Apocalypsis decimo quarto[^8]; sed assimilatio fundatur super qualitatem: ergo si de novo fit similis Deo, et non fit mutatio ex parte alterius extremi secundum qualitatem; videtur, quod necesse sit dare animae aliquam qualitatem.

Propterea dixerunt aliqui, quod etsi character dicat *relationem*, dicit nihilominus *qualitatem* aliquam, super quam fundatur; et illa qualitas est sicut dispositio ad recipiendum lumen et calorem gratiae, sicut siccitas in ligno ad calorem, et pervietas in vitro ad lumen. Et ideo posuerunt characterem in *secunda specie qualitatis*[^9]. — Sed illud non potest stare, quia illa est naturalis potentia, vel impotentia, quae consequitur naturam. Et praeterea, siccitas et pervietas sunt *impotentiae* resistendi: ergo character esset *impotentia*; quod stultum est dicere.

Propterea alii dixerunt, quod character non tantum est ad *disponendum*, sed etiam ad *distinguendum*; et est sicut quaedam qualitas animam fidelem decorans et ornans, per quam ab infideli distinguitur[^10]; unde infert sensui spirituali *passionem mulcebrem*, et ideo est *passibilis qualitas* et in *tertia specie* qualitatis. — Sed illud non potest stare, quia non dicitur *passibilis qualitas*, quae infert passionem sensui *interiori* vel intellectui. Cum enim omnis veritas sit obiectum intellectus, tunc omnia essent in tertia specie qualitatis[^11]; quod stultum est dicere: ergo similiter est de charactere.

Propterea alii dixerunt, quod character, quantum est de suo nomine, importat *configurationem* — unde ubi nos habemus, ad Hebraeos primo[^12], *figura substantiae*, alia translatio dicit *character substantiae* — et ideo character est in quarta specie, quae « est *forma* et circa aliquid constans *figura* ». — Et si tu obiicias, quod figura est in quantitate et quantitas in corpore; dicunt, quod sicut duplex est *quantitas*, scilicet spiritualis et corporalis sive molis[^13]; sic duplex est *figura*. — Sed istud non videtur posse stare, quia figura est clausio terminorum, secundum quod est quarta species. Sed in spiritualibus configuratio non est similis terminorum clausioni, sed est similitudinis impressio sive assimilatio. Item, si figura ponatur in spiritualibus, non est nisi translative; sicut ergo quantitas virtutis non ponitur in genere quantitatis naturae[^14], sic nec figura in specie figurae. Et iterum, quomodo secundum qualitatem virtutis actu dicitur clausio terminorum? Et iterum, super quid fundatur illa clausio? Si super virtutem datam, vel innatam, oportet ad aliam speciem recurrere: ergo cum figura spiritualis non habeat convenientiam cum illa, videtur, quod character non dicat figuram sive configurationem in quarta specie.

Propterea alii se extendentes dicunt, quod non est in aliqua specie qualitatis a Philosopho assignata, quia ipse non assignat nisi solum species qualitatis innatae, vel acquisitae, non infusae; et

<!-- page 138 -->

hoc etiam ipse Philosophus innuit, cum dicit[^15]: « Fortassis et alii apparebunt qualitatis modi ». Et dicunt, characterem sub alia specie contineri, ut scilicet una species qualitatis sit *qualitas infusa*. — Sed illud videtur quaerere fugam, quia nos *virtutes* theologicas et alias virtutes, quae sunt infusae, in *prima specie qualitatis*[^16] collocamus; quare ergo non similiter *characterem*?

Et ideo dicendum, quod character, cum sit in anima et sit *qualitas spiritualis*, *quod est habitus*, non *passio* nec *potentia*. — Sed notandum, quod *habitus* accipitur dupliciter: uno modo *proprie*, prout distinguitur contra *dispositionem* per perfectum et imperfectum; alio modo *communiter*, prout *utrumque* comprehendit, scilicet omnem qualitatem animam disponentem, sive facile mobilem sive difficile, sive perfectam sive imperfectam[^17]. Et sic accipitur, cum dicitur: tria sunt in anima: *potentia*, *passio* et *habitus*; nam dispositio est in anima et non est *potentia* nec *passio*.

Dicendum igitur, quod si accipiatur habitus *large*, sic character est *habitus*; si autem *stricte* sic, quia character est perpetuus, ideo *habitus*; sed quia non perficit, sed disponit ad ulteriorem perfectionem, scilicet gratiae, quantum ad hoc est *dispositio*. — Similiter est in oculo corporali, quia ibi est *lumen* connaturale, inseparabile, sed insufficiens ad videndum, nisi veniat spiritus visibilis a nervo optico[^18]. Similiter in intellectu est *lumen* connaturale, inseparabile, non sufficiens ad dirigendam cognitionem, nisi superveniat *habitus scientiae*. — Secundum rem igitur character est quaedam qualitas animam non omnino perficiens, sed disponens ad ulteriorem perfectionem, scilicet gratiae. Unde potest esse, quod sit quoddam *lumen spirituale* semiplenum et quidam *calor* gratis datus; et illud lumen dicitur *signaculum* animae vel *signari* in anima, secundum illud quod dicitur in Psalmo[^19]: *Signatum est super nos lumen vultus tui, Domine*; signatum, inquam, per *naturam*, sed specialius per *Sacramenta* divina, specialissime per *dona* Spiritus sancti gratuita.

Duo igitur obiecta solvuntur, quia intelliguntur de habitu *perfecto*, qui plene rationem habitus habet. Nam ille habilitat ad opus aliquod de se, et sic vel habilitat *cognitivam*, et est *scientia*; vel *motivam*, et est *virtus*; non autem est verum de habitu, qui est *imperfectus*, disponens ad aliud, sicut *lumen naturale* ad scientiam et *lumen oculi* ad habitum videndi. Sic intelligendum est in charactere: nihilominus et aliquem actum habet, sicut patebit in sequenti problemate.

### Scholion

**I.** Contra errorem a Wikleff et posterioribus haereticis sparsum, quod nullatenus per quaedam Sacramenta imprimatur character, antiqua Ecclesiae doctrina, explicata a Concil. Florent., postea definita est a Concil. Trident. (Sess. VII. de Sacram. in genere can. 9.) sic: « Si quis dixerit, in tribus Sacramentis, baptismo scilicet, confirmatione et ordine, non imprimi characterem in anima, hoc est signum quoddam spirituale et indelebile, unde ea iterari non possunt, a. s. ». — Totam de charactere doctrinam miro modo exhibet *Breviloq.* p. VI. c. 6. — Quid autem proprie sit character, in scholis antiquitus dubium esse videbatur. Primam opinionem hic relatam secutus est Guliel. Parisiensis (*De Sacram.* c. 3.), qui dicit, eundem esse sanctitatem quandam, « cuius exemplum et figura est sanctitas basilicarum consecratarum et vasorum ». Cui fere consentit Durand. (IV. Sent. d. 4. q. I.), qui, postquam multa contra positiones S. Thomae arguit, concludit, quod « character non est aliqua natura absoluta, sed est sola relatio rationis, per quam ex institutione vel pactione divina deputatur aliquis ad sacras actiones ». De hac autem opinione, quod character non sit aliqua entitas realis, iam Thom. de Argentina

<!-- page 139 -->

recte pronuntiavit, quod « a nullo Catholico videtur esse tenenda »; de quo post Concilia Florent. et Trident. non potest esse dubium. — Scot., qui « propter solam auctoritatem Ecclesiae » ponendum esse dicit, characterem imprimi (hic q. 9. n. 14.), resolvit, eum esse quidem *reale* aliquid (hic q. 10. n. 13.), et vel *realem relationem*, vel *aliquid absolutum*; tamen ibi non determinat, quid duorum proprie sit; praestantiores tamen et plures eius discipuli docent, eum magis propendere in *formam absolutam*, quae plures involvat respectus, quatenus est *signum* et *configurativum* et *distinctivum*. Quod autem character sit *aliquid reale et absolutum*, et quidem *in genere qualitatis*, est sententia communissima; licet doctores non conveniant, in qua sit specie qualitatum ab Aristotele positarum (cfr. pag. 136. nota 3). S. Thom. (S. III. q. 63. a. 2; cfr. IV. Sent. d. 4. q. 1. a. 1. 2.) docet, eundem non esse proprie in genere vel specie, sed reduci ad *secundam speciem qualitatis*, scilicet *potentiam*, cum importet quandam potentiam spiritualem, ordinatam ad ea quae sunt divini cultus; hanc autem potentiam esse *instrumentalem* et quiddam *fluens et incompletum* (sicut est virtus physica, quae secundum ipsum est in Sacramentis; cfr. supra d. 1.) — Alex. Hal., quem sequuntur S. Bonav. et B. Albert., eum reducit ad *primam* speciem qualitatis, (scilicet *habitum*); Scotus ad primam simul et secundam. Petr. a Tar. dubius inter opinionem S. Thomae et Bonaventurae haeret; quod etiam de Richardo a Med. dici potest. — In 2. quaest. ad 1. 2. docetur, characterem non sua sola natura, ut quidam putant, sed ex institutione Dei esse signum traditae potestatis.

De hac 1. quaestione praeter laudatos: Alex. Hal., p. IV. q. 8. m. 8. a. 1. § 1. — B. Albert., hic a. 3. — Petr. a Tar., IV. Sent. d. 5. q. 2. a. 1. quaestiunc. 1. 2. — Richard. a Med., IV. Sent. d. 5. a. 2. q. I. — Dionys. Carth., omnes de charactere quaestiones in fine d. 6. q. 4. simul tractat.

## English

<!-- page 136 -->

### SOLE ARTICLE. *On the character.*
### QUESTION I. *What the character is according to its essence.*

Concerning the first [question] one proceeds thus, and it is shown first that the character is a habit:

1. « Because there are three things in the soul, namely *powers*, *passions*, and *habits*[^1] »; but the character is not a *power*, since it is not in all [souls]; nor a *passion*, since according to it the soul does not undergo anything: therefore it is a *habit*.

2. Likewise, it is established that the character is some spiritual quality of the soul; but « nothing is in any determinate genus that is not in some species of it[^2] »: since therefore a spiritual quality is in the first species of quality, and that is a *habit* or a *disposition*: therefore the character is a *habit* or a *disposition*. But it is not a *disposition*, since it is not easily movable: therefore a *habit*.

3. Likewise, if the character is a *habit*, it is either *natural* or innate, or *acquired*, or *infused*. That it is not innate, this is established: therefore [it is] *acquired* or *infused*; but every such habit, since it is in the soul by way of perfection, is *knowledge* or *virtue*[^4]; but the character is none of these: therefore it is not a habit.

**On the contrary:** 1. Augustine, *On the Good of Marriage*[^3]: A habit is that by which someone does something; but by the character a man does not do anything: therefore the character is not a habit.

> ### Conclusion.
> The character is a habit taken broadly, namely a certain spiritual quality, not perfecting the soul entirely, but disposing it toward a further perfection.

**I respond:** To this some have said that the imprinting-of-a-character is nothing other than a *consigning*, and the character is the same as a *sign*; and this sign adds nothing over the powers of the soul that would be something absolute, just as neither does the marking on a circle[^5]; but it is founded upon the bare powers of the soul and is there according to the institution and act of the one consigning, with no change made; whence it is a pure *relation*, like paternity. And they say that the saying of the Philosopher[^6] is to be understood of those things which signify something *absolute*. And they offer the example that the character is in the soul as the consecration in a church, in which consecration of a church no new quality is given, but only an ordination by him who has the power, for celebrating sacred things therein; thus they say that the character is a certain seal of the soul, in which the soul is said to be sanctified, so that God may dwell in it as in a temple. And they say that Dionysius held this

<!-- page 137 -->

in the book *On the Ecclesiastical Hierarchy*[^7], where he says: The character is a holy sign of the knowledge or communication of faith and of sacred ordination, given to one approaching from the hierarch. — But that cannot stand: because the character implies some *assimilation* and configuration to Christ, as the character of the beast [implies configuration] to the devil, concerning which Apocalypse fourteen[^8]; but assimilation is founded upon a quality: therefore if [a soul] becomes anew similar to God, and no change is made on the part of the other extreme according to quality; it seems that it is necessary to give to the soul some quality.

Therefore some have said that, although the character implies a *relation*, it nevertheless implies some *quality* upon which it is founded; and that quality is like a disposition for receiving the light and warmth of grace, as dryness in wood for warmth, and transparency in glass for light. And therefore they placed the character in the *second species of quality*[^9]. — But that cannot stand, because that [second species] is a natural power, or impotence, which follows upon nature. And besides, dryness and transparency are *impotences* of resisting: therefore the character would be an *impotence*; which is foolish to say.

Therefore others have said that the character is not only for *disposing*, but also for *distinguishing*; and it is like a certain quality adorning and ornamenting the faithful soul, by which it is distinguished from the unfaithful[^10]; whence it brings to the spiritual sense a *soothing passion*, and therefore is a *passible quality* and in the *third species* of quality. — But that cannot stand, because that is not called a *passible quality* which brings a passion to the *interior* sense or to the intellect. For since every truth is the object of the intellect, then all things would be in the third species of quality[^11]; which is foolish to say: therefore it is likewise concerning the character.

Therefore others have said that the character, as far as concerns its name, implies *configuration* — whence where we have, to the Hebrews chapter one[^12], *the figure of the substance*, another translation says *the character of the substance* — and therefore the character is in the fourth species, which « is *form* and *figure* constant about something ». — And if you object that figure is in quantity and quantity is in body; they say that just as *quantity* is twofold, namely spiritual and corporeal or of mass[^13]; so *figure* is twofold. — But that does not seem able to stand, because figure is the enclosure of boundaries, insofar as it is the fourth species. But in spiritual things configuration is not similar to an enclosure of boundaries, but is an impression of likeness or an assimilation. Likewise, if figure be placed in spiritual things, it is only transferredly; therefore just as the quantity of virtue is not placed in the genus of quantity of nature[^14], so neither is figure in the species of figure. And again, in what manner is an enclosure of boundaries said according to the quality of virtue in act? And again, upon what is that enclosure founded? If upon a given or innate virtue, it is necessary to have recourse to another species: therefore since spiritual figure has no agreement with that [fourth species], it seems that the character does not imply figure or configuration in the fourth species.

Therefore others, extending themselves further, say that it is not in any species of quality assigned by the Philosopher, because he does not assign except only the species of quality that is innate or acquired, not infused; and

<!-- page 138 -->

this too the Philosopher himself intimates, when he says[^15]: « Perhaps other modes of quality also will appear ». And they say that the character is contained under another species, namely so that one species of quality be *infused quality*. — But that seems to seek an escape, because we place the theological *virtues* and other virtues, which are infused, in the *first species of quality*[^16]; why therefore not likewise the *character*?

And therefore it must be said that the character, since it is in the soul and is a *spiritual quality*, *is a habit* (*quod est habitus*), not a *passion* nor a *power*. — But it must be noted that *habit* is taken in two ways: in one way *properly*, insofar as it is distinguished against *disposition* by the perfect and the imperfect; in another way *commonly*, insofar as it comprehends *both*, namely every quality disposing the soul, whether easily movable or with difficulty, whether perfect or imperfect[^17]. And it is taken thus, when it is said: there are three things in the soul: *power*, *passion*, and *habit*; for a disposition is in the soul and is not a *power* nor a *passion*.

It must be said therefore that if habit be taken *broadly*, then the character is a *habit*; but if [taken] *strictly* thus, then, because the character is perpetual, therefore [it is] a *habit*; but because it does not perfect, but disposes toward a further perfection, namely of grace, with respect to this it is a *disposition*. — It is likewise in the bodily eye, because there is there a connatural, inseparable *light*, but insufficient for seeing, unless the visible spirit come from the optic nerve[^18]. Likewise in the intellect there is a connatural, inseparable *light*, not sufficient for directing cognition, unless the *habit of knowledge* supervene. — According to the thing, therefore, the character is a certain quality not perfecting the soul entirely, but disposing it toward a further perfection, namely of grace. Whence it can be that it is a certain *spiritual light* half-full and a certain *warmth* freely given; and that light is called the *seal* of the soul or to be *signed* in the soul, according to that which is said in the Psalm[^19]: *The light of thy countenance is signed upon us, O Lord*; signed, I say, through *nature*, but more specially through the divine *Sacraments*, most specially through the gratuitous *gifts* of the Holy Spirit.

The two objections, therefore, are resolved, because they are understood of a *perfect* habit, which fully has the account of a habit. For that one enables [the soul] toward some work of itself, and thus either it enables the *cognitive* [power], and is *knowledge*; or the *motive* [power], and is *virtue*; but it is not true of a habit which is *imperfect*, disposing toward something else, as *natural light* [disposes] toward knowledge and the *light of the eye* toward the habit of seeing. So it must be understood in the case of the character: nonetheless it does have some act, as will be evident in the following problem.

### Scholion

**I.** Against the error spread by Wycliffe and later heretics, that in no way is a character imprinted through certain Sacraments, the ancient doctrine of the Church, explained at the Council of Florence, was afterward defined at the Council of Trent (Session VII, on the Sacraments in general, canon 9) thus: « If anyone shall say that in the three Sacraments, namely baptism, confirmation, and order, a character is not imprinted in the soul, that is a certain spiritual and indelible sign, whence they cannot be repeated, let him be anathema ». — The whole doctrine concerning the character the *Breviloquium*, p. VI, c. 6, sets forth in a wonderful manner. — But what the character properly is, in the schools of old seemed to be a matter of doubt. The first opinion related here was followed by William of Paris (*On the Sacraments* c. 3), who says that it is a certain holiness, « of which an exemplar and figure is the holiness of consecrated basilicas and vessels ». With him Durandus nearly agrees (IV Sent., d. 4, q. I), who, after arguing much against the positions of St. Thomas, concludes that « the character is not any absolute nature, but is solely a relation of reason, by which, from divine institution or compact, someone is deputed to sacred actions ». But concerning this opinion, that the character is not any real entity, already Thomas of Strasbourg

<!-- page 139 -->

rightly declared that « it seems to be held by no Catholic »; concerning which, after the Councils of Florence and Trent, there can be no doubt. — Scotus, who says that it must be posited « on account of the sole authority of the Church » that a character is imprinted (here q. 9, n. 14), resolves that it is indeed something *real* (here q. 10, n. 13), and either a *real relation* or *something absolute*; yet there he does not determine which of the two it properly is; the more eminent and the majority of his disciples, however, teach that he inclines rather to an *absolute form*, which involves several respects, insofar as it is a *sign* and *configurative* and *distinctive*. But that the character is *something real and absolute*, and indeed *in the genus of quality*, is the most common opinion; although the doctors do not agree on which species of the qualities posited by Aristotle it is in (cfr. p. 136, note 3). St. Thomas (*Summa* III, q. 63, a. 2; cfr. IV Sent., d. 4, q. 1, a. 1. 2) teaches that it is not properly in a genus or species, but is reduced to the *second species of quality*, namely *power*, since it implies a certain spiritual power, ordered toward the things that pertain to divine worship; and that this power is *instrumental* and something *flowing and incomplete* (as is physical virtue, which according to him is in the Sacraments; cfr. above d. 1). — Alexander of Hales, whom St. Bonaventure and B. Albert follow, reduces it to the *first* species of quality (namely *habit*); Scotus to the first and the second at once. Peter of Tarentaise hesitates in doubt between the opinion of St. Thomas and of Bonaventure; which can also be said of Richard of Mediavilla. — In the 2nd question, ad 1. 2, it is taught that the character is a sign of a transmitted power, not by its own nature alone, as some suppose, but from the institution of God.

Concerning this 1st question, besides those cited: Alexander of Hales, p. IV, q. 8, m. 8, a. 1, § 1. — B. Albert, here a. 3. — Peter of Tarentaise, IV Sent., d. 5, q. 2, a. 1, quaestiunc. 1. 2. — Richard of Mediavilla, IV Sent., d. 5, a. 2, q. I. — Dionysius the Carthusian treats all the questions concerning the character together at the end of d. 6, q. 4.
