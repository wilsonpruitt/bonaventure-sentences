### Articulus Unicus. *De consensu coacto.*
### Quaestio I. *Utrum consensus coactus faciat matrimonium.*

Circa primum ostenditur, quod consensus coactus faciat matrimonium; et quod sic, videtur:

1. Quia Augustinus[^1] dicit, quod «coacta voluntas voluntas est»: ergo cum consensus sit voluntatis, consensus est; sed «posita causa, ponitur effectus»: ergo posito tali consensu, est matrimonium.

2. Item, hoc videtur ratione per simile: quia qui baptizari consentit timore mortis, baptizatus est et pro baptizato habetur[^2]: ergo similiter est in matrimonio.

3. Item, hoc videtur per rationem: quia consensus non potest cogi, sed tantum induci[^3], sed sicut inducitur minis, ita inducitur blanditiis; sed si aliquis inductus blanditiis consentiat in matrimonium, matrimonium tenet: ergo similiter, si inducitur minis.

4. Item, qui negat Christum timore mortis, peccat; sed nullus peccat nisi liber[^4]: ergo consentiens in aliquid timore mortis libere consentit. Sed consensus liber facit matrimonium: ergo qui consentit timore mortis etc.

**Contra:**

1. Decretalis, de Sponsalibus et matrimonio[^5]: «Cum locum non habeat consensus, ubi metus, vel coactio intercedit, necesse est, ut ubi consensus communis requiritur, coactionis materia repellatur»: ergo etc.

2. Item, consensus matrimonialis signum est coniunctionis animae et Dei[^6]; sed anima non coniungitur Deo consensu coacto: ergo si signum est congruum, matrimonium non contrahitur consensu timore coacto.

3. Item, nunquam anima fit sponsa Dei, nisi uniatur per amorem: ergo mulier[^7] nunquam fit sponsa viri, nisi uniatur per amorem; sed unio, quae est per consensum coactum, non fit per amorem: ergo etc.

4. Item, in consensu matrimonii acquiritur potestas[^8] in alterutrum: ergo necesse est, quod in consentiendo sint aequales; sed cum alter cogit alterum ad consentiendum, non sunt aequales: ergo tali consensu non contrahitur matrimonium.

> **Conclusio.** Consensus coactus vel vi, vel metu cadente in virum constantem non facit matrimonium, nisi post coactionem in carnalem copulam consentiatur.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod consensus coactus potest esse dupliciter, scilicet vi, vel metu. «Vis est maioris rei impetus, qui repelli non potest[^9]»; metus est instantis periculi trepidatio. Tunc autem fit vis, quando capitur, ducitur, trahitur invitus, vel ligatus, ipso reclamante et renitente; haec est coactio, in qua non est consensus, et in hac omnino non est matrimonium. — Est et alia coactio per metum, ut cum aliquis minatur mortem vel intentat, nisi consentiat; et de hac coactione distinguunt aliqui, quod talis consentiens aut consentit ore, sed non corde, et tunc non est matrimonium aliquo modo; aut corde consentit, quamvis coactus, et tunc matrimonium est in conspectu Dei, quia «voluntas coacta voluntas est»; sed non est in conspectu Ecclesiae, quae praesumit, quod talis non consenserit corde; et ideo sic iudicat et sententiat, ut uterque alteri in Domino nubat[^10]. — Sed certe istud non videtur consentire decretali praedictae, quae dicit, quod «consensus non habet locum, ubi metus, vel coactio intercedit».

<!-- page 700 -->

Et ideo distinguitur aliter, quod metus duplex est, quidam levis, quidam iustus: iustus, utpote «qui in virum constantem cadere potest[^11]»; levis est, cum quis parvum malum minatur, vel cum talis minatur, de quo praesumitur, quod implere non possit; et talis metus non facit consensum adeo defectivum, quod impediat matrimonium contrahendum, vel dirimat iam contractum, sed consensus cum tali metu facit matrimonium. Tunc autem est metus iustus, quando aliquis comminatur damnum mortis, mutilationis, servitutis et stupri[^12]; tunc autem cadit in virum constantem, quando praesumitur probabiliter, quod ille qui minatur, possit minas implere; non enim esset timendum, si simplex rusticus minaretur duci. In hoc metu dicunt iura consensum non facere matrimonium.

Sed ratio huius sumitur a diversis diversimode. Quidam enim dicunt, quod hoc est ex indulgentia iuris. Iura enim indulgent, tales separari, primum, quia praesumunt, consentientem ore non consensisse corde, et quia verbum solum dixit, ut poenam redimeret; vel etiam ratione finis, quia coacta matrimonia pessimos exitus consueverunt habere[^13]. — Alii dicunt, quod non ex iuris indulgentia, sed hoc facit defectus perfecti et liberi consensus, qui debet esse in matrimonio, quia, sicut ostensum est, plenus debet ibi esse consensus; et hoc videtur probabilius. Si tamen post coactionem in carnalem copulam consensit, ex tunc incipit esse matrimonium[^14].

*Ad 1.* Ad illud ergo quod primo obiicitur, quod coacta voluntas voluntas est; dicendum, quod verum est; sed nunquam sufficit talis voluntas ad faciendum bonum[^15], nec etiam ad contrahendum matrimonium, pro eo quod Dominus voluit, hoc Sacramentum contrahi per consensum liberum, non coactum.

*Ad 2.* Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod qui coactus consentit in baptismum baptizatur; respondetur, quod baptismus est opus Dei, non hominum, sed matrimonium est a consensu hominum. — Aliter potest dici, quod non est simile: quia non tanta libertas ibi requiritur, quanta in matrimonio; totum enim esse matrimonii a consensu causatur[^16].

*Ad 3.* Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod consensus inducitur per blanditias; dicendum, quod blanditiae non ita detrahunt de ratione consensus, sicut minae, quia blanditiae ad amorem excitant, sed minae ad terrorem.

*Ad 4.* Ad illud quod obiicitur de eo qui negat Christum timore, dicendum, quod facilius est peccare quam bene facere[^17], unde plura exiguntur ad construendum bonum quam ad faciendum malum. — Praeterea, nullus timor respectu peccati perpetrandi potest nec debet cadere rationabiliter in virum constantem, sed respectu alicuius rei faciendae, quae non est nostrae saluti contraria; sicut vir constans rationabiliter prius deberet permittere, se interfici, quam Deum negaret[^18]; sed non rationabiliter ageret, si dimitteret, se interfici, antequam in aliquid indifferens consentiret; matrimonium autem non est de his quae sunt saluti contraria vel contra salutem. Et sic patet, quomodo intelligitur de metu, qui potest cadere in constantem virum.

### Article Unique. *On coerced consent.*
### Question I. *Whether a coerced consent effects marriage.*

Concerning the first it is shown that a coerced consent effects marriage; and that it is so, it seems:

1. Because Augustine[^1] says that "a coerced will is [still] a will": therefore, since consent is of the will, it is a consent; but "a cause being posited, an effect is posited": therefore, such a consent being posited, marriage exists.

2. Likewise, this seems so by an argument from a like case: because one who consents to be baptized out of fear of death is baptized and is held as baptized[^2]: therefore it is likewise in marriage.

3. Likewise, this seems so by reason: because consent cannot be coerced, but only induced[^3]; but just as it is induced by threats, so it is induced by blandishments; but if someone induced by blandishments consents to marriage, the marriage holds: therefore likewise if he is induced by threats.

4. Likewise, one who denies Christ out of fear of death sins; but no one sins unless free[^4]: therefore one who consents to something out of fear of death consents freely. But a free consent effects marriage: therefore one who consents out of fear of death, etc.

**On the contrary:**

1. The Decretal, On Betrothals and Marriage[^5]: "Since consent has no place where fear or coercion intervenes, it is necessary that, where a common consent is required, the matter of coercion be excluded": therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, the matrimonial consent is a sign of the conjoining of the soul and God[^6]; but the soul is not conjoined to God by a coerced consent: therefore, if it is a fitting sign, marriage is not contracted by a consent coerced through fear.

3. Likewise, the soul never becomes the bride of God unless it is united through love: therefore the woman[^7] never becomes the bride of the man unless she is united through love; but a union which comes about through a coerced consent does not come about through love: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, in the consent of marriage a power[^8] is acquired over one another: therefore it is necessary that they be equal in consenting; but since one compels the other to consent, they are not equal: therefore marriage is not contracted by such a consent.

> **Conclusion.** A consent coerced either by force, or by fear falling upon a steadfast man, does not effect marriage, unless after the coercion consent is given to the carnal union.

**I respond:** It must be said that a coerced consent can be twofold, namely by force, or by fear. "Force is the onset of a greater thing, which cannot be repelled[^9]"; fear is the trepidation of an imminent danger. Now force occurs when one is seized, led, dragged unwilling, or bound, while he himself protests and resists; this is coercion in which there is no consent, and in this case there is absolutely no marriage. — There is also another coercion through fear, as when someone threatens or intends death unless [the other] consents; and concerning this coercion some distinguish, that one who thus consents either consents with the mouth but not with the heart, and then there is no marriage in any way; or he consents with the heart, although coerced, and then the marriage exists in the sight of God, because "a coerced will is [still] a will"; but it is not in the sight of the Church, which presumes that such a one has not consented with the heart; and therefore it so judges and pronounces, that each may marry the other in the Lord[^10]. — But surely this does not seem to agree with the aforesaid decretal, which says that "consent has no place where fear or coercion intervenes."

<!-- page 700 -->

And therefore it is distinguished otherwise, that fear is twofold, one light, another just: just, as being one "which can fall upon a steadfast man[^11]"; it is light when someone threatens a small evil, or when such a one threatens of whom it is presumed that he cannot carry it out; and such fear does not make the consent so defective as to impede the contracting of marriage, or to dissolve one already contracted, but a consent given under such fear effects marriage. Now the fear is just when someone threatens the harm of death, mutilation, servitude, and violation[^12]; and it falls upon a steadfast man when it is probably presumed that he who threatens can carry out the threats; for it would not need to be feared, if a simple rustic were to threaten to be led away. In this fear the laws say that consent does not effect marriage.

But the reason for this is taken by different people in different ways. For some say that this is from an indulgence of the law. For the laws indulge that such persons be separated, first, because they presume that one who consents with the mouth has not consented with the heart, and because he said the word only that he might buy off the penalty; or also by reason of the end, because coerced marriages are wont to have the worst outcomes[^13]. — Others say that this comes not from an indulgence of the law, but from a defect of a perfect and free consent, which ought to be present in marriage, because, as has been shown, a full consent ought to be there; and this seems more probable. If, however, after the coercion he consents to the carnal union, from then it begins to be a marriage[^14].

*To the first.* To the first, then, which was objected, that a coerced will is [still] a will: it must be said that this is true; but such a will never suffices to do good[^15], nor even to contract marriage, since it is the Lord's will that this Sacrament be contracted through a free consent, not a coerced one.

*To the second.* To that which is objected, that one who is coerced and consents to baptism is baptized: it is answered that baptism is a work of God, not of men, but marriage is from the consent of men. — It can be said otherwise, that it is not similar: because as much freedom is not required there as in marriage; for the whole being of marriage is caused by consent[^16].

*To the third.* To that which is objected, that consent is induced through blandishments: it must be said that blandishments do not detract from the nature of consent as much as threats do, because blandishments incite to love, but threats to terror.

*To the fourth.* To that which is objected concerning one who denies Christ out of fear: it must be said that it is easier to sin than to do good[^17], whence more is required for the building up of good than for the doing of evil. — Moreover, no fear with respect to the perpetrating of a sin can or ought reasonably to fall upon a steadfast man, but only with respect to some deed which is not contrary to our salvation; just as a steadfast man ought reasonably first to allow himself to be killed, rather than deny God[^18]; but he would not act reasonably if he allowed himself to be killed before consenting to something indifferent; but marriage is not among the things which are contrary to salvation or against salvation. And thus it is clear how this is to be understood concerning the fear which can fall upon a steadfast man.

### Scholion

**I.** The distinction of a twofold coerced consent is common [to the doctors]. On the relation of each to the voluntary, see more in II Sent. d. 25, p. II, q. 5; d. 24, p. I, a. 2, q. 3. — In that ancient controversy, whether a consent coerced by a fear falling upon a steadfast man renders marriage void by ecclesiastical law only, or also by divine law, Durandus stands for the first part along with not a few later writers; but that it is voided by divine law is held — along with St. Thomas, Scotus, Peter of Tarantaise, Richard of Middleton, and others — by St. Bonaventure, as more probable. Cf. also below, dub. 2, 3. — On question 1: Scotus (on this and the two following questions), in both Scripta, here q. unica. — St. Thomas, here a. 1, 2, 3; Suppl., q. 47, a. 1, 2, 3. — Bl. Albert, here a. 1–4. — Peter of Tarantaise, here a. 1, 2. — Richard of Middleton, here a. 1, q. 1. — Durandus, here q. 1, 2. — Dionysius the Carthusian, on this and the two following questions, here q. unica.

**II.** St. Thomas expresses question 2 thus (in the places cited, a. 3, quaestiuncula 2; Suppl., a. 4): whether a coerced consent effects marriage on the part of the one who coerces. Richard of Middleton also treats the same question explicitly, here a. 1, q. 3.

<!-- page 701 -->

**III.** Concerning the solution to question 3, all agree. But what is determined in the solution to [question] 3 concerning the power of the Supreme Pontiff, that he can command the acceptance of a bishopric — this St. Thomas approves (in the places cited, a. 4, ad 4; a. 6, ad 4), but there he makes no mention of that restriction which Bonaventure lays down, namely concerning one who knows his own insufficiency and reasonably fears the peril of his soul. — On the question itself, besides St. Thomas: Scotus, loc. cit., n. 12. — [?] Albert, above, d. 28, a. 4. — Peter of Tarantaise, here a. 4. — Richard of Middleton, here a. 1, q. 4.
