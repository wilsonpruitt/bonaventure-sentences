---

## Latin
<!-- page 280 -->

### Quaestio III. *Utrum accidentia illa operationes habeant per naturam, an per miraculum.*

Tertio quaeritur de operationibus, quas habent illa accidentia, utrum habeant per naturam an per miraculum. Et quaeritur primo de immutationis operatione, quae est illarum specierum, utrum sit naturalis, vel miraculosa. Et quod naturalis, videtur:

1. Quia quod per se inest alicui non solum inest illi coniuncto materiae, sed etiam abstracto et separato; sed movere sensus per se inest sensibilibus[^4]: ergo per se inest illis a materia separatis; sed quae per se insunt naturaliter insunt: ergo etc.

2. Item, forma substantialis nunquam movet sensum separata a formis accidentalibus[^5]: ergo forma substantialis nihil facit, nihil privat, quin accidentia moveant sensum, dummodo accidentia habeant esse: ergo per naturam movent.

3. Item, sensus est susceptivus specierum praeter materiam, tamen sub conditionibus materialibus hic et nunc[^6]: si ergo est aliqua species sensibilis sine materia, habens tamen materiales conditiones, illa maxime habet movere sensum: ergo cum huiusmodi sint istae species, patet etc.

4. Item, hoc patet per simile: si idolum[^7] relinqueretur in speculo, abstracto corpore obiecto, moveret visum, et tamen idolum in speculo habet esse spirituale: ergo videtur, quod species illae, cum sint ibi relictae sine materia, quod habent naturaliter immutare.

5. Item, si navis per miraculum levaretur in aëra, licet existentia navis in aëre esset miraculosa, tamen existentia nautae in navi esset naturalis, et operationes consequentes illam existentiam essent naturales: cum igitur accidentia fundentur in quantitate, et quantitas in materia[^8]; sublevatio quantitatis a materia est miraculosa; sed existentia aliorum accidentium est naturalis: ergo operatio in sensus consequens illam existentiam est naturalis.

**Contra:** 1. Philosophus in primo de Anima[^9]: «Recto, in quantum rectum, contingit tangere aeneam sphaeram secundum punctum, tangit autem nunquam separatum», quia aenea sphaera non est nata tangi nisi a naturali: si ergo rectum separatum tangeret aeneam sphaeram, hoc esset supra naturam, vel contra. Cum igitur sensus particularis non sit natus immutari nisi a specie existente in materia; si immutetur a separata, immutatio ista non est naturalis.

2. Item, sicut se habet sensus interior ad speciem sine materia, sic exterior ad speciem in materia; et permutatim, sicut interior ad speciem in materia, sic exterior ad speciem sine materia; sed interior nunquam naturaliter apprehendit speciem in materia, sed abstractam[^1b]: ergo nec exterior speciem separatam a materia.

<!-- page 281 -->

3. Item, formae convenit esse hic et nunc ratione materiae[^2b]; sed per eam naturam, per quam est hic et nunc, habet movere sensum: ergo cum hoc sit a materia, naturaliter ergo habet movere: ergo si fiat separatio a materia, iam non movebit naturaliter.

4. Item, illa species motiva sensus habet triplicem comparationem, ad subiectum scilicet, ad medium et ad organum[^3b], et sunt istae comparationes ordinatae: ergo destructa prima, et destruuntur aliae consequenter: ergo si habeat species illa comparationes duas sequentes sine prima, non habet naturaliter, sed miraculose.

Secundo quaeritur de opere transmutationis et maxime de putrefactione, utrum scilicet sit naturalis. Et quod sic, videtur:

1. Quia quod convenit alicui naturaliter, salvata natura, adhuc illi convenit; sed speciebus illis naturaliter convenit corrumpi et deficere propter defectum virtutis naturalis, et quia causantur a contrariis qualitatibus[^4b]; et constat, quod illae species salvantur in eadem natura: ergo etc.

2. Item, corruptio per contrarietatem est naturalis, hoc patet; sed species illae ibi existentes habent vere naturam contrarietatis: ergo sunt corruptibiles naturaliter. Minor patet sic: ubi contraria sunt secundum esse spirituale, simul possunt esse in eodem et secundum idem, ut in eodem medio, ut in sensu et intellectu contrarii colores[^5b]: cum ergo hic non possint esse contrarii colores nec sapores simul, immo mutuo se expellunt: habent igitur naturam verae contrarietatis.

3. Item, ubi sunt species secundum spirituale esse, excellentia talis speciei non est corruptiva[^6b]: si ergo in speciebus sacramentalibus excellentia sensibilium contristat sensum: ergo est ibi species habens naturam verae contrarietatis. Sed quod sic est corruptibile est naturaliter: ergo etc.

4. Item, Anselmus, de Conceptu virginali[^7b], distinguit triplicem cursum: naturalem, voluntarium et mirabilem, et dicit, quod mirabilis a Deo administratur immediate: cum igitur Deus nihil immediate corrumpat, illa corruptio sive putrefactio cum non sit a Deo immediate: ergo non est mirabilis, ergo est naturalis, vel voluntaria; sed non voluntaria: ergo naturalis.

**Contra:** 1. «Forma de sui natura est in simplici et invariabili essentia consistens[^8b]»: si ergo accidat ei variatio, hoc est per materiam: ergo separata a materia est invariabilis per naturam. Si ergo varietur, hoc est supra naturam: ergo etc.

2. Item, ad transmutationem physicam concurrunt tria principia, scilicet subiectum et duo contraria[^9b]: ergo ubi deficit materia, non est intelligi mutationem physicam; sed hoc est in huiusmodi speciebus: ergo putrefactio illa non est physica.

3. Item, «sicut a forma est agere, ita est a materia pati[^10b]»; sed non contingit agere physice nisi per formam: ergo nec pati nisi per materiam: ergo ubi deficit materia, manifestum est, quod non est passio physica: ergo nec transmutatio.

4. Item, non est vere contrarietas sine subiecto; sed materia prima est subiectum omnis contrarietatis corporalis[^11b]: ergo ubi deficit prima materia, non est vera contrarietas. Sed actio naturalis est per veram contrarietatem: ergo etc. Similiter posset argui de contactu, quod est formae in materia, et actio et passio per contactum.

Tertio quaeritur de opere nutritionis, utrum sit naturalis, vel supernaturalis[^12b].

> **Conclusio.** Duplici via probatur, operationes immutationis, transmutationis et nutritionis partim esse naturales, partim miraculosas.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum ad intelligentiam praedictorum secundum unum modum dicendi satis probabilem[^13b] ex comparatione virtutis ad operationes

<!-- page 282 -->

ex virtute egredientes. Operationes enim habent regulari secundum potentias et virtutes, a quibus egrediuntur.

Propter quod notandum, quod aliquando datur alicui potestas conformis suae naturae; et egressus ab illa potentia est naturalis, sicut cum caeco redditur potentia videndi. — Aliquando datur potestas alicui partim conformis naturae et partim supra naturam; et tunc actus egrediens quodam modo est naturalis, quodam modo supra naturam, sicut caeco videre de nocte. — Aliquando vero datur potestas omnino supra naturam; et tunc actus egrediens ab illa potentia est miraculosus simpliciter et supra naturam, sicut Virgini data est potestas pariendi[^1c].

Considerandum est igitur, quod in hoc Sacramento est haec omnia reperire. Nam primo quantitas naturaliter habet substare aliis accidentibus, et alia accidentia naturaliter sustentari[^2c] ab illa. Si ergo detur quantitati potentia sustentandi alia accidentia, illorum existentia in quantitate est naturalis, et operatio consequens habet potentiam naturalem. Unde dico, quod operationes illarum specierum in sensus sunt naturales, quia in quantitate habent esse conveniens immutationi sensuum.

Omnes igitur rationes hoc ostendentes sunt concedendae.

Ad oppositiones manifesta est responsio: quia quantitas est ibi loco materiae, et per illam sunt hic et nunc et habent esse materiale et conveniens immutationi sensuum, et accidentia primo sunt in quantitate; et hoc potest satis videri per exemplum supra de navi positum. — Quod vero datur ulterius quantitati illi potentia sustinendi transmutationes illarum formarum[^3c]; talis potentia partim est conformis naturae, partim supra naturam. Conformis est quantum ad subsistentiam contrarietatis, quia quantitati per se insunt contraria; sed supra quantum ad hoc, quod manet eadem secundum se, facta fractione: hoc enim est proprium solius substantiae[^4c], et ita supra naturam quantitatis.

Ad illud ergo quod quaeritur de transmutatione, utrum sit naturalis, vel miraculosa; dicendum, quod ratione naturarum illarum specierum, quae habent naturam contrarietatis, illa transmutatio est naturalis, sed ratione sustinentis[^5c] secundum hoc est supra naturam; et ita partim naturalis, partim supra naturam.

Rationes igitur ad partem oppositam factae in secundo problemate currunt secundum has duas vias. Nam quae probant, quod sunt naturales, sumuntur a natura contrarietatis formarum illarum; quae vero probant quod sunt supra naturam, procedunt secundum viam sustinentis. — Et sic patet secundo quaesitum de transmutatione. Si vero datur illis accidentibus potentia convertendi in substantiam, cum desinunt esse, et cum corpus Christi ibi desinit esse; talis potentia omnino est supra naturam, et ideo talis actus omnino supra naturam est et miraculosus.

Ad illud quod quaeritur de nutritione, dicendum, quod ibi triplex est operatio[^6c]. Prima est, quod species illae per actionem virtutis agentis transmutantur; et haec partim est naturalis, partim supra naturam. — Secunda est, quod in fine transmutationis cedunt in substantiam, ita quod in illam convertuntur, sicut converteretur substantia panis, si esset praesens; et haec est miraculosa[^7c]. — Tertia est, quod substantia illa postea convertitur in carnem; et haec est simpliciter naturalis.

Sed quamvis iste modus dicendi probabiliter possit sustineri, aliter potest dici et securius et brevius, quod operationes accidentium sive virtus operans habet comparari et ad illud, a quo egreditur, et ad illud, in quod operatur. Si loquamur per comparationem ad illud a quo; sic dico, quod cum per naturam omnis virtus operans vel sit substantialis, vel saltem ab illa dependeat et reguletur; quod substantia circumscripta, si ulterius accidens operetur, ita miraculum est, quod operetur per se, sicut miraculum est, quod per se manet; et ita omnis operatio est miraculosa. — Si autem loquamur per comparationem ad obiectum, sive sit[^8c] organum sensus, sive aliud quodcumque corpus, sic dico, quod accidentia multas habent ibi operationes naturales; unde quod albedo movet visum et sapor gustum, hoc non est supra naturam nec

<!-- page 283 -->

contra, sed secundum naturam. Quoniam ergo albedo non separatur ibi, ut habeat esse mathematicum, sed salvatur esse naturale et singulare[^1d]; ideo illae operationes, quae inerant albedini et aliis accidentibus, adhuc naturaliter insunt.

Concedendum ergo, quod immutatio et transmutatio quodam modo naturalis est in relatione ad obiectum, quia salvatur ibi natura accidentium; quodam modo est supra naturam in comparatione ad subiectum, quia accidentia per se agunt. — Et secundum hoc currunt rationes ad partes oppositas adductae[^2d].

## English

### Question III. *Whether those accidents have their operations by nature or by miracle.*

Thirdly it is asked concerning the operations which those accidents have, whether they have them by nature or by miracle. And it is asked first about the operation of altering [the senses], which belongs to those species, whether it is natural or miraculous. And that it is natural, seems to be the case:

1. Because what belongs to something *per se* belongs to it not only when joined to matter, but also when abstracted and separated; but to move the senses belongs *per se* to sensible things[^4]: therefore it belongs *per se* to those [species] separated from matter; but the things that belong *per se* belong naturally: therefore etc.

2. Likewise, a substantial form never moves the sense when separated from accidental forms[^5]: therefore the substantial form does nothing, deprives of nothing, but that the accidents should move the sense, provided the accidents have being: therefore they move by nature.

3. Likewise, the sense is receptive of species apart from matter, yet under material conditions, here and now[^6]: if therefore there is some sensible species without matter, yet having material conditions, that one most of all is apt to move the sense: therefore since these species are of this kind, it is clear etc.

4. Likewise, this is clear by a similar [case]: if an image[^7] were left in a mirror, the opposed body being removed, it would move the sight, and yet the image in the mirror has spiritual being: therefore it seems that those species, since they are left there without matter, that they alter [the sense] naturally.

5. Likewise, if a ship were raised into the air by a miracle, although the existence of the ship in the air would be miraculous, nevertheless the existence of the sailor in the ship would be natural, and the operations consequent upon that existence would be natural: since therefore the accidents are founded in quantity, and quantity in matter[^8]; the raising of quantity from matter is miraculous; but the existence of the other accidents is natural: therefore the operation upon the senses consequent upon that existence is natural.

**On the contrary:** 1. The Philosopher in the first [book] *On the Soul*[^9]: «A straight [line], insofar as it is straight, happens to touch a bronze sphere at a point, but it never touches a separated [thing]», because a bronze sphere is not apt to be touched except by a natural [thing]: if therefore a separated straight [line] touched a bronze sphere, this would be above nature, or against [it]. Since therefore the particular sense is not apt to be altered except by a species existing in matter; if it be altered by a separated [species], that alteration is not natural.

2. Likewise, as the interior sense is related to a species without matter, so the exterior to a species in matter; and conversely, as the interior to a species in matter, so the exterior to a species without matter; but the interior never naturally apprehends a species in matter, but [only] an abstracted one[^1b]: therefore neither does the exterior [apprehend] a species separated from matter.

<!-- page 281 -->

3. Likewise, it belongs to a form to be here and now by reason of matter[^2b]; but by that nature by which it is here and now, it is apt to move the sense: therefore since this is from matter, it therefore moves naturally: therefore if a separation from matter occurs, it will no longer move naturally.

4. Likewise, that species that moves the sense has a threefold relation, namely to the subject, to the medium, and to the organ[^3b], and these relations are ordered: therefore, the first being destroyed, the others are consequently destroyed: therefore if that species has the two following relations without the first, it does not [act] naturally, but miraculously.

Secondly it is asked concerning the work of transmutation and especially of putrefaction, namely whether it is natural. And that it is, seems to be the case:

1. Because what belongs to something naturally, the nature being preserved, still belongs to it; but to those species it belongs naturally to be corrupted and to fail because of the defect of natural power, and because they are caused by contrary qualities[^4b]; and it is established that those species are preserved in the same nature: therefore etc.

2. Likewise, corruption through contrariety is natural — this is clear; but those species existing there have truly the nature of contrariety: therefore they are corruptible naturally. The minor is shown thus: where contraries exist according to spiritual being, they can be at once in the same [thing] and according to the same [respect], as in the same medium, as in the sense and in the intellect contrary colors [exist][^5b]: since therefore here contrary colors or savors cannot exist at once, but rather mutually expel each other: they therefore have the nature of true contrariety.

3. Likewise, where there are species according to spiritual being, the excellence of such a species is not corruptive[^6b]: if therefore in the sacramental species the excellence of the sensibles distresses the sense: therefore there is there a species having the nature of true contrariety. But what is thus corruptible is so naturally: therefore etc.

4. Likewise, Anselm, *On the Virginal Conception*[^7b], distinguishes a threefold course: the natural, the voluntary, and the miraculous, and says that the miraculous is administered immediately by God: since therefore God corrupts nothing immediately, that corruption or putrefaction, since it is not from God immediately: therefore it is not miraculous, therefore it is natural or voluntary; but it is not voluntary: therefore it is natural.

**On the contrary:** 1. «A form by its own nature consists in a simple and invariable essence[^8b]»: if therefore variation should befall it, this is through matter: therefore separated from matter it is invariable by nature. If therefore it be varied, this is above nature: therefore etc.

2. Likewise, three principles concur in a physical transmutation, namely the subject and two contraries[^9b]: therefore where matter is lacking, a physical change cannot be understood; but this is the case in species of this kind: therefore that putrefaction is not physical.

3. Likewise, «as acting is from form, so undergoing is from matter[^10b]»; but it does not happen [for anything] to act physically except through form: therefore neither to undergo except through matter: therefore where matter is lacking, it is manifest that there is no physical undergoing: therefore neither transmutation.

4. Likewise, there is no true contrariety without a subject; but prime matter is the subject of all corporeal contrariety[^11b]: therefore where prime matter is lacking, there is no true contrariety. But natural action is through true contrariety: therefore etc. Similarly it could be argued concerning contact, which is of form in matter, and action and passion [occur] through contact.

Thirdly it is asked concerning the work of nutrition, whether it is natural or supernatural[^12b].

> **Conclusion.** By a twofold way it is proved that the operations of alteration, of transmutation, and of nutrition are partly natural, partly miraculous.

**I respond:** It must be said, for the understanding of the foregoing, according to one fairly probable manner of speaking[^13b], from the comparison of the power to the operations

<!-- page 282 -->

issuing from the power. For operations have to be regulated according to the potencies and powers from which they issue.

On account of which it must be noted that sometimes there is given to someone a power conformable to his nature; and the issuance from that potency is natural, as when the power of seeing is restored to a blind man. — Sometimes there is given to someone a power partly conformable to nature and partly above nature; and then the issuing act is in a certain way natural, in a certain way above nature, as for a blind man to see by night. — Sometimes, however, there is given a power wholly above nature; and then the act issuing from that potency is miraculous simply and above nature, as the power of giving birth was given to the Virgin[^1c].

It must be considered therefore that in this Sacrament all these are to be found. For first, quantity naturally has it to underlie the other accidents, and the other accidents naturally to be sustained[^2c] by it. If therefore there is given to quantity the power of sustaining the other accidents, their existence in the quantity is natural, and the consequent operation has a natural potency. Hence I say that the operations of those species upon the senses are natural, because in the quantity they have a being suited to the alteration of the senses.

All the reasons therefore showing this are to be conceded.

To the opposing [arguments] the reply is manifest: because quantity is there in place of matter, and through it [the accidents] are here and now and have material being suited to the alteration of the senses, and the accidents are first in quantity; and this can be sufficiently seen by the example of the ship set forth above. — But as to what is further given to that quantity, namely the power of sustaining the transmutations of those forms[^3c]; such a power is partly conformable to nature, partly above nature. It is conformable as regards the subsistence of contrariety, because contraries belong *per se* to quantity; but it is above [nature] as regards this, that it remains the same in itself when fracture has occurred: for this is proper to substance alone[^4c], and so above the nature of quantity.

To the question therefore which is asked concerning transmutation, whether it is natural or miraculous; it must be said that by reason of the natures of those species, which have the nature of contrariety, that transmutation is natural, but by reason of what sustains [them][^5c] it is on this account above nature; and so partly natural, partly above nature.

The reasons therefore made for the opposing part in the second problem run according to these two ways. For those which prove that they are natural are taken from the nature of the contrariety of those forms; but those which prove that they are above nature proceed according to the way of what sustains [them]. — And thus the second question concerning transmutation is clear. But if there is given to those accidents the power of being converted into the substance, when they cease to be, and when the body of Christ ceases to be there; such a power is wholly above nature, and therefore such an act is wholly above nature and miraculous.

To the question which is asked concerning nutrition, it must be said that there is a threefold operation there[^6c]. The first is that those species are transmuted through the action of the acting power; and this is partly natural, partly above nature. — The second is that at the end of the transmutation they pass into the substance, so that they are converted into it, just as the substance of bread would be converted, if it were present; and this is miraculous[^7c]. — The third is that that substance is afterward converted into flesh; and this is simply natural.

But although this manner of speaking can probably be maintained, it can be said otherwise, both more safely and more briefly, that the operations of the accidents, or the operating power, has to be compared both to that from which it issues and to that upon which it operates. If we speak by comparison to that from which [it issues]; thus I say that, since by nature every operating power is either substantial or at least depends upon and is regulated by it [substance]; [it follows] that a substance being removed, if an accident should further operate, it is as much a miracle that it operates *per se* as it is a miracle that it remains *per se*; and so every operation is miraculous. — But if we speak by comparison to the object, whether it be[^8c] an organ of sense, or any other body, thus I say that the accidents have there many natural operations; hence that whiteness moves the sight and savor the taste, this is not above nature nor

<!-- page 283 -->

against [it], but according to nature. Since therefore whiteness is not there separated so as to have mathematical being, but its natural and singular being is preserved[^1d]; therefore those operations which inhered in whiteness and the other accidents still inhere naturally.

It must be conceded therefore that alteration and transmutation are in a certain way natural in relation to the object, because the nature of the accidents is there preserved; in a certain way they are above nature in comparison to the subject, because the accidents act *per se*. — And according to this the reasons brought forward for the opposing parts run [their course][^2d].
