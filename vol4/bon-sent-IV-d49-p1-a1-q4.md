### Quaestio IV. *Secundum quid beatitudo insit in anima.*

> The numbered footnotes below correspond to markers in both the Latin body above and the English translation. Quaracchi restarts its footnote numbering on every printed page, so the labels are page-qualified: notes 3–9 and 10–12 of page 1006, and notes 1–4 of page 1007. Notes 1–2 of page 1006 anchor in the preceding quaestio (q. 3) and are rendered there; the whole footer register of page 1008 anchors in the following quaestio (q. 5) and is rendered there.

Quarto quaeritur, secundum quid insit beatitudo, utrum sit in anima primo secundum *substantiam*, an secundum *potentiam*. Et quod secundum *substantiam*, videtur:

1. Quia beatitudo est vita; unde Augustinus[^p1006-3] vult, quod esse beatum est vivere in Deo et Deo; sed prius naturaliter vivit anima quam potentiae: ergo prius vivificatur a Deo.

2. Item, beatitudo in anima est una[^p1006-4], sed potentiae sunt plures; sed multiplicato subiecto primo, necesse est, multiplicari perfectionem: ergo etc.

3. Item, Deus est intimus ipsi animae[^p1006-5]: ergo cum beatitudo sit in anima per influentiam a Deo, illa influentia procedit ab intimo; sed substantia rei creatae interior est quam potentia: ergo beatitudo prius influitur in substantiam.

**Contra:** 1. Beatitudo inest animae, secundum quod « capax est Dei et particeps esse potest[^p1006-6] »; sed capax est secundum imaginem, et imago secundum mentem, et mens est oculus et potentia animae: ergo beatitudo inest primo animae secundum *potentiam*.

2. Item, beatitudo inest animae secundum modum specialem[^p1006-7] unionis Dei et animae; sed modus specialis non est nisi per cognitionem perfectam et amorem, et hic est per potentias: ergo etc.

3. Item, beatitudo est habitus secundus et perfectio secunda; sed omnis secundus habitus et perfectio secunda inest secundum aliquam potentiam recipiendi, quae differt a substantia: ergo beatitudo non inest animae per substantiam immediate.

4. Item, si immediate et primo per substantiam, ergo abstractis potentiis, poterit intelligi anima gloriosa: ergo abstracto, quod Deum non videat nec amet, erit gloriosa; quod stultum est dicere et contra Sanctos[^p1006-8].

### Conclusio

> *Beatitudo est in anima primo secundum potentiam, et quidem una in pluribus potentiis.*

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod opinio quorundam est, sicut de gratia, ita de gloria, quod sicut differt *gratia* et *virtus*[^p1006-9], ita et *beatitudo* et *dos*. Et sicut *gratia* respicit animae substantiam, et ideo est una; *virtus* vero *potentiam*, et ideo plures: sic et *gloria*, quae animae *substantiam* respicit, ideo est una; sed *dotes potentias*, ideo plures. Et exemplum naturale est in anima humana, perficiente corpus, quae in quantum *vivificans* respicit *complexionem*, quae est una in toto corpore; in quantum *motor* respicit *organa diversa*[^p1006-10]. — Sed credo, hic esse magis *appropriationem* quam *proprietatem* et veritatem. Corpus enim nunquam dispositum est ad vitam, qualemcumque habeat *complexionem*, nisi etiam habeat *organizationem*; unde nec influentia vitae nec motus est ab anima in corpus, nisi naturaliter praeexistat *dispositio organizationis*[^p1006-11]. Similiter credo in *gratia* et *gloria*, quod anima non est capax gratiae nec gloriae nisi *per mentem*. Unde credo, quod tam *gratia* quam *gloria* primo est in *potentia*. — Sed *esse aliquid in potentia*, hoc est dupliciter: aut secundum dispositionem et habilitationem eius ad *actum proprium*, quia sic potentiae distinguuntur[^p1006-12]; et quod sic est in potentia *multiplicatur* et est in potentia secundum *rationem potentiae*. Aliquid vero est in potentia secundum conformationem ipsius ad *aliquod unum* et unionem ad illud; et quod sic est in potentia non multiplicatur in potentiis, immo *esse in potentia* dicitur, secundum quod habet rationem *substantiae*; et sic[^p1007-1] *gratia* et *gloria*, quarum unaquaeque est deiformitas et conformitas ad Deum. Et quia uni et eidem conformatur et reformatur ipsa anima; ideo una est gratia et gloria in pluribus potentiis, sicut una anima in pluribus organis, et una sanitas in pluribus membris. Et hoc clarius in secundo disputatum est in distinctione vigesima sexta[^p1007-2] de gratia.

<!-- page 1007 -->

**Ad argumenta:**

*Ad 1.* Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod prius est vivificari animam quam potentias; dicendum, quod verum est de vita, quae est *actus primus*; sed non de vita, quae est *actus secundus*.

*Ad 2.* Ad aliud iam patet responsio: quia non est gloria in potentiis secundum id, in quo *distinguuntur*, sed secundum quod *uniuntur* in ratione imaginis, per quam capaces[^p1007-3] sunt unius deiformitatis.

*Ad 3.* Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod Deus est intimus animae, ergo procedit ab intimo; dicendum, quod influentia ista ab intimo procedit; sed non procedit, secundum quod *intimum conservans in esse naturae*, sed secundum quod *obiectum consummans in esse gloriae*; et ideo secundum hanc viam magis est intimus et immediatus potentiae quam essentiae. Unde non sequitur, quodsi divina essentia est intima, quod quaelibet eius influentia[^p1007-4].

### Scholion

**I.** Haec quaestio magis explicat quae dicta sunt in 1. huius articuli quaestione, simulque refellit opinionem illam Henrici Gand., quam in scholio ad 1. q. commemoravimus. Unitatem *gloriae* S. Doctor eodem modo tradit, quo unitatem *gratiae* explicat, II. Sent. d. 26. q. 5, ubi cfr. etiam scholion. — Alii auctores, qui beatitudinem ponunt in *actu secundo*, simul improbant positionem Henrici, quin de hac 4. quaestione specialiter tractent; cfr. Scot., hic q. 2. n. 25. seqq.; S. Thom., hic q. 1. a. 2. quaestiunc. 1. (praesertim ad 1.) et 2; Dionys. Carth., hic q. 2.

**II.** In 5. quaestione S. Doctor proprie agit de perfectione *status* gloriosi et de *dotibus* animae; unde vix tangit celebrem illam controversiam scholasticam, in quo scilicet actu sive operatione *formaliter* consistat actus beatificus, utrum simul in *plurium* potentiarum actibus, an in *unius* potentiae actu (quod Scoto, S. Thomae pluribusque aliis placuit); et hoc supposito, utrum in actu *intellectus* (in visione), quod strenue docet S. Thomas, an in actu *voluntatis* (in fruitione per amorem amicitiae), pro quo certat Scotus cum non paucis etiam extra suam scholam. — De tribus dotibus animae S. Bonav. non agit specialiter (quod tamen faciunt alii), sed de quatuor dotibus corporis tractat infra p. II. sect. 1. et 2.

**III.** Praenotandum est, esse extra controversiam, 1. quod *beatitudo patriae* complectitur plures perfectiones et operationes, speciatim *fruitionem* voluntatis et *visionem* immediatam intellectus. Explicite hoc docet Constitutio Benedicti XII. (*Benedictus Deus*), ubi inter alia habetur, « quod ex tali *visione et fruitione* eorum animae... sunt vere beatae ». Item, Catechismus Roman. (p. 1. c. 13. n. 7.) beatitudinem illam in eo sitam esse dicit, « ut Deum *videamus* eiusque pulcritudine *fruamur* ».

2. Item, non est dubium, beatitudinem esse in voluntate sicut *obiectum*. Hoc concedit S. Thom. (hic q. 1. a. 1. quaestiunc. 2.), ubi dicit: Beatitudinem esse in voluntate « dupliciter potest intelligi: uno modo ita, quod sit voluntatis *obiectum*; et sic beatitudo, cum sit ultimus finis, et ex fine sit ratio boni, quod est voluntatis obiectum, oportet ponere, beatitudinem in voluntate esse. Alio modo ita, quod sit aliquis *actus* voluntatis; et sic beatitudo in voluntate esse non potest ».

3. Denique patet, quod in via *generationis* prius natura est Deus obiectum immediatum *visionis* quam immediatum obiectum *fruitionis*. — Controvertitur autem, utrum hoc verum sit etiam in via *perfectionis*; immo Richard. a Med. (hic a. 1. q. 6. ad 5.) ex illa propositione contrarium arguere vult, quia prioritas in via generationis est « prioritas, qua *imperfectum* est prius perfecto... quia ut in pluribus videmus, quod actuum coordinatorum inter se *prior* ordine naturae in *via generationis* minus est perfectus quam posterior. Nec hoc dicens intendo dicere, quod illa visio sit imperfecta in *genere visionis*, sed volo dicere, quod ipsa fruitio est actus perfectior, sicut declarabitur in quaestione sequenti ».

**IV.** Richard. a Med., (locis citt.), adductis multis argumentis pro utraque parte, concludit, quod beatitudo consistat tam in actu intellectus quam in actu voluntatis; principalius tamen in *voluntate*, « quia in visione inchoatur et in fruitione consummatur » (q. 6. ad 8.). Hanc opinionem etiam Scot. (hic q. 3. n. 5.) non improbat, si intelligatur, beatitudinem *praecise* et pro *ultimo complemento* optime essendi consistere in *unica* operatione, si vero pro unione *vitali immediata ad obiectum* beatificum, in *duplici* operatione intellectus et voluntatis.

Quoad sententiam S. Bonaventurae patet iam ex 1. solut. ad oppos., eum hic non tenere sententiam S. Thomae. Cum autem hoc loco proprie tractet *generaliter* de tribus *dotibus*, recurrendum est ad alios locos, praecipue ad I. Sent. d. 1. a. 2. q. unica et a. 3. q. 2; II. Sent. d. 38. a. 1. q. 2; III. Sent. d. 27. a. 2. q. 3; d. 31. a. 3. q. 1. ad 4; Breviloq. p. VII. c. 7, ubi satis declaratur, eum convenire potius cum praedicta opinione Richardi; quam etiam Petr. a Tar. tenet (hic q. 1. a. 1. quaestiunc. 3.), docens, illam beatitudinem consistere « in actu intellectus *originaliter*, in actu vero voluntatis *completive* ».

Quoad *trium dotium* actus, de quibus in hac quaestione agitur, supponendum, quod S. Bonav. ipsam voluntatem *rationalem* dividat in concupiscibilem et irascibilem (cfr. III. Sent. d. 26. a. 2. q. 5. scholion), unde etiam in patria *irascibili* tribuit actum, scil. tentionis, quae interdum non proprie, sed *per appropriationem* vocatur *fruitio*. Hanc enim proprie et essentialiter in actu *amoris amicitiae* consistere, dicitur I. Sent. d. 1. a. 2. q. unica ad 2. Tamen de fruitionis ratione, sicut de quibusdam connexis, iterum doctores secundum principia diversa, quae ponunt de essentia beatitudinis, diversa sentiunt, ut notatum est in scholio ad ultimo cit. locum.

**V.** Hae controversiae scholarum antiquae aliquo certo et omnibus explorato principio determinari non possunt, cum ipsa argumenta ab utraque parte copiose in medium prolata plerumque innitantur principiis non concessis ab adversariis; v. g. schola S. Thomae (cfr. S. 1. q. 82. a. 3.) in subsidium suae sententiae affert, quod *intellectus* sit potentia animae omnium nobilissima, dum Scot. (hic q. 4. later.) diffuse certat pro principatu *voluntatis*, cui suffragatur Henr. Gand. (Quodl. 1. q. 14), et favet etiam S. Bonav. cum Alexandro Hal., Richardo a Med., aliisque, ut dictum est II. Sent. d. 25. p. 1. q. 6. in scholio.

<!-- page 1008 -->

Hinc Estius (hic § 3.), licet praeferat sententiam S. Thomae, adductis duodecim argumentis pro sententia Scoti, pensatis omnibus, concludit, pleraque eorum « non esse ita levia, ut *temere* Scotum aliosque quosdam in diversam adduxerint sententiam, ut proinde non immerito inter problemata theologica numeretur haec quaestio ».

**VI.** De ipsa 5. quaestione praeter locos citt.: S. Thom., hic q. 1. et etiam 3. (de delectatione); S. I. II. q. 3. a. 4. 5. 8, q. 4. a. 1. 2. — Durand., hic q. 4. 5. — Dionys. Carth., hic q. 2.

**VII.** Conclusio 6. quaestionis, quam negavit Iovinianus, refutatus a S. Hieronymo, confirmatur Decreto Eugen. IV. contra Graecos, ubi dicitur, Deum videre Beatos, « pro meritorum tamen diversitate alium alio perfectius ».

## English

### Question IV. *According to what beatitude is present in the soul.*

Fourthly it is asked, according to what beatitude is present — whether it is in the soul first according to *substance*, or according to *power*. And that it is according to *substance*, it seems:

1. Because beatitude is life; whence Augustine[^p1006-3] holds that to be blessed is to live in God and by God; but the soul naturally lives prior to the powers: therefore it is first vivified by God.

2. Likewise, beatitude in the soul is one[^p1006-4], but the powers are many; but when the first subject is multiplied, the perfection must necessarily be multiplied: therefore etc.

3. Likewise, God is most inward to the soul itself[^p1006-5]: therefore, since beatitude is in the soul through an influence from God, that influence proceeds from what is inmost; but the substance of a created thing is more interior than its power: therefore beatitude is first infused into the substance.

**On the contrary:** 1. Beatitude is present in the soul according to the fact that "it is capable of God and can be a partaker[^p1006-6]"; but it is capable according to the image, and the image is according to the mind, and the mind is the eye and a power of the soul: therefore beatitude is present in the soul first according to *power*.

2. Likewise, beatitude is present in the soul according to a special mode[^p1006-7] of the union of God and the soul; but the special mode is only through perfect knowledge and love, and this is through the powers: therefore etc.

3. Likewise, beatitude is a second habit and a second perfection; but every second habit and second perfection is present according to some power of receiving, which differs from the substance: therefore beatitude is not present in the soul through the substance immediately.

4. Likewise, if it is present immediately and first through the substance, then, with the powers abstracted, the soul could be understood as glorious: therefore, with it abstracted that it neither sees nor loves God, it will be glorious; which is foolish to say and against the Saints[^p1006-8].

### Conclusion

> *Beatitude is in the soul first according to power, and indeed one in many powers.*

**I respond:** It must be said that the opinion of certain men is that, as with grace, so with glory: that just as *grace* and *virtue* differ[^p1006-9], so also do *beatitude* and *dowry*. And just as *grace* regards the substance of the soul, and therefore is one, whereas *virtue* regards *power*, and therefore they are many: so also *glory*, which regards the *substance* of the soul, is therefore one; but the *dowries* regard the *powers*, and therefore they are many. And the natural example is in the human soul perfecting the body, which, insofar as it is *vivifying*, regards the *complexion*, which is one in the whole body; insofar as it is *mover*, it regards *diverse organs*[^p1006-10]. — But I believe that here there is rather an *appropriation* than a *property* and truth. For the body is never disposed to life, whatever complexion it may have, unless it also has *organization*; whence neither the influence of life nor motion is from the soul into the body, unless the *disposition of organization*[^p1006-11] naturally pre-exists. Likewise I believe concerning *grace* and *glory*, that the soul is not capable of grace nor of glory except *through the mind*. Whence I believe that both *grace* and *glory* are first in the *power*. — But *for something to be in a power* is twofold: either according to the disposition and fitting of that power to its *proper act*, since it is in this way that powers are distinguished[^p1006-12]; and what is in a power in this way *is multiplied* and is in the power according to the *account of the power*. But something is in a power according to its conformation to *some one thing* and its union to that; and what is in a power in this way is not multiplied in the powers, but rather is said to be *in a power* according as it has the account of *substance*; and thus[^p1007-1] are *grace* and *glory*, each of which is a deiformity and conformity to God. And because the soul itself is conformed and reformed to one and the same thing, therefore grace and glory are one in many powers, just as one soul is in many organs, and one health in many members. And this was disputed more clearly in the second book, in the twenty-sixth distinction[^p1007-2], concerning grace.

<!-- page 1007 -->

**To the arguments:**

*To 1.* To that which is objected, that the soul is vivified prior to the powers, it must be said that this is true of the life which is *first act*; but not of the life which is *second act*.

*To 2.* To the other the answer is already clear: because glory is not in the powers according to that in which they *are distinguished*, but according as they *are united* in the account of the image, through which they are capable[^p1007-3] of one deiformity.

*To 3.* To that which is objected, that God is most inward to the soul, therefore [the influence] proceeds from what is inmost, it must be said that this influence does proceed from what is inmost; but it does not proceed according as He is the *inmost thing conserving in the being of nature*, but according as He is the *object consummating in the being of glory*; and therefore along this path He is more inward and immediate to the power than to the essence. Whence it does not follow that, if the divine essence is inmost, then any influence of His whatever [is so][^p1007-4].

### Scholion

**I.** This question explains more fully what was said in the 1st question of this article, and at the same time it refutes that opinion of Henry of Ghent which we recalled in the scholion to q. 1. The unity of *glory* the holy Doctor hands down in the same way in which he explains the unity of *grace*, II Sent. d. 26. q. 5, where see also the scholion. — Other authors, who place beatitude in the *second act*, likewise disprove Henry's position, though they do not treat specially of this 4th question; cf. Scotus, here q. 2. n. 25. ff.; St. Thomas, here q. 1. a. 2. quaestiuncula 1. (especially ad 1.) and 2; Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 2.

**II.** In the 5th question the holy Doctor properly treats of the perfection of the glorious *state* and of the *dowries* of the soul; whence he scarcely touches that celebrated scholastic controversy, namely in what act or operation the beatific act *formally* consists — whether at once in the acts of *several* powers, or in the act of *one* power (which pleased Scotus, St. Thomas and many others); and, this being supposed, whether in the act of the *intellect* (in vision), which St. Thomas strenuously teaches, or in the act of the *will* (in fruition through the love of friendship), for which Scotus contends together with not a few even outside his own school. — Of the three dowries of the soul St. Bonaventure does not treat specially (which others nevertheless do), but he treats of the four dowries of the body below, p. II. sect. 1 and 2.

**III.** It must be premised that it is beyond controversy, 1. that the *beatitude of the fatherland* embraces several perfections and operations, especially the *fruition* of the will and the immediate *vision* of the intellect. Explicitly the Constitution of Benedict XII (*Benedictus Deus*) teaches this, where among other things it is held that "from such *vision and fruition* their souls... are truly blessed." Likewise the Roman Catechism (p. 1. c. 13. n. 7.) says that that beatitude is situated in this, "that we may *see* God and *enjoy* His beauty."

2. Likewise, there is no doubt that beatitude is in the will as its *object*. St. Thomas concedes this (here q. 1. a. 1. quaestiuncula 2.), where he says: That beatitude is in the will "can be understood in two ways: in one way, that it is the *object* of the will; and thus beatitude, since it is the ultimate end, and since from the end comes the account of the good, which is the object of the will, one must hold that beatitude is in the will. In another way, that it is some *act* of the will; and thus beatitude cannot be in the will."

3. Finally it is clear that in the way of *generation* God is by nature prior as the immediate object of *vision* than as the immediate object of *fruition*. — But it is disputed whether this is true also in the way of *perfection*; indeed Richard of Middleton (here a. 1. q. 6. ad 5.) wishes to argue the contrary from that proposition, because priority in the way of generation is "a priority by which the *imperfect* is prior to the perfect... because for the most part we see that, of acts coordinated among themselves, the one *prior* in the order of nature in the *way of generation* is less perfect than the later one. Nor in saying this do I intend to say that that vision is imperfect in the *genus of vision*, but I wish to say that fruition itself is the more perfect act, as will be declared in the following question."

**IV.** Richard of Middleton (in the passages cited), having adduced many arguments on both sides, concludes that beatitude consists both in the act of the intellect and in the act of the will; yet principally in the *will*, "because it is begun in vision and consummated in fruition" (q. 6. ad 8.). This opinion Scotus also (here q. 3. n. 5.) does not disprove, if it be understood that beatitude *precisely* and as the *ultimate complement* of best being consists in a *single* operation, but if [taken] for the *vital immediate union to the beatific object*, in a *twofold* operation of intellect and will.

As to the mind of St. Bonaventure, it is already clear from the 1st solution to the opposing arguments that he does not here hold St. Thomas's opinion. But since in this place he properly treats *generally* of the three *dowries*, one must have recourse to other passages, especially to I Sent. d. 1. a. 2. q. unica and a. 3. q. 2; II Sent. d. 38. a. 1. q. 2; III Sent. d. 27. a. 2. q. 3; d. 31. a. 3. q. 1. ad 4; Breviloquium p. VII. c. 7, where it is sufficiently declared that he agrees rather with the aforesaid opinion of Richard; which Peter of Tarentaise also holds (here q. 1. a. 1. quaestiuncula 3.), teaching that that beatitude consists "in the act of the intellect *originally*, but in the act of the will *completively*."

As to the acts of the *three dowries*, of which this question treats, it must be supposed that St. Bonaventure divides the *rational* will itself into the concupiscible and the irascible (cf. III Sent. d. 26. a. 2. q. 5. scholion), whence also in the fatherland he attributes an act to the *irascible*, namely of holding, which sometimes is called *fruition* not properly but *by appropriation*. For that fruition properly and essentially consists in the act of the *love of friendship* is stated in I Sent. d. 1. a. 2. q. unica ad 2. Yet concerning the account of fruition, as concerning certain connected matters, the doctors again think diversely according to the diverse principles which they lay down concerning the essence of beatitude, as was noted in the scholion to the last-cited passage.

**V.** These ancient controversies of the schools cannot be determined by any certain principle explored by all, since the very arguments copiously brought forward on both sides for the most part rest upon principles not conceded by their adversaries; for example, the school of St. Thomas (cf. *Summa* I. q. 82. a. 3.) brings forward in support of its own opinion that the *intellect* is the noblest of all the powers of the soul, while Scotus (here q. 4. later) contends at length for the primacy of the *will*, whom Henry of Ghent supports (Quodl. 1. q. 14), and St. Bonaventure also favours, together with Alexander of Hales, Richard of Middleton and others, as was said in II Sent. d. 25. p. 1. q. 6. in the scholion.

<!-- page 1008 -->

Hence Estius (here § 3.), although he prefers St. Thomas's opinion, having adduced twelve arguments for Scotus's opinion, all things weighed, concludes that most of them are "not so slight that they *rashly* led Scotus and certain others into a different opinion, so that consequently this question is not undeservedly numbered among the theological problems."

**VI.** On the 5th question itself, besides the passages cited: St. Thomas, here q. 1. and also 3. (on delight); *Summa* I-II. q. 3. a. 4. 5. 8, q. 4. a. 1. 2. — Durandus, here q. 4. 5. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 2.

**VII.** The conclusion of the 6th question, which Jovinian denied and for which he was refuted by St. Jerome, is confirmed by the Decree of Eugene IV against the Greeks, where it is said that the Blessed see God, "yet according to the diversity of merits one more perfectly than another."

On the same: Scotus, below d. 50. q. 5. 6. — St. Thomas, here q. 2. a. 4; *Summa* I-II. q. 5. a. 2. — Blessed Albert, here a. 10. — Peter of Tarentaise, here a. 3. — Richard of Middleton, here a. 2. q. 8. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 3.
