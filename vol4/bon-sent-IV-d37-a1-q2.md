## Latin
<!-- page 803 -->

### Quaestio II. *Utrum sacer ordo matrimonium impediat.*

Secundo quaeritur, utrum sacer ordo matrimonium impediat. Et quod sic, videtur:

1. Per auctoritates multas, quas adducit Magister in littera[^p803-2], et sumuntur plures de distinctione trigesima secunda.

2. Item, in *Extra, de Clericis coniugatis*[^p803-3], Alexander tertius: «Si in subdiaconatu et aliis superioribus ordinibus uxores accepisse noscuntur, eos uxores dimittere compellere procuretis».

3. Item, omnis qui non vovet continentiam, est ineptus ad sacros ordines; sed qui habent uxores, sunt huiusmodi: ergo etc. Probatio maioris: Gregorius dicit, et habetur in littera[^p803-4]: «Nullum subdiaconum facere praesumant episcopi, nisi qui se promisit caste victurum»: ergo si qui promittit in manu episcopi continentiam, ipse vovet et tenetur, patet etc.

4. Item, in sacris ordinibus ministrant circa altare; sed sanctius est altare novae legis et sacrificium quam legis figurae; sed sacerdos, cum ingrediebatur sancta sanctorum, tenebatur a coniuge abstinere, sicut dicitur in Exodo[^p803-5]: ergo multo fortius ministri altaris nunc: ergo qui iugiter ministrant nunquam debent mulieribus adhaerere.

**Contra:**
1. Virtus nullam habet oppositionem cum virtute[^p803-6], ergo nec Sacramentum cum Sacramento; sed quod non habet oppositionem cum alio non praestat ei impedimentum: ergo ordinis Sacramentum nullo modo impedit matrimonium.

2. Item, oppositio est relatio aequiparantiae[^p803-7]: ergo si unum Sacramentum repugnat et impedit susceptionem alterius, pari ratione et e converso; sed matrimonium non impedit susceptionem ordinis, quia multi coniugati promoti sunt in episcopos: ergo nec ordo sacer impedit matrimonium.

3. Item, idem est ordo apud nos et apud Ecclesiam orientalem; sed apud illos ordo non impedit matrimonium, nec e converso[^p803-8]: ergo nec apud

<!-- page 804 -->
nos. *Si tu dicas*, quod apud nos est superadiectum votum, ratione cuius impedit; tunc videtur, quod impedimentum ordinis non differat ab impedimento voti.

> **Conclusio.** Sacer ordo impedit matrimonium contrahendum et dirimit contractum.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod absque dubio, sicut sacri canones dicunt, sacer ordo impedit matrimonium contrahendum et dirimit iam contractum.

Verumtamen quantum ad modum dicendi sive intelligendi inter doctores diversos diversitas opinionum resultat.

Quidam enim dicunt, quod ordo sacer impedit matrimonium propter *votum annexum*, ad quod obligatur qui suscipit ordinem sacrum. — Sed quoniam in susceptione ordinis non videtur voti emissio, aut si est emissio quantum ad *factum*, quamvis non sit quantum ad *verbum*; non videtur tunc differre ab impedimento voti; hoc falsum est, quia apud Graecos non est votum continentiae, quia illi sacerdotes licite utuntur uxoribus, et tamen nullus potuit nec potest in sacris ordinibus constitutus contrahere, sicut ex Synodo sexta[^p804-1] habetur, quae celebrata fuit apud eos, in qua se huic statuto subiecerunt praeter votum continentiae.

Ideo aliorum est opinio, quod ordo praestat impedimentum ratione *statuti ecclesiastici*, quod ligat clericos suscipientes ordines ad nunquam contrahendum.

Sed quoniam auctoritates canonum inveniuntur, quae utrumque sonare videntur, et quod in susceptione ordinis est solemnizatio voti; ideo dicendum et melius, quod etsi apud Graecos impediat, hoc non est nisi ratione statuti; et sic impedivit, ut dicunt quidam, a tempore Apostolorum[^p804-2]; tamen apud Latinos impedit et ratione statuti et ratione voti. Sicut enim ordo monachorum et habitus continentiam habet annexam per illum qui habitum instituit et regulam edidit monachalem; et ideo qui profitetur et suscipit habitum dicitur emittere votum consequenter: sic ordo apud Latinos ex ecclesiastica institutione habet inseparabiliter continentiam annexam; et ideo qui illum suscipit spontanee se obligat, ita quod illa obligatio procedit ex statuto pariter et ex voto. — Et sic patet, quod impedit et qualiter impedit.

**Ad argumenta:**
*Ad 1.* Ad illud ergo quod obiicitur, quod virtus cum virtute non habet oppositionem; dicendum, quod verum est quantum ad *habitum*; sed tamen *status* aliquando sunt incompossibiles, ut coniugalis et virginalis[^p804-3]. Et quoniam Sacramenta diversa *status* diversos respiciunt, ut ordo statum continentiae virginalis vel vidualis, matrimonium statum continentiae coniugalis; hinc est, quod impedit, sicut status saecularis et religiosus suo modo opponuntur.

*Ad 2.* Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod matrimonium non impedit ordinem; dicendum, quod verum est, si uxor fuit virgo et vult continere; sed tamen non est simile: quia *processus* in virtutibus est a statu imperfectiori ad perfectiorem, sed non debet esse *reditus*; et quoniam matrimonium collocat in statu imperfectionis, ordo vero in statu perfectionis: ideo etc.; sicut de saeculo ad religionem licet ascendere, non descendere.

*Ad 3.* Ad illud quod obiicitur ultimo, iam patet responsio: quia statutum Ecclesiae abundantius arctat Latinos quam Graecos, licet illos aliquo modo arctet; et hoc, quia Latini voluerunt se subiicere et plicare, Graeci vero in sua ruditate remanere, et ideo usque hodie in suis faecibus remanserunt[^p804-4]. Patet etiam, quod non solum ratione voti impedit, sed etiam ratione statuti ecclesiastici[^p804-5].

## English

### Question II. *Whether sacred order impedes marriage.*

Secondly it is asked whether sacred order impedes marriage. And that it does, it seems:

1. By the many authorities which the Master adduces in the text[^p803-2], and more are taken from the thirty-second distinction.

2. Likewise, in the *Decretals, On Married Clerics*[^p803-3], Alexander III: «If in the subdiaconate and the other higher orders they are known to have taken wives, see to it that you compel them to dismiss their wives».

3. Likewise, everyone who does not vow continence is unfit for the sacred orders; but those who have wives are of this kind: therefore etc. Proof of the major: Gregory says, and it is found in the text[^p803-4]: «Let bishops presume to make no one a subdeacon, except one who has promised to live chastely»: therefore if someone promises continence into the bishop's hand, he vows and is bound, it is evident, etc.

4. Likewise, in the sacred orders they minister at the altar; but the altar and sacrifice of the new law are holier than [those of] the figure of the law; but the priest, when he entered the holy of holies, was bound to abstain from his spouse, as is said in Exodus[^p803-5]: therefore much more the ministers of the altar now: therefore those who minister continually ought never to cleave to women.

**On the contrary:**
1. Virtue has no opposition to virtue[^p803-6], therefore neither has Sacrament to Sacrament; but what has no opposition to another does not furnish it an impediment: therefore the Sacrament of order in no way impedes marriage.

2. Likewise, opposition is a relation of equiparance[^p803-7]: therefore if one Sacrament conflicts with and impedes the reception of the other, by equal reasoning also conversely; but marriage does not impede the reception of order, since many married men have been promoted to bishops: therefore neither does sacred order impede marriage.

3. Likewise, order is the same among us and in the Eastern Church; but among them order does not impede marriage, nor conversely[^p803-8]: therefore neither among

nos. *If you say* that among us a vow is superadded, by reason of which it impedes; then it seems that the impediment of order does not differ from the impediment of the vow.

> **Conclusion.** Sacred order impedes the marriage to be contracted and dissolves the contracted one.

**I respond:** It must be said that without doubt, as the sacred canons say, sacred order impedes the marriage to be contracted and dissolves the one already contracted.

Nevertheless, as to the manner of speaking or of understanding, a diversity of opinions arises among the various doctors.

For some say that sacred order impedes marriage on account of the *annexed vow*, to which he who receives sacred order is bound. — But since in the reception of order there does not seem to be an emission of a vow, or if there is an emission as to the *deed*, although not as to the *word*; it does not then seem to differ from the impediment of the vow; this is false, because among the Greeks there is no vow of continence, since those priests lawfully make use of wives, and yet no one established in the sacred orders could nor can contract [marriage], as is had from the sixth Synod[^p804-1], which was celebrated among them, in which they subjected themselves to this statute apart from a vow of continence.

Therefore it is the opinion of others that order furnishes the impediment by reason of the *ecclesiastical statute*, which binds clerics receiving orders never to contract.

But since authorities of the canons are found which seem to sound both ways, and [since] in the reception of order there is a solemnization of a vow; therefore it must be said, and better, that although among the Greeks it impedes, this is only by reason of the statute; and thus it has impeded, as some say, from the time of the Apostles[^p804-2]; yet among the Latins it impedes both by reason of the statute and by reason of the vow. For just as the order of monks and the habit has continence annexed through him who instituted the habit and issued the monastic rule; and therefore he who professes and receives the habit is said to emit a vow consequently: so order among the Latins, by ecclesiastical institution, has continence inseparably annexed; and therefore he who receives it binds himself spontaneously, so that that obligation proceeds from the statute equally and from the vow. — And thus it is evident that it impedes, and how it impedes.

**To the arguments:**
*To 1.* To that then which is objected, that virtue has no opposition to virtue; it must be said that this is true as to the *habit*; but nevertheless *states* are sometimes incompossible, as the conjugal and the virginal[^p804-3]. And since diverse Sacraments regard diverse *states*—as order [regards] the state of virginal or widowed continence, marriage the state of conjugal continence—hence it is that it impedes, just as the secular and the religious state are in their own way opposed.

*To 2.* To that which is objected, that marriage does not impede order; it must be said that this is true, if the wife was a virgin and wishes to be continent; but nevertheless it is not similar: because *progress* in the virtues is from a more imperfect state to a more perfect, but there ought not to be a *return*; and since marriage places [one] in a state of imperfection, but order in a state of perfection: therefore etc.; just as it is permitted to ascend from the world to religion, not to descend.

*To 3.* To that which is objected last, the response is already evident: because the statute of the Church binds the Latins more abundantly than the Greeks, although it binds the latter in some way; and this, because the Latins willed to subject and bend themselves, but the Greeks to remain in their crudeness, and therefore to this day they have remained in their dregs[^p804-4]. It is also evident that it impedes not only by reason of the vow, but also by reason of the ecclesiastical statute[^p804-5].
