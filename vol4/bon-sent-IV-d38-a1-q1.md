# d.38 a1-q1
## *Quid sit votum secundum essentiam*

---

## Latin
<!-- page 814 -->

### Articulus I. *De voto in se.*

Circa primum quaeruntur tria. Primo quaeritur de voto, quid sit secundum essentiam. Secundo quaeritur, de quo sit. Tertio, in quo sit.

### Quaestio I. *Quid sit votum secundum essentiam.*

Quantum ad primum sic proceditur et ostenditur, quod votum secundum substantiam sit *promissio*:

1. Per definitionem Hugonis[^p814-3]: « Votum est testificatio quaedam promissionis spontaneae, quae Deo, et de his quae Deo pertinent, proprie fieri debet ».

2. Item, voto se obligat homo ad id, ad quod prius non obligabatur; sed homo ad novum se non obligat nisi propter beneficium, vel propter peccatum, vel propter promissum; sed in voto non obligatur homo beneficio, vel peccato: ergo obligatur promissione: ergo promissio est de substantia voti.

Item, quod sit *obligandi intentio*[^p814-4], videtur:

3. Quia, sicut matrimonium est ab opere nostro, sic et votum; sed nullus contrahit matrimonium, quantumcumque promittat exterius, nisi intendat se obligare sive consentiat interius: ergo etc.

4. Item, votum est obligatio spontanea sive
<!-- page 815 -->
voluntaria; sed nullus obligatione voluntaria obligatur, nisi velit obligari; et nullus vult obligari verbo, nisi qui intendit se obligare: ergo etc.

**Contra:** Ostenditur quod *promissio* non sit de genere voti:

1. Per aliam definitionem voti[^p815-1]: « Votum est melioris boni conceptio cum deliberatione firmata » — haec definitio dicit voti substantiam — sed hoc totum potest esse sine promissione: ergo etc.

2. Item, Gregorius super Ezechielem[^p815-2]: « Qui meliora agere deliberant et deliberata non faciunt, dum se elevant, cadunt »: ergo deliberatio melioris obligat ad faciendum, alioquin non esset casus; sed non est ibi alia obligatio quam voti: ergo sola deliberatio sine promissione est votum: non ergo promissio est de essentia voti.

Item, quod nec sit *obligandi intentio*, videtur:

3. Quia aliquis promotus ad sacros ordines, intendens ordines suscipere et nullo modo obligari ad castitatem, nihilominus obligatur, ac si intenderet: ergo videtur, quod de substantia[^p815-3] voti non sit obligandi intentio.

4. Item, si aliquis iurat alicui, non intendens se obligare ad id quod verba significant, iudicatur nihilominus ita obligatus, ac si intenderet: ergo pari ratione videtur in voto, quod fit sine intentione.

Item, cum ista duo similiter faciant ad esse iuramenti, scilicet promissio et obligandi intentio, quaeritur, quae sit differentia.

### Conclusio

> **Conclusio.** *De essentia voti sunt promissio et obligandi intentio; et in voto simplici requiritur verbum interius, in solemni exterius.*

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod ista duo sunt de essentia voti, scilicet *promissio* et *obligandi intentio*. Cum votum sit *obligatio nova et voluntaria*, quia *de novo* est superaddita, *promissio* est necessaria; quia vero *voluntaria*, oportet, quod *velit obligari*, et ita[^p815-4], quod se obligare intendat. Sed contingit promittere alicui duplici verbo, scilicet *interiori et exteriori*. Promissio quantum ad verbum interius cogitationis et deliberationis est de necessitate voti *simplicis*, quod obligat quantum ad Deum, qui *intuetur cor*[^p815-5]. Sed promissio quantum ad verbum vel signum *exterius* est de necessitate voti *solemnis*, quod obligat quantum ad faciem Ecclesiae, quae iudicat secundum ea quae patent. Secundum hoc duplex datur definitio de voto; illa magistralis et communis est de voto simplici: « Conceptio melioris propositi[^p815-6] » etc.

1. 2. Ad illud quod obiicitur per definitionem voti et auctoritatem Gregorii, in sola deliberatione votum consistere; dicendum, quod non intelligunt[^p815-7] de qualibet deliberatione, sed de illa quae firmat propositum; et haec est quae consensui addit promissionem; aliter non firmat, unde licitum est dissentire.

3. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod obligandi intentio non est necessaria, sicut patet in ordine; dicendum, quod intentio respectu alicuius potest esse dupliciter: aut principaliter, quia de illo cogitat et illud placet, aut quia intendit aliquid antecedens[^p815-8] ad illud, vel simpliciter illi annexum; sicut si aliquis intendit vovere regulam monachorum, per consequens et ad continentiam obligari intendit et ad obedientiam; alioquin stulte appetit et vult. Similiter, quia ordini sacro annexum est votum, si intendit ordinem sacrum suscipere, per consequens et ad continentiam obligari principaliter, vel ex consequenti; quod si nullo modo intendat, non est votum; sic nec matrimonium.

4. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod qui iurat in dolo tenetur; dicendum, quod duplex est forum, scilicet *iudiciale* et *poenitentiale*. In foro *iudiciali* iudicatur secundum exteriora, in foro *poenitentiali* secundum conscientiam. In foro *iudiciali*, quia « fraus nulli debet patrocinari[^p815-9] », accipiuntur verba secundum intellectum communem et sanum; et quia fraus non debet ei patrocinari, iudicatur teneri, sicut si secundum intentionem rectam verba dixisset. In foro *poenitentiali*, ubi est iudicium secundum veritatem, iudicatur, istum peccasse in dolo, sed nequaquam ex illa promissione esse obligatum[^p815-10]; unde
<!-- page 816 -->
sicut matrimonium contractum per verba exteriora sine consensu omni est matrimonium solum secundum interpretationem fori *iudicialis*, non secundum *veritatem*; sic de voto dicendum.

Ad illud quod ultimo quaeritur de differentia voti ad iuramentum obligatorium, dicendum, quod triplex est differentia, scilicet in *obiecto*, *medio* et *fine*. In *obiecto*, quia votum fit Deo, sed iuramentum homini; in *medio*, quia iuratur per Deum, sed vovetur per promissionem; in *fine*, quia iuramentum est ad astruendam veritatem, votum autem ad augendam honestatem et bonitatem.

## English

### Article I. *On the vow in itself.*

Concerning the first, three [questions] are asked. First it is asked about a vow, what it is according to its essence. Second it is asked, of what it is. Third, in whom it is.

### Question I. *What a vow is according to its essence.*

As to the first, one proceeds thus, and it is shown that a vow according to its substance is a *promise*:

1. By the definition of Hugh[^p814-3]: "A vow is a certain attestation of a spontaneous promise, which ought properly to be made to God and concerning the things that pertain to God."

2. Likewise, by a vow a man binds himself to that to which he was not previously bound; but a man does not bind himself to something new except on account of a benefit, or on account of a sin, or on account of a promise; but in a vow a man is not bound by a benefit or by a sin: therefore he is bound by a promise: therefore a promise is of the substance of a vow.

Likewise, that there is an *intention to bind*[^p814-4], seems [to be shown]:

3. Because, just as matrimony is from our own act, so also is a vow; but no one contracts matrimony, however much he may promise outwardly, unless he intends to bind himself or consents inwardly: therefore etc.

4. Likewise, a vow is a spontaneous or
<!-- page 815 -->
voluntary obligation; but no one is bound by a voluntary obligation unless he wills to be bound; and no one wills to be bound by a word unless he intends to bind himself: therefore etc.

**On the contrary:** It is shown that a *promise* is not of the genus of a vow:

1. By another definition of a vow[^p815-1]: "A vow is the conception of a better good confirmed by deliberation" — this definition states the substance of a vow — but this whole can exist without a promise: therefore etc.

2. Likewise, Gregory on Ezekiel[^p815-2]: "Those who deliberate to do better things and do not do the things deliberated, while they lift themselves up, fall": therefore the deliberation of a better [good] binds one to the doing [of it], otherwise there would be no fall; but there is there no other obligation than that of a vow: therefore deliberation alone without a promise is a vow: therefore a promise is not of the essence of a vow.

Likewise, that neither is there an *intention to bind*, seems [to be shown]:

3. Because someone promoted to sacred orders, intending to receive orders and in no way to be bound to chastity, is nonetheless bound, as if he had so intended: therefore it seems that the intention to bind is not of the substance[^p815-3] of a vow.

4. Likewise, if someone swears to another, not intending to bind himself to that which the words signify, he is nonetheless judged to be bound thereby, as if he had so intended: therefore by parity of reasoning it seems [the same] in a vow, that it comes to be without intention.

Likewise, since these two, namely a promise and the intention to bind, similarly make for the being of an oath, it is asked what the difference is.

### Conclusio

> **Conclusion.** *Of the essence of a vow are a promise and the intention to bind; and in a simple vow an inward word is required, in a solemn one an outward word.*

**I respond:** It must be said that these two are of the essence of a vow, namely a *promise* and an *intention to bind*. Since a vow is a *new and voluntary obligation*, because it is *newly* superadded, a *promise* is necessary; but because it is *voluntary*, it is required that he *will to be bound*, and thus[^p815-4] that he intend to bind himself. But it happens that one promises to another by a twofold word, namely *inward and outward*. A promise as regards the inward word of thought and deliberation is of the necessity of a *simple* vow, which binds with respect to God, who *beholds the heart*[^p815-5]. But a promise as regards the outward word or sign, *outwardly*, is of the necessity of a *solemn* vow, which binds with respect to the face of the Church, which judges according to the things that are manifest. According to this a twofold definition of a vow is given; the magisterial and common one is of the simple vow: "The conception of a better resolve[^p815-6]," etc.

1 and 2. To that which is objected by the definition of a vow and the authority of Gregory, that a vow consists in deliberation alone; it must be said that they do not understand[^p815-7] [this] of any deliberation whatever, but of that which confirms the resolve; and this is what adds a promise to consent; otherwise it does not confirm, whence it is permitted to dissent.

3. To that which is objected, that the intention to bind is not necessary, as is clear in the case of [holy] orders; it must be said that intention with respect to something can be twofold: either principally, because one thinks of it and it pleases him, or because he intends something antecedent[^p815-8] to it, or simply annexed to it; just as, if someone intends to vow the rule of monks, consequently he intends also to be bound to continence and to obedience; otherwise he foolishly desires and wills. Likewise, because a vow is annexed to a sacred order, if he intends to receive a sacred order, consequently [he intends] also to be bound to continence, either principally or by consequence; but if he in no way intends it, it is not a vow; and so neither is it matrimony.

4. To that which is objected, that he who swears in deceit is held [bound]; it must be said that there is a twofold forum, namely the *judicial* and the *penitential*. In the *judicial* forum one is judged according to outward things, in the *penitential* according to conscience. In the *judicial* forum, because "deceit ought to profit no one[^p815-9]," the words are taken according to the common and sound understanding; and because deceit ought not to profit him, he is judged to be bound, just as if he had spoken the words according to a right intention. In the *penitential* forum, where there is judgment according to truth, he is judged to have sinned by deceit, but by no means to be bound by that promise[^p815-10]; whence,
<!-- page 816 -->
just as a matrimony contracted by outward words without any consent is a matrimony only according to the interpretation of the *judicial* forum, not according to *truth*; so must it be said of a vow.

To that which is finally asked concerning the difference of a vow from a binding oath, it must be said that there is a threefold difference, namely in *object*, in *medium*, and in *end*. In *object*, because a vow is made to God, but an oath to a man; in *medium*, because one swears by God, but one vows by a promise; in *end*, because an oath is for establishing truth, but a vow for increasing uprightness and goodness.
