# IV Sent., d. 10, p. 2, a. 1, q. 1
## *Utrum aequalis vel maioris virtutis sit panem in corpus Christi convertere quam creare*

---

## Latin
<!-- page 228 -->

### Articulus I. *De eucharistia respectu virtutis transsubstantiantis.*
### Quaestio I. *Utrum aequalis, vel maioris virtutis sit panem in corpus Christi convertere, quam est aliquid de novo creare.*

Quantum ergo ad primum quaeritur, utrum aequalis virtutis, vel maioris sit panem in corpus Christi convertere, quam est aliquid de novo creare.

Et quod non sit aequalis virtutis, videtur:

1. Per verbum Ambrosii, quoniam Ambrosius arguit sic: « Si sermo Christi potuit facere quod non erat, non potest ea quae sunt in id mutare quod non erant »[^1]? quasi dicat: potest; sed ista ratio nihil valeret, nisi illud esset maioris potentiae: ergo potentia creandi est maioris potentiae quam transsubstantiandi.

2. Item, *ratione* videtur, quia ubi est maior distantia, ibi maior virtus reducens; sed inter ens et non-ens est maior distantia, quam inter ens et ens: ergo maior est virtus in creatione, in qua de non-ente fit ens[^2].

3. Item, maior est virtus, quae agit paucioribus mediis et auxiliis; sed virtus, quae producit ex nihilo, sibi sola sufficit, quae vero ex aliquo, aliam rationem[^3] praesupponit: ergo etc.

**Contra:**
1. « Maius est de impio facere pium quam creare caelum et terram »[^4]; sed transsubstantiatio panis in corpus Christi multo maior est quam impium iustificare: ergo illud maioris virtutis est quam creare.

2. Item, illud est impossibilius, quod mens bene disposita difficilius iudicat; sed omnis mens facilius credit, aliquid de novo fieri, quam aliquid in aliud transmutari sine illius augmento et aliqua mutatione[^5]: ergo illud difficilius.

3. Item, ubicumque est finita distantia inter extrema, aliquid manet commune; sed in transsubstantiatione nihil commune manet[^6]: ergo inter extrema est distantia infinita; et si hoc, transsubstantiatio talis est a virtute infinita, et sic non est maioris potentiae illud quam istud.

4. Item, maioris potentiae est facere, quod unum sit in pluribus locis, quam quod unum sit; sed haec virtus, quae facit, corpus Christi esse in altari, facit, ipsum in pluribus locis esse[^7]: ergo istud est maioris virtutis.

> **Conclusio.** Maius est quoad effectum creare quam transsubstantiare; sed quatenus corpus Christi superexcellit omnem creaturam, prout est cum anima et Divinitate, effectus transsubstantiationis excellit omnem rei alterius creationem.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod si loquamur de virtute creante et transsubstantiante, quae maior sit quantum ad se, non est differentia, quia una est, nec intensa nec remissa[^8]. — Si autem quantum ad effectum, quis sit indicativus maioris potentiae, sive magis declarativus potentiae divinae; dicendum, quod in qualibet harum operationum est considerare *tria consideranda*: principium initiale, distantiam mediam et terminum finalem. Quantum ad *principium initiale* maioris virtutis est attestativum *creare* quam *transsubstantiare*, quia ibi ex nihilo, hic operatur in aliquid[^9]. — Quantum vero ad *distantiam mediam*, est aequalis virtutis, quia utrobique nihil commune reperitur, nec manet secundum *genus subiectum*, licet secundum *genus praedicabile* in altero[^10].

<!-- page 229 -->

— Quantum vero ad *terminum finalem*, quia ibi in creatione fit, ut quod *non erat* sit, in transsubstantiatione fit, ut quod erat alicubi sine sui mutatione sit alibi, illud quidem est maius, sed hoc rarius[^11]; et illud maius *est*, sed istud maius *videtur*. Quantum ergo ad terminum finalem maius *est* creare, sed maius *videtur* transsubstantiare.

Rationes igitur primae verae sunt, quia maior effectus est creare et quantum ad terminum finalem et quantum ad principium initiale. In hoc tamen, quod corpus Christi superexcellit omnem creaturam, prout est cum anima et Divinitate; et transsubstantiatio facit, ut illud corpus sit in altari, non de alio: ideo excellit omnem *alterius* rei creationem, non tamen *sui* formationem, sicut videbitur[^12].

**Ad argumenta:**

1. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod maius est de impio facere pium etc.; dicendum, quod illud dicitur maius, salvo libero arbitrio, quia hic ex parte liberi arbitrii est resistentia, ibi[^13] nulla; non sic in proposito.

2. Ad illud quod obiicitur de iudicio mentis dispositae, dicendum, quod faciliora iudicat usitatiora et quae magis apparent, quamvis maiora sint; unde non valet.

3. Ad illud quod obiicitur de distantia, dicendum, quod hoc concedendum est quantum ad hoc, quod nihil commune remanet, sed non quantum ad distantiam in ratione *generis praedicabilis*[^14].

4. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod maius est facere, quod in pluribus locis etc.; dicendum, quod cum aliquid non-ens fit, aut est in potentia, ut sit, aut nullo modo. Si aliquo modo est in potentia, ut sit; tunc bene concedendum est, quod maius est facere, quod in pluribus locis sit, quam quod sit, quia est in potentia ad esse, non ad plura loca simul. Sed quando nullo modo est in potentia, ut sit, et congruitatem habet ad hoc, ut in pluribus locis sit, primum est maius; et sic in proposito, quia illud quod creatur, nullam habet potentiam nec ordinationem ad hoc, ut sit. Sed corpus Christi iam conditum et perfectum idoneitatem habet ad hoc, ut sit in pluribus locis sub Sacramento.

## English

### Article I. *On the Eucharist with respect to the power of transubstantiating.*
### Question I. *Whether it is of equal or greater power to convert bread into the body of Christ than to create something anew.*

As regards the first point, then, it is asked whether it is of equal or greater power to convert bread into the body of Christ than to create something anew.

And that it is not of equal power, it seems:

1. By the word of Ambrose, for Ambrose argues thus: "If the word of Christ could make what was not, can it not change the things that are into what they were not?"[^1] — as if to say: it can; but this argument would have no force unless that were of greater power: therefore the power of creating is of greater power than that of transubstantiating.

2. Likewise, *by reason* it seems so, because where there is greater distance, there is a greater reducing power; but between being and non-being there is a greater distance than between being and being: therefore the power in creation is greater, in which from non-being being comes to be[^2].

3. Likewise, that power is greater which acts with fewer means and aids; but the power that produces from nothing suffices by itself alone, whereas the power that produces from something presupposes another ground[^3]: therefore, etc.

**On the contrary:**
1. "It is greater to make a godly man out of an ungodly one than to create heaven and earth"[^4]; but the transubstantiation of bread into the body of Christ is much greater than to justify the ungodly: therefore that is of greater power than to create.

2. Likewise, that is more impossible which a well-disposed mind judges more difficult; but every mind more readily believes that something comes to be anew than that something is changed into another without any increase of it and without any [observable] change[^5]: therefore that is more difficult.

3. Likewise, wherever there is a finite distance between the extremes, something remains common; but in transubstantiation nothing common remains[^6]: therefore between the extremes the distance is infinite; and if so, such a transubstantiation is from an infinite power, and thus the one is not of greater power than the other.

4. Likewise, it is of greater power to make that one thing be in many places than that it be one; but this power, which makes the body of Christ be on the altar, makes it be in many places[^7]: therefore that is of greater power.

> **Conclusion.** It is greater, as regards the effect, to create than to transubstantiate; but inasmuch as the body of Christ surpasses every creature, as it is with soul and Divinity, the effect of transubstantiation surpasses the creation of any other thing.

**Respondeo:** It must be said that, if we speak of the creating and transubstantiating power, as to which is greater in itself, there is no difference, because it is one, neither intensified nor diminished[^8]. — But if [we speak] as regards the effect, [namely] which is indicative of greater power, or rather more declarative of the divine power; it must be said that in each of these operations there are *three things to consider*: the initial principle, the intermediate distance, and the final term. As regards the *initial principle*, it is attestative of greater power *to create* than *to transubstantiate*, because there [it acts] from nothing, here it acts upon something[^9]. — But as regards the *intermediate distance*, it is of equal power, because in both cases nothing common is found, nor does anything remain according to the *subject genus*, although [it does] according to the *predicable genus* in the one [case][^10].

— But as regards the *final term*, since there in creation it comes about that what *was not* is, [while] in transubstantiation it comes about that what was somewhere, without its own change, is elsewhere, the former is indeed greater, but the latter rarer[^11]; and the former *is* greater, but the latter *seems* greater. As regards the final term, therefore, to create *is* greater, but to transubstantiate *seems* greater.

The first reasons, then, are true, because the greater effect is to create, both as regards the final term and as regards the initial principle. In this, however, that the body of Christ surpasses every creature, as it is with soul and Divinity; and transubstantiation brings it about that that body be on the altar, not from another: therefore it surpasses the creation of *any other* thing, yet not *its own* formation, as will be seen[^12].

**To the arguments:**

1. To that which is objected, that it is greater to make a godly man out of an ungodly one, etc.; it must be said that it is called greater, with free will preserved, because here on the part of free will there is resistance, there[^13] none; not so in the case at hand.

2. To that which is objected concerning the judgment of a disposed mind, it must be said that it judges the more customary and the more apparent things to be easier, although they are greater; hence it is not valid.

3. To that which is objected concerning distance, it must be said that this is to be granted as regards this, that nothing common remains, but not as regards a distance in respect of the *predicable genus*[^14].

4. To that which is objected, that it is greater to make that [something] be in many places, etc.; it must be said that when a non-being comes to be, either it is in potency, that it may be, or in no way. If it is in some way in potency, that it may be; then it is indeed to be granted that it is greater to make it be in many places than that it be, because it is in potency to being, not to many places at once. But when it is in no way in potency, that it may be, and has a fittingness for this, that it be in many places, the first is greater; and so in the case at hand, because that which is created has no potency nor ordination to this, that it may be. But the body of Christ, already constituted and perfected, has a suitability for this, that it be in many places under the Sacrament.

### Scholion
**I.** Concerning the 1st and 2nd questions, the other commentators on the Lombard treat briefly and concordantly (in the following distinction) in a single question; but more fully Alexander of Hales, *Summa* p. IV. q. 10. m. 4. a. 2. § 6. Cf. St. Thomas, d. 11. q. 1. a. 3. subquestion 3; *Summa* III. q. 75. a. 8. ad 3. — Bl. Albert, d. 11. a. 4. — Peter of Tarentaise, d. 11. q. 1. a. 5. — Richard of Mediavilla, d. 11. a. 1. q. 6. — Dionysius the Carthusian, d. 11. q. 2. about the middle.

**II.** In the 3rd question the same controversies and the same principles recur, which were treated above at d. 1. p. 1. a. 1. q. 3. 4. (cf. the scholion there) and d. 5. a. 3. q. 1. Cf. also below d. 13. a. 1. q. 3. Concerning this question none treat it explicitly here except Alexander of Hales, loc. cit. § 6, and Bl. Albert, here a. 7. 8.

**III.** Concerning the solution of the 4th question, the middle opinion, which along with St. Bonaventure Alexander of Hales and Peter of Tarentaise held as more probable, is now obsolete. For commonly the opinion of St. Thomas is received, that whatever maximum quantity of matter, provided it be morally present to the one consecrating, can be consecrated validly, though not licitly. For "the quantity of the matter of this Sacrament is determined by comparison to the use of the faithful: but not to the use of the faithful who are now present... (but) absolutely. Now the number of the faithful is indeterminate; whence it cannot be said that the quantity of the matter of this Sacrament is determined" (St. Thomas, *Summa* III. q. 74. a. 2; cf. IV Sent. d. 11. q. 2. a. 1. subquestion 3.). Concerning this question, besides St. Thomas: Alexander of Hales, loc. cit. § 6, *Consequenter quaeritur.* — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 4. a. 2. — Richard of Mediavilla, here a. 7. q. 1. — Durandus, IV Sent. d. 11. q. 6. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 6.
