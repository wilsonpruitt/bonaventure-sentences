# St. Bonaventure, Commentary on the Sentences of Peter Lombard
## Book I, Distinction 1

*Translated from the Quaracchi critical edition (1882)*
*Doctoris Seraphici S. Bonaventurae Opera Omnia, Tomus I*

---

## Article I: On Use (*Uti*)

### Question I: Whether to use is an act of the will, or of reason, or of every power

Concerning the first — what *uti* is in its essence, whether it is an act of the will, or of reason — the procedure is as follows. That it is an act of the will is shown thus:

1. From the act of using, each person is called good; but no one is called good except from an act of the will: therefore the act of using pertains to the will.

2. Likewise, Augustine in the tenth book of *On the Trinity*: "To use is to take something up into the faculty of the will": therefore *uti* is an act of the will.

3. Likewise, this seems to be shown through the division of *uti* against other things, because in the tenth book of *On the Trinity* Augustine divides these three: *ingenium*, *doctrinam*, and *usum*, and says that natural talent regards what a person can do, learning what he knows, use what he wills: therefore use is an act of the will.

4. Likewise, "opposites are apt to occur in the same subject"; but to use and to misuse are opposites; therefore, since to misuse belongs to the will alone — because to sin belongs to the will alone — to use will likewise be an act of the will alone.

On the contrary: 1. Use, or habitual practice, is a way to acquire a habit; but every power is apt to acquire a habit: therefore to use seems to belong to every power.

2. Likewise, every power is ordered to its end through its own operation; and that by which something is called *useful* — namely, that which is ordinable to an end — is said to be used; but to be ordered to an end through its own proper operation belongs to every power: therefore *uti* likewise.

3. Likewise, as Augustine says and as is found in the text, *uti* is to refer what has come into use, etc. But to refer belongs to the power whose role it is to compare; and to compare belongs to reason alone: therefore *uti* as well, and this from its definition.

4. Likewise, art uses its own instruments; but art is in the rational or cognitive power, since it is a science: therefore, since habit and act belong to the same power, if art is in the rational power, use will likewise be in the same.

CONCLUSION.

*To use can be understood in five ways: if it is taken most commonly and commonly, it is the act of every power; if it is taken properly, more properly, and most properly, it is the act of the will.*

I respond: It must be said that *uti* is taken in five ways, and accordingly this is diversified both according to the thing and according to the definition.

For use, or *uti*, is taken in the first way most commonly, according as it is divided against idleness or inactivity, as the natural operation due to any thing, or to which each thing is ordered, and according to this the Philosopher says: "That whose use is good, etc."

In the second way it is said commonly; and in this way use is divided against disuse. And in this way it is defined by Victorinus: "Use is an act frequently elicited from a power," and in this way it can be said that it is the act of every power and adds nothing over and above act except frequency.

In the third way, *to use* is taken properly; and in this way it is divided against the habits of memory and understanding, namely against natural talent and learning. And in this way it is defined by Augustine in the tenth book of *On the Trinity*: "To use is to take something up into the faculty of the will," and in this way it properly expresses an act of the will.

In the fourth way it is taken more properly; and in this way it is divided against the quietative act, namely against enjoyment. And in this way it is defined by Augustine: "To use is to take something up into the faculty of the will for the sake of something else," and in this way it is the act of the will as related toward something else.

In the fifth way it is taken most properly; and in this way it is divided against the disordered act, namely against misuse. And in this way it is defined by Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: "To use is to refer what has come into use to that which is to be enjoyed," and in this way *to use* expresses an act of the will that is related and ordered to an end.

The reason for the different definitions, therefore, is the multiple acceptation of what it is to use; and the reason for the multiple acceptation is its opposition to different things.

If therefore the question is raised, of which power it is the act: it must be said that, speaking most commonly and commonly, it is the act of every power; and in this way Augustine does not speak; but properly, more properly, and most properly, it is the act of the will itself.

1. And thus the first objection on the contrary side is clear, namely that it is the act of every power.

2. To what is objected second, that every power is referred to its end through its own act: it must be said that that power is said to use its act which is mistress of its act; and it belongs to such a power to refer its act, not merely to be referred. And since the will alone is mistress of its act and alone moves itself, therefore it alone is the power whose role it is to use actively. To the other powers it falls to use materially and passively, because they have to be referred, and not to refer. And hence it is that Augustine says: "To use is to take something up into the faculty of the will"; for *faculty* is said to be its dominion, by which it can readily pass to its act as its sovereign.

3. To what is objected — that to refer belongs to reason — it must be said that to refer is twofold: by way of comparison and inference, as premises are referred to a conclusion; and this belongs to reason, and this is not use. And there is referring by way of inclination and ordering toward something else; and this properly belongs to the will, because it can take something up by resting in it or by intending something else; and this is the referring by which use is defined, etc.

4. To what is objected concerning art, it must be said that art concerns what belongs to operation and what belongs to speculation. Insofar as it concerns what belongs to speculation, it does not involve use, and in this respect it belongs to reason; but insofar as it concerns what belongs to operation, it extends to the will and regulates it in using.

### Question II: Whether every created thing is to be used

Second, having seen what use is in its essence, the question is raised concerning using, namely whether every created thing is to be used. And that it is, seems to be shown in the following way.

1. Proverbs 16: *The Lord has made all things for himself*; therefore all things are capable of being ordered to God; but whatever is capable of being ordered to God can in fact be ordered; but in ordering what is orderable to God we rightly use it: therefore, if all things can be ordered, all things are to be used.

2. Likewise, being and the good are convertible, as Dionysius holds; but all things are beings: therefore all things are good. But everything good is to be loved: therefore every created thing is to be loved; but it is not to be loved for its own sake: therefore for the sake of another; but this is to use: therefore it is possible to use every created thing.

3. Likewise, every virtue can be rightly used: therefore the work of every virtue is right use; but the use of certain virtues is with respect to the evil of punishment, as patience, and of certain others with respect to the evil of fault, as penance: therefore it is possible to rightly use all evils — both evils of fault and evils of punishment — and much more, then, goods: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, "opposites are naturally concerned with the same subject"; but it is possible to misuse all things: therefore it is possible to rightly use all things: therefore every created thing is to be used.

On the contrary: 1. Charity is that by which we rightly use things, because all things that are rightly ordered are ordered to their end through charity; but as Augustine says in *On Christian Doctrine*: "Only four things are to be loved out of charity, and only three of these are created: ourselves, our neighbor, and our own body": therefore only three things are referred or ordered to their end through charity — since there is no right use except through charity — and therefore only three things are to be used; therefore not every created thing.

2. Likewise, all things that we use are subject to our will; but there are certain things that are not subject to our will, such as necessary and eternal things: therefore those things are not to be used.

3. Likewise, everything that we rightly use we do for a good end; but there are certain things that cannot be done for any good end — such as lying and things of that kind, which "as soon as they are named are bound up with evil": therefore such things are not to be used.

4. Likewise, virtue cannot be badly used, as is clear from its definition: therefore, by contrast, things opposed to virtue — evils of fault, or vices — cannot be rightly used: therefore not all things besides God are to be used.

CONCLUSION.

*We cannot use every created thing as an instrument, nor as a habit, nor as an act; yet we can use every thing as an object, but in four ways: either by doing, or by accepting, or by enduring, or by rejecting.*

I respond: It must be said that to use something is said in four ways: either as an instrument by which we operate, as someone uses a tool or organ; and in this way it is not possible to use all things — for instance, purely natural powers cannot be used, taking *to use* in the proper sense as Augustine takes it; or as a habit by which we are regulated, for instance a virtue; and in this way it is not possible to rightly use all things, as for instance a habit of vice, by which one is rather prone to deviate; or as an act by which we are moved; and in this way it is not possible to rightly use every act, as for instance those

which as soon as they are named are bound up with evil; or as an object toward which we are inclined; and in this way it is possible to use and misuse all things. For all things can be taken up into the faculty of the will for approving or rejecting; and this is made clear by Augustine in the book of the *Eighty-three Questions*: "We use things for taking up or rejecting unto health, for enduring unto patience, for ordering unto justice."

Likewise, Augustine in the tenth book of *On the Trinity*: "The will takes up the whole understanding, while I use everything that I understand"; but evil things are understood: therefore it is possible to use evil things.

Yet we use good things in one way and evil things in another. For the ground of ordering in good things is not only on the part of the one ordering, but also on the part of the thing ordered, because good things are by themselves usable and orderable, and this by their nature; but in evil things the ground of ordering is not on the part of the things ordered, but rather on the part of the one ordering; and therefore evil things are not said to be usable.

Hence note that, since to use is to take something up into the faculty of the will, this can occur in four ways: either into the faculty of the will as operating, as is the case with those things that properly pertain to human use; or into the faculty of the will as accepting, as is the love of neighbor and such things; or into the faculty of the will as enduring, as are the evils of punishment; or into the faculty of the will as rejecting, and in this way evils of fault are taken up and ordered. The first two ways are what is meant by: "That whose use is good, etc."

1. To what is therefore objected concerning charity — that only three created things are to be loved — it must be said that to love something out of charity is to order it to the highest good, which charity loves through itself. Now this can occur in two ways: either one orders what is naturally disposed to arrive at the end; and in this way only three created things are to be loved out of charity; or one orders that through which one is to arrive at the highest good; and because this can be the case in its own way through every creature, every creature can be loved out of charity, and it is possible to use every creature.

2. To what is objected — that not all things are subject to the will — it must be said that something is said to be subject to the dominion of the will in two ways: either with respect to the being of the thing itself; and in this way not all things are subject; or with respect to the act of the will, which consists in rejecting or approving the thing, and this either for its own sake or for the sake of another; and in this way all things are subject to our will, namely with respect to the act of seeking or rejecting, though not in the first way.

3. 4. To what is objected — that certain things cannot be done for any good end, and similarly that we always misuse vice — it must be said that the objection proceeds along the path by which it is possible to use something as a mediate act; and in this way it is not possible to use sin; nor in the way of a regulating habit, similarly; yet it is possible to use it as an object rightly, through the movement of contrition and detestation.

### Question III: Whether only created good is to be used

Third, having shown whether every created thing and everything other than God is to be used, the question is whether only created good is to be used. And that it is, seems to be shown.

1. "Every good is either an end or ordered to an end"; but only good that is ordered to an end is to be used, because the character of the useful is to lead to an end: therefore since only created good is ordered to an end, only created good is to be used.

2. Likewise, every good is either created or uncreated. But if it is possible to use uncreated good, it is possible to refer it to something else: either to something created or to something uncreated; but not to something uncreated, because there is only one uncreated thing: therefore to something created. But in this way the end is referred to the way and the cause to the effect; but this is misuse, not use: therefore only created good, or the creature, is to be used.

3. Likewise, Augustine in the book of the *Eighty-three Questions* divides the honorable against the useful: therefore nothing useful is honorable or conversely; but God is the honorable through essence: therefore God is not to be used.

4. Likewise, it seems that not only is God or uncreated good not to be used, but that to use him is always a mortal sin, because Augustine in the book of the *Eighty-three Questions* says: "It is the height of perversity to enjoy what is to be used and to use what is to be enjoyed"; but one who uses uncreated good uses what is to be enjoyed: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. It is possible to rightly serve God in view of a reward, because many are good hired servants, as Ambrose says; but it is not possible to serve God except by loving him: therefore it is possible to rightly love God in view of a reward; but one who loves in this way uses God, because he refers him to something else: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, the highest goodness stands in relation to love as the highest truth stands in relation to knowledge; but it is possible to know created truth more clearly without prejudice to the highest truth and without error: therefore similarly it is possible to love another goodness than the highest more ardently without prejudice to the highest goodness and without disorder: therefore it is possible by ordered love to love something else more than God: therefore it is possible to love God for the sake of something else in an ordered way, and so to use him.

3. Likewise, it seems that it is no sin when someone uses God for the sake of his own salvation, because "that whose end is good is itself good"; but the end of this use is good, namely our salvation: therefore the use of God is good: therefore it will not be a sin.

4. Likewise, use of God is either good — and in this case God is to be used; or it is evil — and in this case God is evil, because "that whose use is evil is itself evil"; but if God is good, his use is also good: therefore God is to be used.

CONCLUSION.

*Only created good is to be used, and to use uncreated good is to misuse.*

I respond: It must be said that only created good is to be used, because if we use uncreated good, it is always misuse, and such misuse is a mortal sin by reason of perversion toward the end with delight, and by reason of the disorder of the will, which loves what it uses less than that for the sake of which it uses it.

1. To what is therefore objected — that it is possible to rightly serve God in view of a reward — it must be said that that reward is either God himself, as he said to Abraham in Genesis 15: *I am your reward*; and in this case one who looks to the reward does not use, because he does not refer to something else; or that reward is something else, and then one can look to it either as a cause that moves — and in this case he is a bad hired servant; or as a reason that persuades — and in this case he is a good one, and in this way he does not use.

2. To what is objected — that clearer knowledge of a creature does not prejudice the Creator, therefore, etc. — it must be said that the cases are not alike, because the clarity of knowledge is not in our power, but the ardor of affection is in our power; therefore it is required of us that we order this, not the former. Yet a certain act of knowledge is in our power, for instance the act of faith; and if someone assents to the first truth on account of something else, this rightly prejudices the truth, just as loving on account of something else prejudices goodness.

3. To what is objected — that the end of the use of God is good — it must be said that an end is called good in two ways: either with respect to substance or with respect to the intention of ending; and that proposition is understood with respect to the intention of ending, and this consists in right ordering.

4. To the last objection it must be said that that proposition is understood of things that have an ordering to the end, but not of the end itself. — Or it is understood of the natural and proper act of things themselves, which is more frequently elicited from the thing itself, not of the ordering to the end; and in this way the proposition does not apply to the case at hand.

## Article II: What Enjoyment Is (*Frui*)

### Question Unica: Whether enjoyment is an act of the will or of other powers

Having treated of use and what is to be used, the question is subsequently raised concerning enjoyment and what is to be enjoyed, and first, what enjoyment is in its essence, namely whether it is an act of the will or of other powers.

1. That it is an act of the will seems to be shown by the first definition, which is: *Enjoyment is to cleave in love*, etc.; but love belongs to the will: therefore enjoyment similarly. If you say that love is taken there commonly, according as it is in any power with respect to its act — against this: the same is said in all the other definitions. Whence it is said in the next: "We enjoy things known, in which the will, delighted, comes to rest." But rest belongs to the will. And in another definition similarly: "Enjoyment is to use with joy." But joy properly pertains to the will.

2. Likewise, it is shown by argument, because in enjoyment we are delighted, according as the delightful is a difference of the good, since the good is the honorable, the conferring, and the delightful; but the good is the object of the will: therefore enjoyment belongs to the will alone.

3. Likewise, we enjoy that in which we come to rest; but rest regards the account of the end, "and the end regards the account of the good," and "the good is the object of the will": therefore enjoyment, which orders toward that, similarly.

On the contrary: 1. Every power desires to be united with its object, and once it has it, is delighted, if it knows it, and comes to rest: therefore motion with rest and delight belongs to all the powers: therefore since enjoyment is such, enjoyment will be in all, not only in the will.

2. Likewise, the absence of the beloved thing grieves the lover more than it delights him; for what delights him is that he sees and has it. If therefore the movement of enjoyment is with delight or is delight itself: therefore this is because he sees that in which the delight itself is; but vision regards the cognitive power: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: "This is the highest reward, that we fully enjoy him." But upon that word of the Psalm, *I will show him salvation*, etc., the Gloss says that "vision is the whole reward": therefore if enjoyment is the reward, enjoyment is vision essentially; but vision is in the cognitive power or in reason: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, by faith and hope we tend toward God: either therefore by enjoying or by using; but not by using, since God is not to be used: therefore by enjoying: therefore we enjoy by faith and hope; but faith is a habit of the cognitive reason; but whose is the habit, his is the act: therefore the act of enjoyment is an act of reason; the same seems to be true of the irascible power: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSION.

*Enjoyment taken essentially is an act of the will; taken dispositively it is also an act of the other powers.*

I respond: According to some, enjoyment pertains to all the powers; and they say this because all the powers will be rewarded; they also say that in glory it will be more properly of reason, because it stands in a more immediate relation to delight.

But it seems it must be said otherwise, according to Augustine, namely that it is an act of the will itself. For since three definitions of enjoyment are given, all are given by reference to an act of the will, which is to be considered in three ways. In the first way, commonly, insofar as it expresses motion with delight; and in this way Augustine defines it: "Enjoyment is to use with joy." In the second way, insofar as it expresses motion with rest; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: "Enjoyment is to cleave in love to some thing for its own sake"; and in this way it is taken properly. In the third way it is taken insofar as it encompasses both, namely rest and delight; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in the tenth book of *On the Trinity*: "Enjoyment is to rest in things known, with the will delighted on account of them for their own sake," and in this way it is taken most properly.

Because therefore enjoyment according to every acceptation expresses delight or rest or both, and everything of this kind has the account of the good, and this is the object of the will: therefore speaking essentially, enjoyment is an act of the will. But because the will is neither delighted nor at rest except in what it knows either through faith or through vision, and in what it has either through hope or in reality, therefore the acts of the other powers dispose toward this, yet they are not enjoyment itself, speaking essentially.

From this the solution is clear to what is commonly asked: why enjoyment is not defined through an act of knowledge as it is through delight. Yet this question is founded upon a falsehood. For Augustine places in one definition that it belongs to knowledge, when he says: "Enjoyment is to rest in things known"; yet knowledge falls there as a disposition.

From this it is also clear why enjoyment is not defined through faith and hope as it is through charity, because charity directly moves the concupiscible power, whose act it is to enjoy. Yet this question is similarly founded upon a falsehood, because the love by which enjoyment is defined is common to chaste love and to disordered love, by which the miser enjoys gold, and is not proper to charity itself.

1. To what is therefore objected — that every power is delighted and at rest when united with its object — it must be said that the will itself, as Augustine holds, loves for itself and for others. And as Anselm also says: "The will inclines the other powers and merits on their behalf"; therefore its rest and delight overflow into the other powers. Whence just as the will does not know for itself, but reason knows for itself and for the will, so the will is delighted for itself and for reason and causes it to be at rest.

2. To the objection that the lover does not enjoy unless he sees or has: it must be said that seeing and having are required for enjoyment, and similarly loving. For if someone sees something and has it, he is never delighted unless he loves it; yet vision is required in a different way than love. For vision disposes, and so does possession, but love supplies the delight. Hence it is as it were a penetrating point, and therefore it is most properly suited to unite and consequently to delight and to bring to rest: therefore it is enjoyment essentially, not dispositively. For this reason it is to be understood that the act of the will can be considered in two ways, namely by way of appetite and by way of complacency. In the first way it can precede vision itself; in the second it follows, and in this consists the perfect account of enjoyment itself, namely in complacency with respect to the thing seen and possessed.

3. To what is objected — that vision is the whole reward — it must be said that this is not said properly, but by concomitance, because vision and complacency, in which the perfect account of enjoyment consists, are inseparably related.

4. To what is objected — that we enjoy by faith and hope — it must be said that this is not true through itself, because each of these receives the account of tending from charity; therefore enjoyment is not from these except through charity. For charity alone regards the end under the account of end and object. Hence one is not said to hope God as one is said to love him.

## Article III: On What Is to Be Enjoyed

### Question I: Whether God is to be enjoyed

Concerning the first, that God is to be enjoyed is shown as follows.

1. "That is to be enjoyed which makes us blessed," because in blessedness there is right enjoyment; but by God we are made blessed, because he is our blessedness: therefore God is to be enjoyed.

2. Likewise, the good is lovable; therefore the more good is more lovable and the supremely good is supremely lovable; but we enjoy what we love supremely: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the beautiful delights, and what is more beautiful delights more, therefore what is supremely beautiful delights supremely; but we enjoy that in which we are supremely delighted: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. Where there is enjoyment there is delight; "but delight is the conjunction of the fitting with the fitting"; but between God and the creature there is no fittingness, but rather the greatest distance: therefore neither delight, therefore neither enjoyment.

2. Likewise, an excellent sensible object corrupts the sense or at least causes distress, because delight is in the middle terms: therefore by the same reasoning an excellent intelligible object corrupts or causes distress to the intellect. But God is the most excellent light: therefore he causes distress, not delight, therefore, etc. If you say that the sensory power is corruptible but the intellect is not — this does not resolve the difficulty, because the intellect, as long as it is in the flesh, is wearied and impeded in its act when the body is corrupted.

3. Likewise, where there is enjoyment there is rest; but in the infinite there is no rest, "because there is always something more to take outside": therefore since God is infinite, in God there is no rest, therefore neither enjoyment.

CONCLUSION.

*God properly is to be enjoyed.*

I respond: It must be said that God is to be enjoyed, because he alone perfectly brings the soul to its end and delights it for its own sake and above all things.

1. To what is therefore objected — that delight is the conjunction of the fitting, etc. — it must be said that there is fittingness either through participation in one nature or through common relation. The first fittingness produces the community of univocation, the second the community of analogy or proportion. And this is according to a threefold difference: either according to the similar relation of two things to two, as man stands to animal, so whiteness stands to color; or according to the dissimilar relation of two things to one, as of animal and food to health; or according to the comparison of two things to each other, as for instance when one is an imitation or likeness of the other. For a likeness does not agree with its counterpart in virtue of a third term, but by itself. So it is in the present case, because the soul is an expressed likeness of God. This agreement places inclination and need in one extreme, and rest and sufficiency in the other, because one has been made for the sake of the other, and is therefore ordered toward the other. And since, where there is sense, delight either accompanies or follows the quieting of inclination and the supplying of need, the soul, when it is quieted by God in this manner, enjoys Him.

2. To the objection that an exceedingly intense sensible destroys the sense — therefore, etc. — it must be said that the cases of intellectual understanding and sensing are not alike. And for understanding there is a threefold account of this: one is from the side of the apprehensive power, another from the side of the apprehended object, and a third from the side of the mode of apprehending.

From the side of the apprehensive power: because the sense can be destroyed, but the intellect cannot. The account of this is that the sense depends upon an organ, in which there is a certain mean and harmony, which is corrupted not only by what is contrary to it but also by what is excessive; but the intellect does not depend upon an organ, because it is an immaterial power, and therefore does not suffer in the presence of what is excellent.

From the side of the object the cases are not alike, because an excellent object helps and strengthens, since the influence of such a knowable thing proceeds from within and enters into the power itself, and therefore strengthens and fortifies it. Just as if a great mountain were to give the power to carry itself, it would be carried more easily than a small one; so it is with the intelligible, which God is. A sensible object, however, only arouses from without, and therefore the one destroys while the other does not.

From the side of the mode of apprehending there is similarly a dissimilarity, because the sense in apprehending its object tends toward the exterior, and in perceiving it is dispersed outwardly and not fortified inwardly, and therefore is weakened. But the object of the intellect, since it is most interior to the intellect itself, in the perception of it the power is not dispersed but gathered, and the more united the power is, the stronger it is.

3. To the objection concerning the infinite: it must be said that "infinite" is used in two ways — namely, by way of privation of perfection, and in this way matter is said to be infinite, and such an infinite thing has no power to bring to an end, but rather needs to be brought to an end, to the extent that this is possible. In another way "infinite" is used by way of privation of limitation; and what is infinite in this sense properly has the power to bring to an end, because beyond it, since nothing greater can be conceived, one cannot desire anything further. Hence such infinity belongs to the ultimate end, which most of all has the character of that which brings to an end.

### Question II: Whether God alone, or uncreated good alone, is to be enjoyed

Finally it is asked whether God alone, or uncreated good alone, is to be enjoyed; and that this is so seems evident.

1. That alone is to be enjoyed which is to be loved for its own sake; but that alone is to be loved for its own sake which is good for its own sake; and that is good for its own sake whose goodness it possesses neither from another nor is that goodness ordered toward anything else; but this is God alone: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, that alone is to be enjoyed which quiets the soul's appetite; but the soul's appetite is not sufficiently quieted except in that which is a more perfect and greater good than it; but this is God alone: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the appetite is not sufficiently quieted except by something that fills the soul, because the soul falls short of rest to the extent that it falls short of fullness; but the soul is capable of God according to its appetite: therefore, since every created thing is infinitely less than He, none supplies the soul's appetite. And this is what Augustine says: "Nothing less than the Trinity can fill a soul capable of the whole Trinity."

4. Likewise, a good that can be thought can be desired: therefore if a greater good than some good can be conceived, something greater than it can be desired; but something greater than every finite thing can be conceived: therefore something greater can be desired: therefore no finite thing sufficiently terminates the soul's appetite: therefore only the infinite good, which is God, is to be enjoyed, for once He is possessed, nothing further can be conceived or desired.

On the contrary: 1. It seems that it is possible to enjoy a created good, because, although the appetite of matter is infinite, it is nevertheless sufficiently terminated by an incorruptible created form: therefore it similarly seems that there is some created good that will sufficiently terminate the soul's appetite; but every such thing is to be enjoyed: therefore some created good is to be enjoyed.

2. Likewise, human appetite is of finite capacity, therefore it only grasps the finite, or if it grasps the infinite, it grasps it finitely; therefore if it is terminated by what it grasps, it is terminated by a finite good: therefore it seems that some finite good is to be enjoyed.

3. Likewise, it seems in particular that a human being is to be enjoyed, because everything that God enjoys is to be enjoyed; but God enjoys a human being, because He loves a human being; but He loves a human being with the same love by which He loves Himself, because there is not in Him a twofold love; but the first is of enjoyment: therefore the second is as well.

4. Likewise, it seems in particular concerning virtue; because everything that is to be desired for its own sake is to be enjoyed; this is evident in itself; but every honest good is to be desired for its own sake, "because the honorable is that which by its own force draws us and by its own dignity allures us," as Tullius says, and Augustine divides the honorable against the useful; but virtue falls under the genus of the honorable: therefore virtue is to be enjoyed.

5. Likewise, it seems in particular concerning beatitude. Everything is to be enjoyed whereby, once possessed, the mind rests and delights and can seek nothing further; but created beatitude is of this kind: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSION.

*God alone is properly to be enjoyed; it is permissible to enjoy spiritual goods improperly, insofar as they delight and are joined to the end.*

It must be said that God alone is to be enjoyed, taking "enjoy" in its proper sense, as it denotes movement with both delight and rest. But taking "enjoy" in its common sense, as it denotes movement with delight only, a human being may not wrongly enjoy all those things that delight spiritually and are joined to the end, such as fruits, gifts, and beatitudes; but in the first sense, God alone.

And the account of this is that nothing can sufficiently bring the soul to its end except the good for which it exists. But this is the highest good, which is above the soul, and the infinite good, which exceeds the soul's powers. For the soul's natural cognition is unrestricted cognition; hence it is naturally suited in some way to know all things, and therefore its cognition is not filled by any knowable thing except that which has within itself all knowable things and by the knowledge of which all things are known. Similarly its affection is naturally suited to love every good; therefore the affection is not sufficiently terminated by any good except that which is the good of every good and which is all in all. Concerning this good it is said in Exodus thirty-three: "I will show you all good." But this is the highest good: therefore, etc.

Likewise, nothing sufficiently brings the soul to its end except the infinite good, because for it to be brought to its end, it is necessary that it be terminated according to estimation; otherwise it would not be blessed unless it reckoned itself blessed. But estimation surpasses every finite thing, because greater than any finite thing can always be conceived: therefore, since affection can extend itself wherever estimation can, the affection of the soul necessarily surpasses every finite thing; and if this is so, it is not sufficiently terminated by any finite good. Therefore God alone is to be enjoyed, because He is the highest and infinite good.

And not only is God alone to be enjoyed because of perfect termination, but also because of perfect delight. For delight requires the delightful thing and its conjunction with that which is delighted. But everything that delights the soul delights under the account of the good and the beautiful; and since God alone is goodness and beauty itself, therefore in God alone is there perfect delight.

Likewise, because there is conjunction; but God alone is He who is conjoined most perfectly. For He is conjoined according to truth and intimacy. For God alone, on account of His supreme simplicity and spirituality, glides into the soul, so that according to truth He is in the soul and more intimate to the soul than it is to itself.

All four of these accounts reduce to one, namely this: because the soul is naturally suited to receive the infinite good, which is God, therefore it ought to rest in Him alone and enjoy Him.

1. To the objection concerning the appetite of matter: it must be said that the appetite of matter is ordered toward form as toward its substantial perfection, from which together with matter one thing is made; and therefore it is necessary that the form be of the same genus as matter, and it is not altogether above matter: therefore every such form is finite. But such a form, if it is incorruptible, by reason of its union restricts the appetite of matter and, having restricted it, terminates it; hence it is not terminated in its full amplitude. But the appetite of the soul is not so restricted by its union with the desirable object; on the contrary it is expanded; and therefore it must be perfected by something altogether supreme.

2. To the objection that it grasps only finitely: it must be said that it grasps the infinite good finitely, since the soul itself is finite. But since that good is infinite, the soul is therefore totally absorbed by it, so that its capacity is now bounded on all sides. Hence it will not merely rejoice, but as Anselm says, it will enter into the joy of the Lord. For if it were only grasping and were not overcome and absorbed, there could still arise an appetite to grasp something more. It is therefore clear that in order for the soul to be fulfilled, even though it has finite capacity, it is nonetheless necessary that the infinite good be present.

3. To the objection concerning the human being: it must be said that God loves a human being, and God's love is a love of enjoyment; yet He does not enjoy the human being, because in loving Himself He loves the human being, that is, in enjoying Himself He uses the human being. This is clear as follows: because the love of God toward a creature is not an affection but a communication of effect. Communication according to its habitual account is understood with respect to goodness, because the good is what is naturally suited to communicate itself. According to its actual account, however, it is understood with respect to or regards the will, because it is because He wills that He acts. Therefore the account of communicating comes from will and goodness, just as the account of knowing comes from the exemplar and understanding. Because, therefore, He loves us from the turning of His will upon His own goodness, He loves us in enjoying Himself.

4. To the objection concerning virtue, that virtue is an honest good: it must be said that "honest good" is used in two ways — either that which is purely good, or that in which its likeness shines forth. In the first sense the honest is to be enjoyed, taking "enjoy" properly, because this alone terminates and quiets. In the second sense it is not, unless "enjoy" is taken commonly, as it denotes delight. For things of this kind, by reason of their assimilation to God, have a beauty by which they delight and allure us; yet one must not rest in them, because then they lose their beauty, since "virtues, when referred to themselves, are proud and inflated."

5. To the objection concerning beatitude: it must be said that created beatitude does not terminate the appetite through itself, but through conjunction with uncreated beatitude: hence the soul loves it for the sake of God, not for its own sake.

## Doubts Concerning the Master's Text

DOUBT I.

In this part several doubts arise concerning the text, and first concerning what he says: "But of these there are some whose every use consists in signifying, not in justifying." But on the contrary: All sacraments are medicines, because according to Hugh of Saint Victor the sacraments have been medicines in every age; but every medicine has its effect in curing: therefore the sacraments likewise; but this curing is justification: therefore in every age the sacraments have had the power to justify.

I respond: It must be said that the sacraments of the Old Law can be considered in two ways — namely in themselves or by reason of the work done (*ex opere operato*), and in this sense they were only for signifying; or by reason of the faith attached or charity or by reason of the work of the one performing (*ex opere operantis*), and in this sense the legal sacraments were for justifying, apart from Circumcision.

DOUBT II.

"Every sign, therefore, is something." An objection is raised against this division. For it seems that the Master divides badly, because a superior is not divided against its inferior; but "thing" is superior to "sign": therefore, etc.

I respond: It must be said that it is true that a superior taken in its full generality is not divided against its inferior; but a contracted superior can well be divided. Here, however, "thing" is contracted to stand for a thing that does not signify.

DOUBT III.

Likewise, a doubt arises concerning what the Master says: "But not conversely is every thing a sign." For he seems to speak badly, because a sign is that which leads to the knowledge of something else; but every thing leads to something else, because every thing is either a cause or an effect. But a cause leads to the knowledge of the effect and conversely. If you say that not everything that leads is a sign, but only what leads to what is prior — then every creature is a sign, since it leads to God.

I respond: It must be said that sign is twofold, namely natural and instituted. The first is not what is intended here, but the second; hence the objection does not apply to the matter at hand, since what is intended here is the sign by institution, such as vocal and sacramental signs.

DOUBT IV.

Likewise, the Master seems to speak badly in what he says: "First concerning things, afterward concerning signs." For he seems to have ought rather to say the reverse, because a sign leads to the knowledge of a thing: therefore one ought first to treat or speak of signs than of things.

I respond: It must be said that knowledge of the sign is prior to the thing with respect to which it is a sign; but this need not be true of every thing. Hence he first treats of things of which the sacraments are not signs.

DOUBT V.

Likewise, it seems that this division of things — "Some are those which are to be enjoyed, etc." — is badly assigned with respect to the thing divided, because the thing divided is simpler than the dividing members and something is common to the dividing members; but nothing is simpler than God, nor is anything common to God and creature: therefore, etc.

I respond: It must be said that there is a division of one common thing according to name alone, and this is a division of an equivocal; there is another division of what is common according to both name and thing, and this is a division of a univocal; and there is also a third, in a middle manner — and this is the division of an analogical, where there is no community according to unity of nature, but according to similarity of proportion; and this community is one of reason.

DOUBT VI.

Likewise, it seems that the division is bad with respect to its members: for it seems that the third member is superfluous. For every thing is either to be used or to be enjoyed: therefore the third is superfluous.

Likewise, just as there is a thing that enjoys and uses, so too there is a thing that is both to be enjoyed and to be used, such as Christ: therefore, for the same reason that the third member is posited, a fourth ought to be posited.

I respond: Every thing which is some nature is either an end — and so it is a thing to be enjoyed; or something leading to the end — and so it is a thing to be used; or something arriving at the end — and so it is a thing that enjoys and uses, such as the human being: and so the members are taken. And this division is sufficiently fitting for this science, because since theology is about the Creator and about creatures, it is useful for the theologian to teach how the human being tends toward God through other things.

DOUBT VII.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "Things which are to be enjoyed" — because if the three are things through themselves and not through accident, and from the diversity of things there is diversity or multiplicity of enjoyment: therefore just as there are three things, so there will be three enjoyments.

Likewise, there are three articles of faith, because they concern three persons: therefore by analogy there are also three enjoyments.

I respond: That "thing" is here taken commonly for both essence and person on account of its generality; and because this word "thing" is taken from an act of the soul, therefore three are called things because there are three persons, yet nonetheless one thing, because there is one goodness in them. Because therefore there is one goodness, therefore also one enjoyment, although there are three things. — And since an article of faith states something from the side of the distinguishing soul, namely the intellect, and enjoyment regards the divine essence itself and goodness and the union of affection commonly, therefore enjoyment is one.

DOUBT VIII.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "Everyone who enjoys also uses" — because this seems false, since foolish human beings as well as beasts enjoy, because they are delighted in the delightful for its own sake, but they do not use. If you say it is said by way of transfer; why is the word "using" not similarly transferred?

I respond: "To use" in its general sense implies freedom, and in its specific sense implies freedom with relation to another; "to enjoy" implies freedom with delight; and although neither freedom nor comparison belongs to brutes, delight does belong to them. Therefore "to use" in no way belongs to brutes; but "to enjoy" belongs to them in some way, though improperly.

DOUBT IX.

Likewise, it seems that he speaks badly in saying that hope is not to be placed in a human being, because of the Blessed Virgin the Church sings "Life, sweetness, and hope" — and the Church does not err.

Likewise, in Jonah chapter one: "The helmsman approached him"; Gloss: "It is natural for a human being to trust more in others than in himself in dangers."

I respond: It must be said that to hope in someone is twofold — either as in a helper, or as in a savior. The first hope can be in a human being, the second cannot. — Or: to hope for the final reward — and this ought to be only in God; or for a temporal benefit — and this can be in a human being. If the first is placed in a human being, it merits a curse, according to [the Scripture].

DOUBT X.

Likewise, it is asked why he says concerning a human being: "Therefore, brother, I shall enjoy you in the Lord," rather than concerning a donkey or some other creature, since in all creatures the vestige of the divine goodness shines forth.

I respond: What causes one to enjoy something in God is not representation, but rather indwelling. Hence the converse expression is the proper one, namely: "I shall enjoy the Lord in you," while I know through works that God dwells in you; and the other is a figurative manner of speaking.

DOUBT XI.

Likewise, a question arises concerning this solution of Augustine, by which he resolves the aforementioned authority through that qualification "in the Lord." But in Ecclesiasticus chapter eight it is said simply: "Enjoy the great man." Likewise, in Deuteronomy chapter twenty-eight: "You will beget sons and daughters, and you will not enjoy them" — from this it implies that if they did not sin, they would enjoy them.

I respond: It must be said that the aforementioned solution is not general, but only applies to the proposed authority. And therefore one must have recourse to the solution given outside the letter in the questions — that since "enjoy" is used commonly and properly, in the aforementioned passages it is used only commonly.

DOUBT XII.

Likewise, what the Master says does not seem to hold: "For if he neither enjoys nor uses, etc." — because God loves Himself, yet does not use Himself, because He does not love Himself for the sake of something else; nor does He enjoy Himself, because where there is enjoyment, there is adherence and need, as Augustine says; but God lacks both, etc.

I respond: It must be said that enjoyment in its general account denotes a union of love, namely of the enjoyable with the one enjoying. Since therefore it is possible for something to be united to itself and to another, it is possible not only to enjoy another but also oneself; but where there is union to another, there is dependence, adherence, and need; where however it is union to oneself, if that thing is the highest good, there is no adherence or dependence or need, but utter sufficiency. But if the good is deficient, it stands in need; and hence it is that God alone perfectly enjoys Himself, while nothing other than Him can perfectly enjoy itself. For God alone is the highest good and loves Himself in enjoying Himself, and also loves other things in enjoying Himself: and thus neither God's enjoyment nor His use is accompanied by need, as our enjoyment and use are.

DOUBT XIII.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "Because He is good, we exist": therefore because He is good from eternity, we exist from eternity.

I respond: It must be said briefly that this is so because "good" states a cause. But there is a natural and necessary cause, and a voluntary cause. The divine goodness is the cause of our existing and our being good by way of will, not of nature; but a voluntary cause does not immediately produce its effect when it exists.

DOUBT XIV.

Likewise, it is further objected: why does it similarly not follow: "Because He is just, we are just"? If you say that justice is not His disposition insofar as He is cause; on the contrary: wisdom is a disposition of God insofar as He is cause: therefore by equal reasoning, because He is wise, we are wise. If you say that it is not an immediate disposition, as goodness is; an objection is raised concerning will, which is most immediate, and yet it does not follow: because He is willing, we are willing.

I respond: It must be said that the account of this inference is threefold. The first is that goodness is the condition of a cause insofar as it is a cause; the second is that [it] is most proximate to act; the third, because it is a general disposition in which the character of a vestige consists, for it denotes relation to an end. Hence just as God as one imprints unity, so God as good imprints goodness.

DOUBT XV.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "That it should be loved for its own sake alone" — because as "through itself" is to "through accident," so "for its own sake" is to "for the sake of another." But nothing that belongs to something through accident belongs to it through itself: therefore nothing that belongs to something for the sake of another belongs to it for its own sake. If therefore virtues are to be desired for the sake of another, then not for their own sake.

I respond: As the Philosopher says, "the end and the 'for the sake of which' are the same." But the end is twofold: in one way the ultimate end, which is the end in the most proper sense, in which there is rest; in another way an end under an end, which is called properly a terminus. Thus "for the sake of which" is also diversified; hence in one way it excludes the end, in another it does not, as the Master says.

As for the objection concerning "through itself": I say that, just as "through itself" in one sense is opposed to what is "through another" — and in this sense "through itself" is the same as "according to what it itself is," or "the first universal" — and in another sense is opposed to what is "through accident": so too "for its own sake" is taken in two ways. In one way it is opposed to what is "for the sake of another," and in another way it is opposed to what is "through accident," because it is ordered toward that thing only accidentally. According to this, the Master distinguishes "for its own sake," and consequently the honorable or the enjoyable, and enjoyment itself.

DOUBT XVI.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "And there are certain things through which we enjoy, such as the virtues." It seems that the soul could enjoy with its powers alone. For without any virtue it can enjoy a created good; therefore, since uncreated good is more enjoyable, and the soul is more naturally suited to this, it will be able to enjoy it with its powers alone.

I respond: It must be said that the highest good is that which is above us; therefore, in order for us to be united to it, a twofold medium necessarily intervenes: one medium through which the soul is naturally suited to be united to something diverse from itself — and this is the power; another special one beyond this, which elevates it — and this is virtue. The objection does not apply, because a power can of itself fall short and incline, but not of itself be elevated.
