CONCLUSION.

That there are only three divine persons is both taught by the Catholic faith and shown to be fitting by reason.

I respond: It must be said that, as the Catholic faith declares, only three persons are to be posited — no more and no fewer. And to this end both a reason of necessity and a reason of fittingness are brought forward.

The reason of necessity, as to why there cannot be fewer than three, is supreme beatitude and supreme perfection. For supreme beatitude requires love and co-love; supreme perfection requires a twofold emanation, namely of nature and of generosity; and for this at least three persons are required. — Likewise, the reason of necessity as to why there cannot be more is supreme simplicity, which does not permit persons to be distinguished except according to modes of emanating; and again, principal fruitfulness, which does not permit a person to produce by any kind of emanation unless it is, according to the order of understanding, prior to that emanation. Hence the first person, because it is unbegotten and unbreathed-forth, generates and spirates; the second, because it is capable of being breathed forth but is begotten, does not generate but does spirate; the third person, however, because it is breathed forth and proceeds from the one who generates, neither generates nor spirates. And therefore it is impossible for there to be more than three.

The reason of fittingness is taken from the sufficiency of combinations and from the perfection of the number three.

From the sufficiency of combinations: since, as Richard says, "love is in all the persons," and there are only three kinds of love — namely "gratuitous, owed, and mixed from both" — there will be only three persons: one that only gives, in which there is gratuitous love; another that only receives, in which there is owed love; and a middle one that both gives and receives, in which there is love mixed from both. — Likewise, the persons can be combined in another way according to the account of origin; and the sufficiency of this kind of combination consists in three. For one can conceive of a person that is a principle of a person and is not itself something principiated; and again a person that is something principiated and is not a principle of a person; and a third way, a person that is both something principiated and a principle. A fourth mode, however — that a person be neither a principle nor something principiated — is altogether impossible and inconceivable.

The reason of fittingness on the part of number is that this number — three — has both first and supreme perfection, whether it is considered in itself, in continuous quantity, or in creatures.

Considered in itself, it has first perfection, because it is the first number that is constituted from all its own parts — namely unity and duality, which together make three. The number six, however, is called the first among the perfect numbers, because it is constituted from all its aliquot parts — namely three, two, and one. — Likewise, supreme perfection is in it, because unity, which is the principle and completion of every number, reflected back upon itself by perfect reduplication — such as occurs in a solid square — is tripled according to reason while remaining one according to truth, as when one says: once one, once. And this is very similar to the uncreated Trinity, in which in the unity of substance there is a trinity of accounts; yet it is not altogether similar, because there, together with the unity of substance, there is a trinity of accounts and of things, namely of persons; here only of accounts.

Similarly, if this number is considered in continuous quantity, it has in itself first and supreme perfection: first, because every quantity has a beginning, a middle, and an end; supreme, because the highest perfection of continuous quantity consists in the threefold dimension, namely length, breadth, and height. And this is what the Philosopher says at the beginning of On the Heavens: "We say that everything perfect is determined by three, and with this number we have been accustomed to magnify God, the one creator of all things, who transcends the properties of what has been created."

Similarly, if this number is considered in creatures, it has in itself first and supreme perfection: first, because a trinity of vestige can be found in every creature, however small, however minimal; supreme, because the highest and most noble perfection of the creature — namely beatitude — is found in accordance with the trinity of the image as reformed and made God-like.

1. To the objection, therefore, that the Father gives to the Son everything He is able to give: it must be said that this is true, but He does not give in every way in which He is able. And therefore there is a fallacy of accident in the inference: "He cannot give more, therefore He cannot produce another person" — because this would not be giving more, but giving in another way.

2. Similarly, the following reply is clear: the Son does not declare in every way, because, even if He does so according to the account of nature, He does not do so according to the generosity of will — except insofar as from the Word itself the Spirit proceeds.

3. To the objection that the Holy Spirit ought to generate: it must be said that the cases are not alike, because the person of the Son precedes spiration, and therefore has the character of primacy; the person of the Holy Spirit follows generation, and therefore does not generate, because He is not unbegotten.

4. To the objection concerning the third mode of emanating, namely by way of art: it must be said that art has fruitfulness for emanating or producing only through will; and therefore that mode ought not to be distinguished from the mode of producing by way of generosity or will.

Or, alternatively and better: the mode of producing by way of art agrees with the mode of producing by way of nature in this, that in both cases something like the producer is produced. But it differs, because in natural production what is produced is like in substance and nature but other in person; whereas in production by way of art what is produced is like according to the account of the exemplary form, but unlike in substance and nature. But such a mode of producing is incompossible with the divine essence, which does not admit diversity of essences.

DOUBTS CONCERNING THE MASTER'S TEXT.

DOUBT I.

In this part the first doubt concerning the text arises from what the Master says: that the highest good "is seen by the most pure minds." For he seems to speak badly, because no mind, while still on the way, is most pure — that is only in the homeland.

I respond: It must be said that the mind, in order to contemplate God perfectly, needs to be purified with respect to intellect and affection; therefore he says "through the righteousness of faith" — that is, through faith, which makes one righteous in action and of itself purifies the intellect, while righteousness purifies the affection. And there are three degrees of each kind of purification. For the intellect is purified when it is abstracted from sensible species; more purified, when it is cleansed of phantasmatic images; most purified, when it is cleansed of philosophical reasoning. The degrees of purification of affection are these: the affection is purified when it is cleansed of fault; more purified, when it is cleansed of attachment; most purified, when cleansed of occasion — and in this state it is fitted for contemplation.

DOUBT II.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "the weak gaze of the human mind is not fixed upon so excellent a light," etc. For it seems that even when cleansed it cannot be fixed there, because however much it may be cleansed, that light still exceeds the mind's gaze beyond all proportion: therefore, if on account of its excellence it cannot be seen by one who lacks faith, neither can it be seen by one who has faith.

I respond: There is a difference between considering, being fixed, and comprehending. It can be considered by an unclean mind; but it cannot be fixed upon except by a pure mind; it cannot be comprehended except by the immense. The account of why it cannot be fixed is twofold: one, because it is above the intellect, and therefore the intellect is not fixed in it unless it has the adhesive of affection — otherwise it falls back immediately; another account, because a sound eye is proportionable to that light qualitatively, even if not quantitatively; but a weak or bleary eye is disproportionable in both ways, and therefore cannot be fixed.

DOUBT III.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning this statement of Ambrose: "God and Lord is a name of nature and a name of power" — because Damascene says that the name "God" is imposed from His operation, whence it is derived either from *aithein*, which means to burn, or from *theein*, which means to cherish, or from *theasthai*, which means to see.

I respond: It must be said that concerning this name and similar ones there are two ways of speaking: either with respect to that to which it is imposed — and in this sense it is a name of nature, because it is imposed upon that which is supreme nature; or with respect to that from which it is imposed — and in this sense it is a name of operation, because it is imposed from operation.

DOUBT IV.

Likewise, a question arises concerning this circumlocution: "I am who I am" — whether this name "I am," etc., is a name of essence or of person. That it is of a person seems evident, because a demonstrative pronoun signifies a certain person. And again, speaking is an act of a person. If you perhaps say that "I," because it signifies origin, stands for the person of the Father; "am," because it signifies an act going forth, for the person of the Son; and "who," as a relative connecting both, for the person of the Holy Spirit — this amounts to nothing, because the relative and the antecedent stand for the same thing.

I respond: It must be said that the name "He who is" and "I am who I am" is properly a name of essence — for this is a certain circumlocution, signifying entity in every kind of perfection and absoluteness, and this is the proper name of the divine substance. And to the objection that a pronoun signifies a certain person: it must be said that "person" there refers to a certain supposit of the Word, and this is substance and nature.

DOUBT V.

Likewise, a question arises concerning the authority: "Let us make man according to our image and likeness" — whether "image" there stands for essence or for person. If for essence, then to say "our image" is the same as to say "our essence"; if for person, then it ought to say "my" and not "our"; if for a created image, this is nothing to the matter at hand, since by this the unity of nature would not be proved.

I respond: It must be said that, according to the way the Saints understand it here, "image and likeness" denotes both essence and relation. For "image" imports unity with distinction, and "likeness" similarly, because of the intrinsic relation. And therefore in the name "image and likeness," with respect to some part of its signification, the unity of essence is indicated, and with respect to another part, the plurality of persons is indicated. And therefore Augustine expounds it in one way, and Hilary in another. Augustine considers the part of the signified, namely the unity of essence; but Hilary considers the whole. Hence Hilary says that by the name of image and likeness both unity and plurality are simultaneously understood. Similarly Augustine, in the expressions "let us make" and "our," considers only the co-signified — and therefore plurality. Hilary, however, considers both the signified and the co-signified, and therefore in both expressions he says that both plurality and unity are to be understood.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. IV continued):** Arguments 2–4 of the "On the contrary" section of Question IV, followed by the full *Conclusio* and replies to objections 1–4. Translated seamlessly from the prior chunk's end-point.
- **"Co-love" (condilectio) and Richard of St. Victor:** The argument that perfect love requires both love (*dilectio*) and co-love (*condilectio*) — i.e., a shared love for a third — is explicitly credited to Richard of St. Victor (*De Trinitate* V.16, 23). The footnote confirms this attribution. "Co-love" retained as before.
- **"Incompossible" (incompossibilis):** A scholastic technical term meaning "incapable of existing together with" — stronger than merely "impossible" in isolation. The Quaracchi editors restored this reading over the simpler *impossibilis* found in the Vatican codex. Rendered "incompossible" to preserve the technical precision.
- **Number theology (ternarius):** Bonaventure's elaborate argument for three as the perfect number draws on ancient Pythagorean-Neoplatonic arithmetic (via Aristotle's *De Caelo*, Isidore, Augustine, and ultimately Euclid). The argument that 1×1×1 = 1 while "tripling" unity is central to his analogy for the Trinity: three in reason/account (*rationes*), one in truth. The Scholion (omitted per brief) explains this arithmetic in considerable detail.
- **"Solid square" (solidum quadratum):** A reference to perfect reduplication — a number multiplied by itself twice (the cubic number). The Quaracchi editors note that *quadrata* is a scribal interpolation; the correct term is simply "perfect reduplication" (*reduplicatio perfecta*). The point is that 1 cubed = 1, making three the "perfect" self-reflection of unity.
- **"Trinity of vestige / trinity of image" (trinitas vestigii / trinitas imaginis):** Two degrees of Trinitarian trace in creatures — the vestige (found in all created things: unity, truth, goodness) and the image (found in rational creatures, especially as reformed by grace and glory). These are Bonaventurian technical categories; retained as "vestige" and "image" throughout.
- **Doubt IV on "I am who I am":** Bonaventure's solution — that "He who is" is a name of essence, not of any particular person — follows Augustine (*De Trinitate* V) and will be elaborated at dist. 22, q. 3. The trinitarian reading of the three grammatical elements (ego/sum/qui) is dismissed as a fallacy of identity between relative and antecedent.
- **Doubt V on "image and likeness":** Bonaventure's resolution of the Augustine/Hilary apparent conflict — Augustine takes "image" to signify unity of essence (the *signified*), while Hilary takes it to co-signify both essence and relation (signified + *co-signified*). The scholastic distinction between *significatum* and *consignificatum* is central here.
- **Doubt VI ends mid-text:** The chunk ends in the middle of Bonaventure's response to Doubt VI. Translated as given.
