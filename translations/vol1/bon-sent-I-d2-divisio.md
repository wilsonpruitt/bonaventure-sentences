DIST. II. DIVISION OF THE TEXT.

On the testimonies of the New Testament pertaining to the same.

Now, after the testimonies of the Old Testament concerning the faith of the holy Trinity and Unity, let us come to the authorities of the New Testament, so that the truth may be known in the midst of the two living creatures (that is, of the Testaments), and that the coal may be taken from the altar with tongs to touch the lips of the faithful. The Lord Christ, therefore, openly indicates the unity of the divine essence and the trinity of persons, saying to the Apostles: "Go, baptize all nations in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." "He says 'in the name,' as Ambrose says in the first book On the Trinity, not 'in the names'" — so that the unity of essence may be shown; through the three names which He placed there, He declared that there are three persons. "He also says: 'I and the Father are one.' He said 'one,' as Ambrose says in the same book, so that no separation of power and nature might arise; and He added 'are,' so that you might know the Father and the Son — namely, that the perfect Father is believed to have begotten a perfect Son, and that the Father and the Son are one, not by confusion of person, but by unity of nature." John also in his canonical Epistle says: "There are three who bear witness in heaven: the Father, the Word, and the Holy Spirit, and these three are one." He also at the beginning of his Gospel says: "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God" — where he openly shows that the Son was always and eternally with the Father, as one with another. The Apostle also openly distinguishes the Trinity, saying: "God sent the Spirit of His Son into our hearts." And elsewhere: "If His Spirit, who raised Jesus, dwells in us," etc. Likewise, elsewhere he most evidently commends both the Trinity and the unity, saying: "Because from Him, and through Him, and in Him are all things, to Him be glory." "He says 'from Him,' as Augustine says in the book On the Trinity, on account of the Father; He says 'through Him' on account of the Son; 'in Him' on account of the Holy Spirit." By the fact that he does not say "from them, through them, and in them," nor "to them be glory," but "to Him," he indicated that this Trinity is one Lord God. But since nearly every syllable of the New Testament concordantly indicates this truth of the ineffable Unity and Trinity, let us abstain from accumulating further testimonies on this matter and show by fitting arguments and analogies — to the extent that our weakness allows — that it is so.

On the Unity and Trinity according to what is believed.
"This therefore is to be held with true and pious faith," etc.

DIVISION OF THE TEXT.

This is the second part, in which the Master, having investigated the material of the first book, begins to pursue it further; and this part is divided into two parts, because things to be enjoyed can be considered in two ways — namely in themselves, and in comparison to creatures: in themselves, by reason of trinity and unity; in comparison to creatures, by reason of knowledge, power, and will. First, therefore, he treats of the holy Trinity and Unity; second, of knowledge and power and will, below at distinction thirty-five: "And since above we have discussed."

Concerning the most sacred Trinity itself, it is possible to treat of it in three ways, since one can first believe it, second understand what is believed, and third express or enunciate what is understood. To believe is through authority; to understand is through reason; to express is through Catholic and rational discourse. Therefore he first treats of the Trinity and Unity itself as it is believed; second, of the same as what is believed and understood through reason, at distinction three: "For the Apostle says"; third, as what is believed and understood is expressed rationally and in a Catholic manner, below at distinction twenty-two: "After what has been said above, it seems to us that we must discuss the diversity of names." And the order is clear. For it is prior and more fundamental to believe than to understand — for many believe who do not understand; and similarly it is prior to understand than to express in speech.

The first part, namely according to what is believed, contains the present distinction; and since the matter is most difficult, he first sets out the mode of proceeding; and second, he carries it out, at: "Let us therefore set forth in the midst."

Likewise, the first part has three sub-parts according to the three chapters. In the first he touches on the mode of treatment — that it must be conducted with modesty, fear, and diligence — premising the subject matter around which he intends to proceed. In the second he subjoins the intention of those who have written on the Trinity, at the second chapter: "All the Catholic writers, however." Third, he touches on the order, at: "Furthermore, as in the first book."

The mode of writing about the Trinity must be grounded in the understanding of faith and conducted with modesty and fear on account of the danger. The intention of those writing about the Trinity is to show that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are three persons and one God. The order of writing is, first, to demonstrate the truth through authorities, then through arguments and fitting analogies.

"Let us therefore set forth in the midst." This is the second part, in which the Master intends to show the Trinity and Unity through the authorities of sacred Scripture; and since sacred Scripture has two parts — namely the New and the Old Testament — he therefore first shows this through the authorities of the Old Testament, then through the authorities of the New, at: "But now after the testimonies," etc. And since the Old Testament has two parts — namely the Law and the Prophets, in which the faith is unfolded — he therefore shows it first through the testimonies of the Law, and second through the testimonies of the Prophets, at: "That greatest of prophets." The first part again, in which he proves through the testimonies of the Law, has two parts: for he first proves the unity of the essence; second, unity together with trinity, at: "The Lord also showed at once the plurality of persons." Similarly, that part concerning the testimonies of the Prophets has two: in the first he proves the unity of essence and the plurality of persons in general; in the second, he shows in particular the generation of the Son and the procession of the Holy Spirit, at: "David also openly hints at the eternal generation of the Son." Similarly, that part in which he proves through the authorities of the New Testament has two parts: for first he proves through the authorities or testimonies of Jesus Christ; second, through the authorities of the Apostles, at: "John also in his canonical Epistle." The authorities and their explanations, their number and varieties, are sufficiently clear in the text.

TREATMENT OF THE QUESTIONS.

For the clarification of what the Master says about the holy Trinity and Unity, four questions can be raised concerning this part.

First, it is asked whether the unity of essence or nature is to be posited in God.

Second, whether a plurality of persons is to be posited in God.

Third, whether infinity is to be posited in the divine persons.

Fourth and finally, whether a trinity is to be posited in the divine persons.

On the unity of the divine essence and the plurality of persons.

### Notes

- **Placement of this chunk:** The *Divisio Textus* for Distinction II is a structural commentary by Bonaventure on the Lombard's text, explaining the logical and thematic organization of the material before proceeding to the questions. It also includes the concluding section of the Lombard's own text on New Testament Trinitarian testimonies. Both are translated in sequence as given.
- **"In the midst of the two living creatures" (in medio duorum animalium):** An allegorical reading of Habakkuk 3:2 (LXX: *in medio duorum animalium*) applied to the two Testaments as living creatures flanking the truth. This is Augustine's application of the verse (*De Civitate Dei* XVIII.32). The allusion to the coal from the altar (*forcipe de altari*) is from Isaiah 6:6. Both rendered literally to preserve the typological imagery.
- **Structure of the *Divisio Textus*:** This genre is Bonaventure's architectural map of the Lombard's text — a scholastic *divisio* that identifies every logical subdivision before the commentary proceeds to questions. It is not argumentation but structural analysis, and is translated in that register (analytical, declarative).
- **"Catholic and rational discourse" (catholicam et rationabilem locutionem):** Bonaventure's third mode for treating the Trinity — after *credere* (believe) and *intelligere* (understand through reason) — is *dicere* (express properly). This threefold schema (belief → understanding → expression) organizes the whole of Distinction II, mapping onto Distinctions 2, 3, and 22 respectively.
- **"That than which nothing greater can be conceived" (quo maius excogitari non potest):** Anselm's famous formulation from the *Proslogion*, here cited as a "common conception of the mind" (*communis animi conceptio*). Retained in the standard English translation.
