DIST. VII. DOUBTS.

---

*[Continuation of a Doubt begun in the preceding chunk — the question concerns whether the Holy Spirit could be or could have been the Son:]*

— the more a relation differs from a supposit, it can be said that what is similar to another can be dissimilar to it without any change in itself: therefore, since filiation and spiration are relations, by the same account the Son could be the Holy Spirit and conversely, without any change. Likewise, everything mutable is something other than that according to which it is changed, as when what is not white becomes white, it is other than whiteness: therefore if the Holy Spirit is understood to be changed, if he were to be generated, then generation would be one thing in him and the one who is generated would be another; but this is false: therefore, etc.

I respond: The case of relations in creatures and in God is different: because relations in creatures do not give relational things their existence; hence a thing can exist without them and with them. But in divine things relations give the persons their existence; and therefore if it is understood that a property inheres in a hypostasis, the existence of the hypostasis is understood, and likewise if it is understood not to inhere, the non-existence of the hypostasis is understood. Therefore, since the Holy Spirit cannot simultaneously be spirated and generated — because although one person can produce in two ways, one person cannot be produced in more than one way — it follows of necessity: if the Holy Spirit can be or could have been the Son, he can be or could have been not the Holy Spirit, i.e., not spirated; and if he could have been not spirated, he could have not existed; and again if he could have been generated, he could have existed; and thus it would have been possible for him to pass from being to non-being and from non-being to being, and thereby to be changed.

And thus the last point is clarified: he is not said to be changed by reason of a diversification of properties around the same supposit, as occurs in natural change, but by reason of the corruption and inception of that same supposit. Nor does this parallel hold: the Father can generate and spir­ate without any change in himself: therefore the Holy Spirit can be generated and spirated; because it is not unfitting that one principle produce in multiple ways, but it is impossible and incompossible that one thing produced be produced in multiple ways; and thus the matter is clarified.

DOUBT V.

Likewise it seems false to say, as is said: "The Father is powerful only through nature; for his power is his essence" — because on this basis generating, which in no way belongs to essence, would in no way belong to power.

I respond: Although power, considered absolutely, is the same as essence, nevertheless power, since it is not a name as abstract as essence, is drawn toward the person. Hence the power of generating does not express the divine nature simply, but the nature as in such a person — namely, the innascible; therefore the name "essence" and the name "power" are not parallel in this respect.

---

DOUBT VI.

Likewise it seems to be said improperly where it says: "You would signify another power, by which the Father is able to beget, etc."; because the sufficient division of power is into active and passive; but the power to beget expresses active power, and the power to be begotten expresses passive power: therefore no other power can be signified. If you say that to generate is not properly speaking to act, and likewise that to be generated is not properly speaking to undergo: it is objected that powers are distinguished by acts; but to beget and to be begotten are different: therefore the power of the one and the power of the other are different.

I respond: The distinction that the Master makes between the power to beget in the active sense and in the passive sense is grounded on this, that power can be considered according to what it is; and in this way it expresses nature, and just as one nature is in the one generating and the one generated, but in one way and another, so the same power. It can again be considered according to that for which it is; and in this way it expresses the mode of nature's existing in a person in relation to another who is either principle or principiated. And in this way, since the modes are different, the properties of the power to generate and the power to be generated are said to be different, and are distinguished according to personal acts; but not in the first way.

---

DOUBT VII.

Likewise there is a question concerning his statement that the Son has the power by which he can be Son or by which he can be begotten. For this seems doubtful or false, because to be begotten belongs entirely to the act of begetting, and begetting belongs to the power of the Father: therefore so too does being begotten.

I respond: Just as the power of begetting in the Father expresses nothing other than nature in a person as naturally fitted to produce another from itself, so the power of begetting in the Son — in the passive sense — expresses nothing other than nature in a person as naturally fitted to be produced by another; for just as it befits the hypostasis of the Father to generate, so it befits the hypostasis of the Son to be generated. Nor does the objection on the other side hold. For everything the Son has, whether essential or personal, he has from the Father. Therefore, although being begotten itself is from the power of the Father, this does not exclude there being a passive power of being begotten in the Son.

---

DISTINCTION VIII.

On the truth and proper character of the divine essence.

Now there must be treated the truth or proper character, and the immutability and simplicity, of the divine nature, or substance, or essence. "God is, as Augustine says in the fifth book *On the Trinity*, without any doubt a substance, or — if that name is better — an essence, which the Greeks call *ousia*. For just as wisdom is named from what it is to be wise, and knowledge from what it is to know, so essence is named from what it is to be. And who exists more truly than he who said to his servant Moses in Exodus 3: *I am who I am*; and you shall say to the sons of Israel: *He who is has sent me to you*?" He truly and properly is called essence, whose essence knows neither past nor future. Hence Jerome, writing to Marcella, says: "God alone, who has no beginning, holds the name of true essence, because in comparison with him who truly is — because he is immutable — things that are mutable are as if they are not. For of what is said 'it was,' it is not; and of what is said 'it will be,' it is not yet. But God alone is, who does not know having been or being about to be. God alone therefore truly is, compared to whose essence our existence is not."

Here it must be carefully noted how those words of Jerome are to be understood: "he does not know having been or being about to be, but only being" — as though it could not be said of God that he was or will be, but only that he is — since we find it frequently written of him: "he was from eternity," "he was always," "he will be for ages," and the like; whence it seems that of God one should say not only that he was, or that he is, or that he will be. For if it were said only that he was, it would be thought that he has ceased to be; if it were said only that he is, it would be thought that he has not always been but that he began to be; if it were said only that he will be, it would be thought that he does not exist now. Let it be said therefore that he always was, is, and will be, so that it is understood that he neither began nor ceased nor ceases nor will cease to be. Concerning this, Augustine on John says: "When 'is' is properly said of an eternal thing, according to our manner of speaking 'was' and 'will be' and 'is' are well said: 'was,' because he never ceased; 'will be,' because he will never be lacking; 'is,' because he always is: he has not passed away as something that does not endure; he does not arise as something that did not exist. Therefore, when our speech varies through tenses, the verbs of every tense are truly said of him who was absent at no time, is not absent, and will not be absent; and therefore it is no wonder if, speaking of the Spirit of truth, the Truth said by way of the future tense: *Whatever he will hear, he will speak* — 'will hear,' namely from him from whom he proceeds. His hearing is his knowing, and it is also his being. From whom, therefore, he has his essence, from him he has his hearing, that is, his knowledge, which is nothing other than his essence. 'He will hear,' therefore, was said of what he heard and hears, that is, of what he always knew, knows, and will know." Behold, Augustine here says that the verbs of every tense are said of God, but that properly speaking "is" is the right one. Therefore what Jerome says is to be understood thus: "he does not know having been or being about to be, but only being" — that is, when it is said of God that he was or will be, it is not to be understood that he has passed away or is yet to come, but that he simply exists without any temporal motion. For although the substantive verbs of various tenses are said of God — was, will be, is, was [imperfect], is [present] — yet they do not then distinguish temporal movements, such as past, future, imperfect past, perfect past, or pluperfect, but —

DISTINCTION VIII, PART II.

On the immutability of the same.

The essence of God alone is also properly called immutable, because it neither is changed nor can be changed. Hence Augustine in the fifth book *On the Trinity* says: "Other essences or substances admit accidents, through which change, great or small, is brought about in them; but nothing of this kind can befall God; and therefore the substance or essence alone which is God is immutable, to which certainly it most truly and fully belongs to be. For what is changed does not preserve being itself; and what can be changed, even if it is not changed, can cease to be what it was; and therefore that alone which not only is not changed but also absolutely cannot be changed is most truly said to be" — namely the substance of Father and Son and Holy Spirit. And therefore the Apostle, speaking of God, says: *Who alone has immortality.* For as Augustine says in the first book *On the Trinity*: "Since the soul is said in a certain way to be immortal and is so, the Apostle would not say *God alone has immortality* unless it were because true immortality is immutability, which no creature can have, since it belongs to the Creator alone." Hence James says: *With whom there is no change nor shadow of alteration.* And David: *You will change them and they will be changed; but you are the same.* Therefore Augustine on Genesis says that God is moved neither through places nor through times, while creatures are moved through times and places. And to be moved through times is to be changed through affections; but God can be changed neither by place nor by affection, who says through the Prophet: *I am God and I am not changed* — who alone is immutable. Hence he is rightly said alone to have immortality. "For in every mutable nature," as Augustine says *Against Maximinus*, "there is a certain death in change itself, because it causes something to not be in it that previously was. Hence even the human soul, which is therefore said to be immortal because in its own manner it never ceases to live, nevertheless has its own death; because if it was living justly and sins, it dies to justice; if it was sinful and is justified, it dies to sin — to say nothing of its other changes, about which it would take long to dispute now. And the nature of heavenly creatures could die, because it could sin. For the angels too sinned and became demons, of whom the devil is prince; and those who did not sin could have sinned; and to whatever rational creature it is granted that it cannot sin, this is not from its own nature but from the grace of God. And therefore God alone, as the Apostle says, has immortality, who not by the grace of anyone but by his own nature neither could nor can be changed by any conversion, nor could nor will he ever sin by any change." "Therefore, as Augustine says in the first book *On the Trinity*, to contemplate and know the substance of God — making mutable things without any change in himself and creating temporal things without any temporal motion in himself — though it is difficult, is necessary." Truly therefore and properly immutable is the essence of the Divinity alone, which without any change in itself established all natures.

Chapter III.

On the simplicity of the same.

And this same essence alone is properly and truly simple, in which there is no diversity or variation or multiplicity either of parts or of accidents or of any forms whatsoever. But that you may know how that substance is simple, as Augustine teaches you in the sixth book *On the Trinity*, "observe first why every creature is multiple and in no way truly simple — first concerning corporeal, then concerning spiritual creatures. Corporeal creature certainly consists of parts, such that there is one part smaller and another larger, and the whole is greater than any part; and in any given body, magnitude is one thing, color another, and figure another. For with magnitude diminished, the same color and the same figure can remain; and with color changed, the same figure and the same magnitude can remain. And thereby the nature of body is shown to be multiple, but in no way simple."

---

On how corporeal and spiritual creatures are multiple and not simple.

"Spiritual creature too, such as the soul, in comparison with body is indeed simple, but without comparison with body it is multiple and not simple. It is called simple in respect of body because it is not diffused through space by bulk, but in any given body it is whole in the whole and whole in any part of it. And therefore when something occurs in any small particle of a body that the soul may sense, although it does not occur in the whole body, yet the soul senses it wholly, because it does not escape it wholly. Yet even in the soul itself there is no true simplicity" —

---

That God, though simple, is nonetheless spoken of in many ways.

Here it must be carefully noted that, since Augustine says that God alone is truly simple, why he says that the same God is spoken of in many ways. But he does not say this on account of diversity of accidents or parts, but on account of the diversity and multiplicity of names that are said of God; which, though multiple, signify one thing, namely the divine nature. For these names are not accepted, when said of that immutable and eternal substance incomparably simpler than the human mind, in the same way as when they are said of creatures. Hence Augustine in the sixth book *On the Trinity*: "For God, to be is the same as to be strong, or to be wise, or to be just, and whatever of that simple multiplicity or multiple simplicity you may say, by which his substance is signified. But for the human mind, to be is not the same as to be strong, or prudent, or just; for a mind can exist and have none of those virtues."

---

Chapter VI.

That the simplicity of God is subject to none of the categories.

That in the divine nature there is no diversity of accidents and absolutely no mutability but perfect simplicity, Augustine shows in the fifth book *On the Trinity* saying: "Let us understand God, as far as we are able, as good without quality, great without quantity, creator without need, presiding without position, containing all things without habitus, wholly everywhere without place, everlasting without time, making mutable things without any change in himself, and suffering nothing. Whoever thinks of God in this way, even if he cannot yet fully discover what he is, nevertheless piously takes care, as far as he can, not to think of him something that he is not." Behold, if you attend carefully, from these words and what was said before it becomes clear that the categories of the dialectical art are wholly unsuitable to the nature of God, which is subject to no accidents.

---

Chapter VII.

That God is improperly called substance.

Hence "substance" is not even properly predicated of him, as Augustine shows in the seventh book *On the Trinity*: "Just as 'essence' is named from what it is to be, so we call 'substance' from what it is to subsist, if at least it is fitting that God be said to subsist. For this is rightly understood of those things in which inhere what are said to be in some subject, as color or form in a body. For body subsists and is therefore a substance. Mutable things therefore, and non-simple things, are properly called substances. But if God subsists such that he can properly be called substance, then something inheres in him as in a subject, and he is not simple. But it is impious to say that God subsists and is subject to his own goodness, and that goodness is not itself a substance or rather essence, and that God himself is not his own goodness but that it is in him as in a subject. Hence it is manifest that God is called substance improperly, so that by a more common name what is truly and properly called 'essence' may be understood, so that perhaps God alone ought to be called essence. For he alone truly is, because he is immutable."

---

Chapter VIII.

That there is nothing in God that is not God.

The simplicity and purity of this essence is so great that there is nothing in it that is not it itself; but the possessor and what is possessed are the same. Hence Hilary in the seventh book *On the Trinity* says: "God, who is life, does not subsist from things composed; nor is he who is power contained by weak things; nor is he who is light fitted together from dark things; nor is he who is Spirit formed from disparate things: all that is in him is one." The same in the eighth book *On the Trinity*: "God is not in the human manner from composed things, such that in him would be one thing —"

VIII. Bonaventure's own *Divisio Textus* and *Tractatio Quaestionum* for Distinction VIII are not yet present and will follow in subsequent chunks.

- **D.VII Doubt IV (completion)**: The opening resolves a question about whether the Holy Spirit could be the Son. The key move is that in God, relations *constitute* hypostases rather than modifying pre-existing ones (as in creatures). Therefore, if the Holy Spirit's constitutive property (spiration) were replaced by generation, the Holy Spirit would cease to exist and a different person would begin — genuine change in the ontological sense, not mere relational variation.

- **"incompossible" (*incompossibile*)**: Established term from earlier chunks. Here deployed to assert that one principiated thing being produced by two distinct principles of origin is incompossible — the modes of being produced are mutually exclusive for a single divine person.

- **D.VII Doubt V — "power" drawn toward the person**: Bonaventure's resolution clarifies the D.VII Q.I *Conclusio* from a different angle: "power" (*potentia*), unlike "essence" (*essentia*), is not fully abstract and therefore is drawn (*trahitur*) toward the personal mode. Thus *potentia generandi* picks out the nature as it exists in the innascible person. The term "innascible" (*innascibilis*) refers to the Father's incapacity to be generated — his defining personal property.

- **D.VII Doubt VI — active/passive distinction grounded in relational mode**: The Master's distinction between the power to beget (active) and the power to be begotten (passive) is not, Bonaventure clarifies, a distinction in the power *as such* (which is the single divine nature) but in the *mode* of the nature's existing in a person relative to another — whether as principle or as principiated. The distinction is personal, not essential.

- **D.VIII base text**: The Lombard's text for Distinction VIII treats three divine attributes: (1) truth/proper being (*veritas / proprietas*), (2) immutability (*incommutabilitas*), (3) simplicity (*simplicitas*). These are expounded through a dense sequence of Augustine citations (De Trinitate V, VI, VII; Ep. ad Marcellam; In Ioannem; De Genesi; Contra Maximinum), Jerome, Hilary, James, and the Psalms. The translation preserves Bonaventure's full reproduction of the Lombard's text, including embedded patristic quotations, without editorial compression.

- **"essence" vs. "substance"**: Chapter VII contains the Lombard's important Augustinian point that "substance" (*substantia*) is technically improper for God because it implies a subject in which accidents inhere (*subsistit* → *substat*), whereas "essence" (*essentia*) from *esse* is the only fully proper name. This will be theologically significant for Bonaventure's own questions in D.VIII.
