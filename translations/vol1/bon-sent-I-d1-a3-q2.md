This agreement places inclination and need in one extreme, and rest and sufficiency in the other, because one has been made for the sake of the other, and is therefore ordered toward the other. And since, where there is sense, delight either accompanies or follows the quieting of inclination and the supplying of need, the soul, when it is quieted by God in this manner, enjoys Him.

2. To the objection that an exceedingly intense sensible destroys the sense — therefore, etc. — it must be said that the cases of intellectual understanding and sensing are not alike. And for understanding there is a threefold account of this: one is from the side of the apprehensive power, another from the side of the apprehended object, and a third from the side of the mode of apprehending.

From the side of the apprehensive power: because the sense can be destroyed, but the intellect cannot. The account of this is that the sense depends upon an organ, in which there is a certain mean and harmony, which is corrupted not only by what is contrary to it but also by what is excessive; but the intellect does not depend upon an organ, because it is an immaterial power, and therefore does not suffer in the presence of what is excellent.

From the side of the object the cases are not alike, because an excellent object helps and strengthens, since the influence of such a knowable thing proceeds from within and enters into the power itself, and therefore strengthens and fortifies it. Just as if a great mountain were to give the power to carry itself, it would be carried more easily than a small one; so it is with the intelligible, which God is. A sensible object, however, only arouses from without, and therefore the one destroys while the other does not.

From the side of the mode of apprehending there is similarly a dissimilarity, because the sense in apprehending its object tends toward the exterior, and in perceiving it is dispersed outwardly and not fortified inwardly, and therefore is weakened. But the object of the intellect, since it is most interior to the intellect itself, in the perception of it the power is not dispersed but gathered, and the more united the power is, the stronger it is.

3. To the objection concerning the infinite: it must be said that "infinite" is used in two ways — namely, by way of privation of perfection, and in this way matter is said to be infinite, and such an infinite thing has no power to bring to an end, but rather needs to be brought to an end, to the extent that this is possible. In another way "infinite" is used by way of privation of limitation; and what is infinite in this sense properly has the power to bring to an end, because beyond it, since nothing greater can be conceived, one cannot desire anything further. Hence such infinity belongs to the ultimate end, which most of all has the character of that which brings to an end.

QUESTION II.

Whether God alone, or uncreated good alone, is to be enjoyed.

Finally it is asked whether God alone, or uncreated good alone, is to be enjoyed; and that this is so seems [evident].

1. That alone is to be enjoyed which is to be loved for its own sake; but that alone is to be loved for its own sake which is good for its own sake; and that is good for its own sake whose goodness it possesses neither from another nor is that goodness ordered toward anything else; but this is God alone: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, that alone is to be enjoyed which quiets the soul's appetite; but the soul's appetite is not sufficiently quieted except in that which is a more perfect and greater good than it; but this is God alone: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the appetite is not sufficiently quieted except by something that fills the soul, because the soul falls short of rest to the extent that it falls short of fullness; but the soul is capable of God according to its appetite: therefore, since every created thing is infinitely less than He, none supplies the soul's appetite. And this is what Augustine says: "Nothing less than the Trinity can fill a soul capable of the whole Trinity."

4. Likewise, a good that can be thought can be desired: therefore if a greater good than some good can be conceived, something greater than it can be desired; but something greater than every finite thing can be conceived: therefore something greater can be desired: therefore no finite thing sufficiently terminates the soul's appetite: therefore only the infinite good, which is God, is to be enjoyed, for once He is possessed, nothing further can be conceived or desired.

On the contrary: 1. It seems that it is possible to enjoy a created good, because, although the appetite of matter is infinite, it is nevertheless sufficiently terminated by an incorruptible created form: therefore it similarly seems that there is some created good that will sufficiently terminate the soul's appetite; but every such thing is to be enjoyed: therefore some created good is to be enjoyed.

2. Likewise, human appetite is of finite capacity, therefore it only grasps the finite, or if it grasps the infinite, it grasps it finitely; therefore if it is terminated by what it grasps, it is terminated by a finite good: therefore it seems that some finite good is to be enjoyed.

3. Likewise, it seems in particular that a human being is to be enjoyed, because everything that God enjoys is to be enjoyed; but God enjoys a human being, because He loves a human being; but He loves a human being with the same love by which He loves Himself, because there is not in Him a twofold love; but the first is of enjoyment: therefore the second is as well.

4. Likewise, it seems in particular concerning virtue; because everything that is to be desired for its own sake is to be enjoyed; this is evident in itself; but every honest good is to be desired for its own sake, "because the honorable is that which by its own force draws us and by its own dignity allures us," as Tullius says, and Augustine divides the honorable against the useful; but virtue falls under the genus of the honorable: therefore virtue is to be enjoyed.

5. Likewise, it seems in particular concerning beatitude. Everything is to be enjoyed whereby, once possessed, the mind rests and delights and can seek nothing further; but created beatitude is of this kind: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSION.

God alone is properly to be enjoyed; it is permissible to enjoy spiritual goods improperly, insofar as they delight and are joined to the end.

It must be said that God alone is to be enjoyed, taking "enjoy" in its proper sense, as it denotes movement with both delight and rest. But taking "enjoy" in its common sense, as it denotes movement with delight only, a human being may not wrongly enjoy all those things that delight spiritually and are joined to the end, such as fruits, gifts, and beatitudes; but in the first sense, God alone.

And the account of this is that nothing can sufficiently bring the soul to its end except the good for which it exists. But this is the highest good, which is above the soul, and the infinite good, which exceeds the soul's powers. For the soul's natural cognition is unrestricted cognition; hence it is naturally suited in some way to know all things, and therefore its cognition is not filled by any knowable thing except that which has within itself all knowable things and by the knowledge of which all things are known. Similarly its affection is naturally suited to love every good; therefore the affection is not sufficiently terminated by any good except that which is the good of every good and which is all in all. Concerning this good it is said in Exodus thirty-three: "I will show you all good." But this is the highest good: therefore, etc.

Likewise, nothing sufficiently brings the soul to its end except the infinite good, because for it to be brought to its end, it is necessary that it be terminated according to estimation; otherwise it would not be blessed unless it reckoned itself blessed. But estimation surpasses every finite thing, because greater than any finite thing can always be conceived: therefore, since affection can extend itself wherever estimation can, the affection of the soul necessarily surpasses every finite thing; and if this is so, it is not sufficiently terminated by any finite good. Therefore God alone is to be enjoyed, because He is the highest and infinite good.

And not only is God alone to be enjoyed because of perfect termination, but also because of perfect delight. For delight requires the delightful thing and its conjunction with that which is delighted. But everything that delights the soul delights under the account of the good and the beautiful; and since God alone is goodness and beauty itself, therefore in God alone is there perfect delight.

Likewise, because there is conjunction; but God alone is He who is conjoined most perfectly. For He is conjoined according to truth and intimacy. For God alone, on account of His supreme simplicity and spirituality, glides into the soul, so that according to truth He is in the soul and more intimate to the soul than it is to itself.

All four of these accounts reduce to one, namely this: because the soul is naturally suited to receive the infinite good, which is God, therefore it ought to rest in Him alone and enjoy Him.

1. To the objection concerning the appetite of matter: it must be said that the appetite of matter is ordered toward form as toward its substantial perfection, from which together with matter one thing is made; and therefore it is necessary that the form be of the same genus as matter, and it is not altogether above matter: therefore every such form is finite. But such a form, if it is incorruptible, by reason of its union restricts the appetite of matter and, having restricted it, terminates it; hence it is not terminated in its full amplitude. But the appetite of the soul is not so restricted by its union with the desirable object; on the contrary it is expanded; and therefore it must be perfected by something altogether supreme.

2. To the objection that it grasps only finitely: it must be said that it grasps the infinite good finitely, since the soul itself is finite. But since that good is infinite, the soul is therefore totally absorbed by it, so that its capacity is now bounded on all sides. Hence it will not merely rejoice, but as Anselm says, it will enter into the joy of the Lord. For if it were only grasping and were not overcome and absorbed, there could still arise an appetite to grasp something more. It is therefore clear that in order for the soul to be fulfilled, even though it has finite capacity, it is nonetheless necessary that the infinite good be present.

3. To the objection concerning the human being: it must be said that God loves a human being, and God's love is a love of enjoyment; yet He does not enjoy the human being, because in loving Himself He loves the human being, that is, in enjoying Himself He uses the human being. This is clear as follows: because the love of God toward a creature is not an affection but a communication of effect. Communication according to its habitual account is understood with respect to goodness, because the good is what is naturally suited to communicate itself. According to its actual account, however, it is understood with respect to or regards the will, because it is because He wills that He acts. Therefore the account of communicating comes from will and goodness, just as the account of knowing comes from the exemplar and understanding. Because, therefore, He loves us from the turning of His will upon His own goodness, He loves us in enjoying Himself.

4. To the objection concerning virtue, that virtue is an honest good: it must be said that "honest good" is used in two ways — either that which is purely good, or that in which its likeness shines forth. In the first sense the honest is to be enjoyed, taking "enjoy" properly, because this alone terminates and quiets. In the second sense it is not, unless "enjoy" is taken commonly, as it denotes delight. For things of this kind, by reason of their assimilation to God, have a beauty by which they delight and allure us; yet one must not rest in them, because then they lose their beauty, since "virtues, when referred to themselves, are proud and inflated."

5. To the objection concerning beatitude: it must be said that created beatitude does not terminate the appetite through itself, but through conjunction with uncreated beatitude: hence the soul loves it for the sake of God, not for its own sake.

DOUBTS CONCERNING THE MASTER'S TEXT.

DOUBT I.

In this part several doubts arise concerning the text, and first concerning what he says: "But of these there are some whose every use consists in signifying, not in justifying." But on the contrary: All sacraments are medicines, because according to Hugh of Saint Victor the sacraments have been medicines in every age; but every medicine has its effect in curing: therefore the sacraments likewise; but this curing is justification: therefore in every age the sacraments have had the power to justify.

I respond: It must be said that the sacraments of the Old Law can be considered in two ways — namely in themselves or by reason of the work done (ex opere operato), and in this sense they were only for signifying; or by reason of the faith attached or charity or by reason of the work of the one performing (ex opere operantis), and in this sense the legal sacraments were for justifying, apart from Circumcision.

DOUBT II.

"Every sign, therefore, is something." An objection is raised against this division. For it seems that the Master divides badly, because a superior is not divided against its inferior; but "thing" is superior to "sign": therefore, etc.

I respond: It must be said that it is true that a superior taken in its full generality is not divided against its inferior; but a contracted superior can well be divided. Here, however, "thing" is contracted to stand for a thing that does not signify.

DOUBT III.

Likewise, a doubt arises concerning what the Master says: "But not conversely is every thing a sign." For he seems to speak badly, because a sign is that which leads to the knowledge of something else; but every thing leads to something else, because every thing is either a cause or an effect. But a cause leads to the knowledge of the effect and conversely. If you say that not everything that leads is a sign, but only what leads to what is prior — then every creature is a sign, since it leads to God.

I respond: It must be said that sign is twofold, namely natural and instituted. The first is not what is intended here, but the second; hence the objection does not apply to the matter at hand, since what is intended here is the sign by institution, such as vocal and sacramental signs.

DOUBT IV.

Likewise, the Master seems to speak badly in what he says: "First concerning things, afterward concerning signs." For he seems to have ought rather to say the reverse, because a sign leads to the knowledge of a thing: therefore one ought first to treat or speak of signs than of things.

I respond: It must be said that knowledge of the sign is prior to the thing with respect to which it is a sign; but this need not be true of every thing. Hence he first treats of things of which the sacraments are not signs.

DOUBT V.

Likewise, it seems that this division of things — "Some are those which are to be enjoyed, etc." — is badly assigned with respect to the thing divided, because the thing divided is simpler than the dividing members and something is common to the dividing members; but nothing is simpler than God, nor is anything common to God and creature: therefore, etc.

I respond: It must be said that there is a division of one common thing according to name alone, and this is a division of an equivocal; there is another division of what is common according to both name and thing, and this is a division of a univocal; and there is also a third, in a middle manner — and this is the division of an analogical, where there is no community according to unity of nature, but according to similarity of proportion; and this community is one of reason.

DOUBT VI.

Likewise, it seems that the division is bad with respect to its members: for it seems that the third member is superfluous. For every thing is either to be used or to be enjoyed: therefore the third is superfluous.

Likewise, just as there is a thing that enjoys and uses, so too there is a thing that is both to be enjoyed and to be used, such as Christ: therefore, for the same reason that the third member is posited, a fourth ought to be posited.

I respond: Every thing which is some nature is either an end — and so it is a thing to be enjoyed; or something leading to the end — and so it is a thing to be used; or something arriving at the end — and so it is a thing that enjoys and uses, such as the human being: and so the members are taken. And this division is sufficiently fitting for this science, because since theology is about the Creator and about creatures, it is useful for the theologian to teach how the human being tends toward God through other things.

DOUBT VII.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "Things which are to be enjoyed" — because if the three are things through themselves and not through accident, and from the diversity of things there is diversity or multiplicity of enjoyment: therefore just as there are three things, so there will be three enjoyments.

Likewise, there are three articles of faith, because they concern three persons: therefore by analogy there are also three enjoyments.

I respond: That "thing" is here taken commonly for both essence and person on account of its generality; and because this word "thing" is taken from an act of the soul, therefore three are called things because there are three persons, yet nonetheless one thing, because there is one goodness in them. Because therefore there is one goodness, therefore also one enjoyment, although there are three things. — And since an article of faith states something from the side of the distinguishing soul, namely the intellect, and enjoyment regards the divine essence itself and goodness and the union of affection commonly, therefore enjoyment is one.

DOUBT VIII.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "Everyone who enjoys also uses" — because this seems false, since foolish human beings as well as beasts enjoy, because they are delighted in the delightful for its own sake, but they do not use. If you say it is said by way of transfer; why is the word "using" not similarly transferred?

I respond: "To use" in its general sense implies freedom, and in its specific sense implies freedom with relation to another; "to enjoy" implies freedom with delight; and although neither freedom nor comparison belongs to brutes, delight does belong to them. Therefore "to use" in no way belongs to brutes; but "to enjoy" belongs to them in some way, though improperly.

DOUBT IX.

Likewise, it seems that he speaks badly in saying that hope is not to be placed in a human being, because of the Blessed Virgin the Church sings "Life, sweetness, and hope" — and the Church does not err.

Likewise, in Jonah chapter one: "The helmsman approached him"; Gloss: "It is natural for a human being to trust more in others than in himself in dangers."

I respond: It must be said that to hope in someone is twofold — either as in a helper, or as in a savior. The first hope can be in a human being, the second cannot. — Or: to hope for the final reward — and this ought to be only in God; or for a temporal benefit — and this can be in a human being. If the first is placed in a human being, it merits a curse, according to [the Scripture].

DOUBT X.

Likewise, it is asked why he says concerning a human being: "Therefore, brother, I shall enjoy you in the Lord," rather than concerning a donkey or some other creature, since in all creatures the vestige of the divine goodness shines forth.

I respond: What causes one to enjoy something in God is not representation, but rather indwelling. Hence the converse expression is the proper one, namely: "I shall enjoy the Lord in you," while I know through works that God dwells in you; and the other is a figurative manner of speaking.

DOUBT XI.

Likewise, a question arises concerning this solution of Augustine, by which he resolves the aforementioned authority through that qualification "in the Lord." But in Ecclesiasticus chapter eight it is said simply: "Enjoy the great man." Likewise, in Deuteronomy chapter twenty-eight: "You will beget sons and daughters, and you will not enjoy them" — from this it implies that if they did not sin, they would enjoy them.

I respond: It must be said that the aforementioned solution is not general, but only applies to the proposed authority. And therefore one must have recourse to the solution given outside the letter in the questions — that since "enjoy" is used commonly and properly, in the aforementioned passages it is used only commonly.

DOUBT XII.

Likewise, what the Master says does not seem to hold: "For if he neither enjoys nor uses, etc." — because God loves Himself, yet does not use Himself, because He does not love Himself for the sake of something else; nor does He enjoy Himself, because where there is enjoyment, there is adherence and need, as Augustine says; but God lacks both, etc.

I respond: It must be said that enjoyment in its general account denotes a union of love, namely of the enjoyable with the one enjoying. Since therefore it is possible for something to be united to itself and to another, it is possible not only to enjoy another but also oneself; but where there is union to another, there is dependence, adherence, and need; where however it is union to oneself, if that thing is the highest good, there is no adherence or dependence or need, but utter sufficiency. But if the good is deficient, it stands in need; and hence it is that God alone perfectly enjoys Himself, while nothing other than Him can perfectly enjoy itself. For God alone is the highest good and loves Himself in enjoying Himself, and also loves other things in enjoying Himself: and thus neither God's enjoyment nor His use is accompanied by need, as our enjoyment and use are.

DOUBT XIII.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "Because He is good, we exist": therefore because He is good from eternity, we exist from eternity.

I respond: It must be said briefly that this is so because "good" states a cause. But there is a natural and necessary cause, and a voluntary cause. The divine goodness is the cause of our existing and our being good by way of will, not of nature; but a voluntary cause does not immediately produce its effect when it exists.

### Notes

- **"Expressed likeness" (expressa similitudo):** Bonaventure's characteristic term for the soul's relation to God — stronger than *vestigium* (trace) or generic *similitudo*, implying a direct imprint or stamp. Rendered "expressed likeness" to preserve this technical weight.
- **"Brings to an end" / "terminates" (finire, finitur):** The Latin *finire* serves double duty as both "to terminate/limit" and "to fulfill/complete." Rendered contextually as "bring to an end," "terminate," or "fulfill" depending on whether the sense is limiting or fulfilling — the argument in q. 2 turns precisely on this ambiguity with *infinitum*.
- **"Enjoys" / "enjoyment" (frui, fruitio):** Following standard Augustinian-scholastic usage. Bonaventure's distinction between *proprie* (proper) and *communiter* (common) senses of *frui* is central to the *Conclusio* and preserved explicitly in translation.
- **"Honest good" (bonum honestum):** Scholastic term from Cicero via Augustine for intrinsically valuable good (as opposed to *utile*, useful). Rendered "honest good" to preserve the technical term; the Cicero quotation is translated directly from Bonaventure's citation.
- **Anselm citation (Proslogion c. 26):** Bonaventure quotes Anselm on "entering into the joy" — a reversal of the intuitive reading of Matthew 25:21 ("enter into the joy of your Lord"), making the soul enter *into* joy rather than joy entering the soul. The translation preserves this nuance.
- **"Glides into the soul" (illabitur animae):** Technical term for divine immanence. *Illabi* carries the sense of flowing or slipping into something imperceptibly; "glides into" is more idiomatic than "flows into" while preserving the connotation.
- **Doubt XII resolution:** The argument that God enjoys Himself without need (*indigentia*) by being united to Himself rather than to another is a subtle distinction; the translation preserves the logical structure carefully.
