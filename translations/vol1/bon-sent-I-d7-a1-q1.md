QUESTION II.

Whether the power of generating is in the Son.

Second, it is inquired whether the power of generating is in the Son; and that it is, seems to be shown by authorities and by arguments.

1. First, as follows: Hilary says in the ninth book *On the Trinity*: "The Son has in himself by nature a nature fitted to beget": therefore the nature in the Son is naturally disposed to generate.

2. Likewise, Augustine in the book *Against Maximinus*: "For it was not that the Son could not generate"; but two negations are equivalent to one affirmation: therefore he was able to generate.

3. Likewise, it is shown by arguments as follows: to generate is more ordered toward the power of generating than toward the power of spirating; but the Father, in generating the Son, communicates to him the power of spirating: therefore, much more so, the power of generating.

4. Likewise, the Son is the most express image of the Father: therefore he imitates the Father to such a degree that no more similar image can be conceived; but if the Son is conceived as generating, he is conceived as imitating more fully: therefore if he imitates more than anyone can conceive, he does so also in the act of generating.

5. Likewise, a parallel case seems to show this: there is greater communication and diffusion in eternal light than in created light; but created light gives to its radiance the power of producing another, and so proceeding; and if it were of infinite power, it would produce infinite radiances: therefore, etc.

6. Likewise, in carnal generation a father is not said to have perfectly generated a son unless he gives him the power of generating: therefore by the same account, and indeed much more so, the Son will not be perfect unless he has the power for that which the Father is able to do; therefore the power to generate. Or if it is not so in divine things, then I ask the reason why, in the perfection of eternal generation, there is no communication of the power of generating such as there is in created generation.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. Augustine, in *Against Maximinus*, says "that it was not fitting for the Son to generate"; but if it was not fitting, neither was it appropriate; but everything unfitting in God is impossible: therefore it was impossible for the Son to generate: therefore the Son cannot generate: therefore he does not have the power of generating, because whoever has a power is powerful with respect to that power: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, Anselm in the *Proslogion*: "One who can do what does not befit him — the more he is able to do it, the more adversity and perversity can prevail against him": therefore, since it does not befit the Son to generate, if he were able to do so, that power would be in him a misery; but he has no misery: therefore neither does he have the power of generating.

### Notes

- **Chunk structure**: This chunk opens with the tail of Q.I's reply to objections (the four-fold taxonomy of divine names and the final responses) and then begins Q.II in full. Q.II asks the more pointed question arising from Q.I: even if the power of generating is a relational predicate proper to the Father, does the Son nonetheless possess it in some sense? The chunk ends mid-argument in the *sed contra* of Q.II.

- **Four-fold taxonomy of divine names**: A compact but important classificatory scheme —
  (1) *essentialia essentialiter dicta* (e.g., deity, majesty): absolutely common to all three persons;
  (2) *essentialia personaliter dicta* (e.g., power of generating): essentially grounded but personally expressed — this is Bonaventure's preferred characterization of *potentia generandi*;
  (3) *personalia personaliter dicta* (e.g., generating, generated): relational and proper;
  (4) *personalia essentialiter dicta* (e.g., sent, incarnate): personal in reference but expressed through an essential effect in the creature.
  This taxonomy resolves the apparent tension between Q.I's *Conclusio* (power of generating signifies relation *in recto*) and the Master's position (it signifies substance): both capture something true, depending on whether the compound phrase *potentia generandi* is read as a single term or analyzed into its components.

- **"the power to beget and the power to be begotten are the same in God"**: Response to arguments 2–3 of the *sed contra* in Q.I. The identity of active and passive potency in God blocks the inference from act to power that would work in creaturely contexts. This is an unusual claim that requires the context of divine simplicity and the identity of essence with all divine attributes.

- **Q.II argument 3 (*spirating* vs. *generating*)**: The argument draws on a common scholastic intuition: if the Father communicates the power of spirating (which belongs to Father and Son jointly) to the Son in the very act of generating him, why not also the power of generating? Bonaventure will need to show why the two powers are asymmetric.

- **Q.II argument 6 (perfect generation)**: A forceful argument from the logic of perfect communication: in creaturely generation, a father who does not give his son the capacity to reproduce has not fully communicated his nature. The challenge to the opposing view is explicit — Bonaventure anticipates a *quaero rationem* (I ask the reason) if the analogy is simply denied.

- **Anselm, *Proslogion***: The citation is to the chapter on divine omnipotence (*Proslogion* 7), where Anselm distinguishes genuine power from the power to do what is unfitting, which is actually a form of weakness or liability. The argument is used here against the Son's possession of the power of generating: if the Son had it but it was unfitting to exercise it, this would be a deficiency rather than a perfection.

The transition is seamless in the translation. Cross-reference apparatus at the scholion boundary has been omitted.
