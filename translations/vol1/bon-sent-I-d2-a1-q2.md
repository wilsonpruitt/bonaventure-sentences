QUESTION II.

Whether a plurality of persons is to be posited in God.

Second, it is asked whether a plurality of persons is to be posited in God. And that this is so is shown by supposing four things concerning God: the first is that in Him there is supreme beatitude; the second is supreme perfection; the third is supreme simplicity; the fourth is supreme primacy.

1. From the first supposition the argument runs thus: if there is in Him supreme beatitude — but wherever there is supreme beatitude, there is supreme goodness, supreme charity, and supreme joy. But if there is supreme goodness, since it belongs to goodness supremely to communicate itself, and this is especially so in producing from itself an equal and in giving its own being: therefore, etc. If there is supreme charity, since charity is not private love but is toward another: therefore it requires plurality. Likewise, if there is supreme joy, since "the possession of no good is joyful without a companion": therefore for supreme joy, fellowship is required, and thus plurality.

2. Likewise, from the second supposition thus: if there is in Him supreme perfection — but "it belongs to perfection to produce another such as itself in nature": therefore it is necessary that there be multiplication there; but this cannot be according to another essence: therefore it must be according to another person or supposit.

3. Likewise, from the third supposition thus: if there is in Him supreme simplicity — but it belongs to simplicity that a nature be in many, as is evident in the universal; but it is from a defect of simplicity that it is numbered in them: therefore if in God there is simplicity deficient in nothing, there will be an essence in many that is not numbered: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, from the fourth supposition thus: if there is in Him supreme primacy — but the more prior something is, the more fruitful it is and the more it is the principle of others: therefore just as the divine essence, because it is first, is the principle of other essences, so the person of the Father, since He is first because He is from no one, is a principle and has fruitfulness with respect to the persons; but fruitfulness in God with respect to God cannot exist except conjoined to act: therefore it is necessary that there be several persons.

On the contrary: 1. It seems that from the same suppositions the contrary could be argued, and thus the arguments and the conclusion are undermined. For if there is supreme beatitude there, since that which is blessed through its essence suffices to itself alone for beatitude: therefore it is not necessary to posit another person for beatitude or joy.

2. Likewise, against the second supposition the objection runs thus: if there is supreme perfection there, the essence is equally fully and perfectly in one person as in many. If therefore beyond perfection any addition is superfluous, plurality is superfluous; and if this is so, since in divine things nothing is superfluous, plurality is not in divine things.

3. Likewise, against the third argument thus: if there is supreme simplicity there, since plurality is opposed to simplicity and "opposites cannot obtain with respect to the same thing": therefore if God is one and in Him there is simplicity, then not plurality — since He is simple in every respect.

4. Likewise, against the fourth supposition thus: if there is supreme primacy there — therefore, since rest is in the first principle and rest is in unity: therefore primacy does not posit plurality but unity: therefore it seems that there is only one person.

CONCLUSION.

That a plurality of persons is to be posited in divine things is both taught by faith and shown to be fitting by arguments of congruence.

I respond: It must be said that in divine things a plurality of persons is to be posited, as faith declares and the foregoing arguments show, if one considers them without contradiction. For by reason of simplicity the essence is communicable and capable of being in many. By reason of primacy, a person is naturally suited to produce another from itself; and I here call primacy "innascibility," by reason of which, as the ancient opinion holds, there is fontal fullness in the Father for every emanation — and this will be made clear below. By reason of perfection it is apt and ready for this; by reason of beatitude and charity, voluntary. With these conditions in place, it is necessary to posit a plurality of persons.

1. To the contrary objection, therefore, that what is blessed through its essence suffices to itself alone, and therefore has no need of another, etc.: it must be said that it is true that it has no need; nor is another posited on account of need, nor as one who beatifies, but as one sharing in beatitude.

2. Similarly, to the objection that the deity is equally fully in one as in many: it must be said that even if it is equally fully present, it is nevertheless not so fully declared. And moreover, by the very fact that it is fully in the Father, it overflows into the other persons by the overflow of perfection.

3. To the objection that plurality is incompatible with simplicity: it must be said that there is a certain plurality by addition, and this is incompatible; and a certain plurality by origin, and this neither adds nor composes nor is incompatible with simplicity, but rather with solitude; and so it is in divine things, as will be made clear below.

4. To the objection that in the first there is rest: it must be said that just as in essences there is one essence that is first, from which the others are and to which they are ordered, so too in the persons there is one person from which the others are and to which they are ordered; and in that person there is the rest of origin, because that person is from no one — and this is the person of the Father. Hence Augustine appropriates unity to that person, saying: "In the Father, unity," etc.

QUESTION III.

Whether the number of the divine persons is infinite.

Next, in the third place, it is asked whether in the divine persons one ought to posit infinity. And that this is so is shown in the following way.

1. Because a creature is finite, and whatever is in it is actually finite: therefore, from the opposites, since the divine essence is infinite, whatever is in it is infinite: therefore, since in God there is a number of persons, it will be infinite; and thus the persons will be infinite.

2. Likewise, since infinity is twofold — virtual and numerical — virtual infinity is in God: therefore by equal reasoning numerical infinity is as well: therefore in God there are infinite persons.

### Notes

- **Opening fragment (Scholion reference list):** The text opens with a string of bibliographic cross-references in the Quaracchi editorial format (*Breviloq., Itiner., Alex. Hal.,* etc.). These are editorial apparatus, not Bonaventure's text, and have been omitted per the brief.
- **"Fontal fullness" (fontalis plenitudo):** A technical Bonaventurian term for the Father's inexhaustible originating capacity — He is the fount from which both Son and Spirit proceed. Rendered literally; this is a term of art Bonaventure uses throughout the Trinitarian questions.
- **"Innascibility" (innascibilitas):** Bonaventure identifies *primitas* (primacy) with *innascibilitas* — the property of being from no other principle. This is a standard scholastic term for the Father's distinctive personal property; retained untranslated.
- **"Plurality by addition / plurality by origin" (pluralitas per additionem / per originem):** The key distinction in reply to objection 3. Plurality by addition would imply composition and thus conflict with simplicity; plurality by origin (the persons proceeding from one another) involves no such addition. This is central to Bonaventure's Trinitarian theology.
- **"Overflow of perfection" (redundantia perfectionis):** *Redundare* carries the sense of overflowing or spilling over — the Father's perfection so fully realized that it necessarily communicates itself to the Son and Spirit. Rendered as "overflow" to preserve this image.
- **Seneca citation:** The phrase "the possession of no good is joyful without a companion" is from Seneca's *Epistulae ad Lucilium* I.6, cited here as the Quaracchi editors note. Translated directly from Bonaventure's quotation.
- **Question III:** The text ends mid-question (only the two opening arguments are present before the chunk ends). Translated as given; the response and solution to Question III are not in this chunk.
- **"Virtual and numerical infinity" (infinitas virtualis et numeralis):** A distinction between God's infinite power/perfection (virtual infinity) and an infinite number of instances (numerical infinity). The argument proposes these should stand or fall together — Bonaventure's reply (not yet in this chunk) will deny the inference.
