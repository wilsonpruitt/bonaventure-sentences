CONCLUSION.

That the number of the divine persons is finite is both held by faith and persuaded by reason.

I respond: It must be said that in the divine persons, with respect to number, one ought not to posit infinity but finitude.

The account of this is that numerical infinity is incompatible with perfection and order, because it arises from recession from unity or from its origin. Likewise, infinity of mass; and therefore neither is in God. But infinity of power arises from approach toward unity and origin; and therefore, since this belongs to perfection, it is to be posited in God — the other is not.

1. and 2. To the objection, therefore, that whatever is in God is infinite: it must be said that this is true — it is infinite, but not with just any infinity, but with that infinity by which God is infinite; and this is the infinity of immensity, and in this way the Trinity is infinite — not with numerical infinity, which does not belong to God.

3. To the objection that an infinite power, emanating infinitely, produces infinite things: it must be said that to divine power there belongs only the production of the perfect; and therefore there belongs to it only the production of such infinity as is consistent with supreme perfection. But this is not numerical infinity, and therefore it is not in God.

4. To the last objection, that it belongs to power to multiply itself: it must be said that it does not belong to power in every way, but it belongs to power to multiply itself perfectly; and therefore it does not follow that to multiply itself more is a greater perfection, unless "perfectly" is understood. But to multiply itself infinitely according to number is to do so imperfectly; and therefore this does not belong to God.

QUESTION IV.

Whether there are only three divine persons.

Fourth and finally, it is asked whether in the divine persons one ought to posit a trinity. And that there is not — that there is rather a duality — is shown thus:

1. The Father gives to the Son everything He is able to give; but one who gives everything he is able to give cannot give more: therefore He cannot produce another person either, since that would be a form of giving.

2. Likewise, the Father begets the Son as the Word, equal to Himself in all respects and expressing and articulating Him fully; but the multiplication of persons is for the declaration of power: therefore it seems superfluous to produce another.

3. Likewise, it is shown that there ought to be a quaternity there, by an argument from emanation. For since the Son emanates by generation and not by procession, therefore, even though He does not generate, He nonetheless produces by spirating; by the same reasoning it seems that the Holy Spirit, even though He does not produce by spirating, could generate or does generate, since He is not generated.

4. Likewise, since in divine things there is a twofold mode of producing — one by way of nature, and another by way of will — and since a complete account of production ought to be present there, it seems also that there ought to be a third mode of producing, by way of art. And if so, a fourth person would have to be posited according to this mode of producing.

On the contrary: that there is only a trinity there is shown from the suppositions made above — that in that Trinity there is beatitude, perfection, simplicity, and primacy.

1. From the first supposition it is shown thus: if there is supreme beatitude there, then there is supreme concord; therefore there is supreme kindredship and supreme charity. If there is a fourth person, it either proceeds from one, from two, or from three. ### Notes

- **Opening (Q. III continued):** Arguments 3 and 4 are the continuation of Question III from the previous chunk. Translated seamlessly from where the prior chunk ended.
- **"Infinity of immensity" (infinitas immensitatis):** Bonaventure's term for God's properly divine infinity — not quantitative but qualitative, denoting the absence of any limiting boundary or deficiency. Distinguished here from *infinitas numeralis* (numerical infinity) and *infinitas molis* (infinity of mass/extension). All three terms rendered literally to preserve the technical taxonomy.
- **"Recession from / approach toward unity" (recessus ab unitate / accessus ad unitatem):** The key structural contrast in the *Conclusio* of Question III. Numerical and material infinities represent flight from the unity and determinacy that characterize perfection; virtual/intensive infinity represents concentration toward the primordial unity. A characteristically Neoplatonic framework running through Bonaventure's Trinitarian theology.
- **"Supreme completion" (summa completio):** The fourth property cited against numerical infinity — rendered "supreme completion" rather than "fullness" to preserve the active sense of *completio* (bringing to completion, fulfilling). The argument is that infinite persons would make beatific knowledge impossible for any finite soul.
- **"Co-love" (condilectio):** A technical term from Richard of St. Victor's Trinitarian theology (*De Trinitate* III), denoting the love that two persons share toward a third. Bonaventure's argument here tracks Richard's famous argument that perfect love requires a third person as the shared object of mutual love. Rendered "co-love" to preserve this technical usage; the footnote confirms the Quaracchi editors' awareness of this source.
- **"Kindredship" / "germanitas":** *Germanitas* literally means the relationship of brothers born of the same parents — rendered "kindredship" to convey this sense of close consanguineal bond among the persons, which would be disrupted if any person shared less fully in the common procession.
- **Question IV ends mid-text:** The chunk ends after argument 2 of the "on the contrary" section, in the middle of Question IV. Translated as given; the resolution and objection responses are not in this chunk.
- **Aristotle citation (Physics II):** The phrase "every agent acts either by way of nature or by way of will" is cited as from the *Philosopher* — following Bonaventure's standard attribution. The objection (argument 4) proposes a third mode of divine production "by way of art," which Bonaventure will reject in the response (not yet translated) on the grounds that divine art is not distinct from divine will.
