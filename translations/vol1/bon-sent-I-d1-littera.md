# I Sent., d. 1, Littera Magistri (Caps. I–III)

## Distinction I

### Chapter 1.
**All teaching is either concerning things or concerning signs.**

Considering with diligent inquiry, again and again, the continuity of the Old and New Law, it has been disclosed to us, by God's prevenient grace, that the treatment of sacred Scripture turns principally upon things or signs. For as the eminent doctor Augustine says in his book *On Christian Doctrine*: "All teaching is either of things or of signs; but things also are learned through signs. Properly, however, those are called 'things' here which are not employed for signifying something; 'signs,' on the other hand, are those whose use lies in signifying." Now some of these [signs] are such that their whole use lies in signifying, not in justifying—that is, those which we use only for the sake of signifying something, as are some of the legal sacraments; others not only signify but also confer what helps inwardly, as the evangelical Sacraments. "From which it is clearly understood what things are here called signs: namely, those things which are employed for signifying something. Every sign, therefore, is also some thing; for what is no thing, as Augustine says in the same place, is altogether nothing. But the converse is not true: not every thing is a sign," because not every thing is employed for signifying something. And since the studious and modest speculation of theologians has turned its attention to these matters, it observes that divine Scripture retains the prescribed form in its teaching. For us, therefore, wishing under God's guidance to open some access for understanding divine things, there is matter to be discussed; and we shall speak first of things, and afterwards of signs.

### Chapter 2.
**Of the things which are to be enjoyed or used, and of those which enjoy and use.**

"This, then, is to be considered in things," as Augustine says in the same place, "that some things are to be enjoyed, others to be used, and others which enjoy and use. Those which are to be enjoyed make us blessed; by those which are to be used, while we tend toward blessedness, we are helped and, as it were, supported, so that we may arrive at and cling to those things which make us blessed. But the things which enjoy and use are ourselves, constituted as it were between the two," together with the angels and the saints. "To enjoy, moreover, is to cling in love to something for its own sake; but to use is to refer what comes into use toward obtaining that which is to be enjoyed—otherwise it is abuse, not use. For illicit use ought rather to be called abuse or abusion." "The things, therefore, which are to be enjoyed are the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. And yet this same Trinity is a kind of highest thing, common to all who enjoy it—if indeed it is to be called a 'thing' and not rather the cause of all things, if indeed it is a cause at all. For no name can easily be found which befits such excellence, except that it is better called 'this Trinity, one God.'" But the things which are to be used are the world and what is created in it. Whence Augustine in the same work: "This world is to be used, not enjoyed, so that through the things that are made, the invisible things of God may be understood and seen—that is, so that from temporal things we may grasp eternal ones." Again in the same place: "Among all things, those alone are to be enjoyed which are eternal and unchangeable; the rest are to be used, so that we may arrive at the full enjoyment of those." Whence Augustine in Book X of *On the Trinity*: "We enjoy things known, in which the will itself, delighting in them for their own sake, comes to rest; but we use those things which we refer to something else, which is to be enjoyed."

### Chapter 3.
**What it is to enjoy and to use.**

It is to be noted, however, that the same Augustine, in Book X of *On the Trinity*, taking "to use" and "to enjoy" otherwise than above, says: "To use is to take something into the power of the will; but to enjoy is to use with joy—no longer of hope, but now of the reality itself. Therefore everyone who enjoys, uses; for he takes something into the power of the will with the end of delight. But not everyone who uses, enjoys—if what he takes into the power of the will he seeks not for its own sake but for the sake of something else."

Notice that Augustine seems here to say that only those enjoy who rejoice in the reality itself, and no longer in hope; and so in this life we would seem not to enjoy but only to use, where we rejoice in hope—although it was said above that to enjoy is to cling in love to something for its own sake, in which manner many here cling to God even now.

These [statements], then, which seem to contradict each other, we resolve by saying this: that we enjoy both here and in the world to come, but there properly, perfectly, and fully—where we shall see by sight that which we enjoy; here, however, while we walk in hope, we do indeed enjoy, but not so fully. Whence Augustine in Book X of *On the Trinity*: "We enjoy things known, in which the will itself, delighting for their own sake, comes to rest." And the same in his book *On Christian Doctrine* says: "The angels, enjoying Him whom we too desire to enjoy, are already blessed; and insofar as we already enjoy in this life, whether through a glass or in a riddle, so much the more tolerably do we endure our pilgrimage, and the more ardently do we long to bring it to an end."

It can also be said that one who enjoys even in this life has not only the joy of hope, but also of the reality, because he already delights in what he loves, and so already holds the thing itself to some degree. It is clear, therefore, that we ought to enjoy God, not use Him. "For you enjoy that," as Augustine says, "by which you are made blessed and in which you place your hope, so that you may come to it." On this same point he says in *On Christian Doctrine*: "We say that we enjoy a thing which we love for its own sake, and that we should enjoy only that by which we are made blessed; but the rest is to be used." Frequently, however, "to enjoy" is said of using with delight. For when the thing that is loved is present, it brings delight with it. But if you pass through it and refer it to that where you must abide, you use it—and use it abusively—and are not properly said to enjoy. But if you cling fast and abide, placing there the end of your joy, then you are truly and properly said to enjoy. And this is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, in the highest and unchangeable Good.

Now since human beings, who enjoy and use other things, are themselves things, the question arises whether they ought to enjoy themselves, or use themselves, or both. To which Augustine replies in his book *On Christian Doctrine*: "If a human being is to be loved for his own sake, then we enjoy him; if for the sake of something else, then we use him. But it seems to me that he is to be loved for the sake of something else. For that which is to be loved for its own sake is that in which the blessed life consists, whose hope even at this time consoles us. But in a human being, hope is not to be placed, because *Cursed is he who does this*. Therefore, if you consider clearly, no one ought even to enjoy himself, because he ought not to love himself for his own sake, but for the sake of that which is to be enjoyed." But contrary to this it seems that the Apostle, speaking to Philemon, says: *Yes, brother, let me have joy of thee in the Lord*. Augustine resolves this thus: "If he had said merely *Let me have joy of thee* and had not added *in the Lord*, he would seem to have placed the end of love and of hope in him; but because he added this, he signified that he had placed his end in the Lord and that he enjoyed him there." "For when," as the same Augustine says, "you enjoy a human being in God, you are enjoying God rather than the human being."

But since God loves us, as Scripture frequently says (which much commends His love toward us), Augustine asks how He loves us—whether as one who uses, or as one who enjoys. And he proceeds thus: "If He enjoys us, He needs our good—which no sane person would say. For the Prophet says: *You have no need of my goods*. For all our good is either Himself or from Him; therefore He does not enjoy us, but uses us. For if He neither enjoys us nor uses us, I do not find how He loves us." "Yet He does not use us as we use other things. For we refer the things we use to this end, that we may enjoy the goodness of God; but God refers our use to His own goodness. For He has mercy on us for the sake of His own goodness; but we have mercy on one another for the sake of His goodness. He has mercy on us, that we may fully enjoy Him; but we have mercy on one another, that we may enjoy Him. For when we have mercy on someone and help him, we do so for his benefit and with a view to him—but our own benefit follows consequently, since God does not leave without reward the mercy we show to others. And this reward is the highest: that we may fully enjoy Him Himself." Again: "Because He is good, we exist; and insofar as we exist, we are good. Further, because He is also just, we are not with impunity evil; and insofar as we are evil, so much the less do we exist. That use, therefore, by which God uses us, is referred not to His benefit but to ours—and to His goodness only."

Here it must be considered whether the virtues are to be used or to be enjoyed. To some it seems that they are to be used and not enjoyed, and they confirm this by the authority of Augustine, who (as has been mentioned) says that nothing is to be enjoyed except the Trinity, that is, the highest and unchangeable Good. They also say that the virtues are not to be enjoyed for this reason: that they are not to be loved for their own sake, but for the sake of eternal blessedness. Now that which is to be enjoyed must be loved for its own sake. And they prove that the virtues are not to be loved for their own sake, but only for the sake of blessedness, by the authority of Augustine, who in Book XIII of *On the Trinity*, against certain people, says: "Perhaps the virtues, which we love only for the sake of blessedness, dare to persuade us not to love blessedness itself; but if they do this, we certainly cease even to love them, when we cease to love that for whose sake alone we loved them." Here Augustine seems to show in these words that the virtues are to be loved not for their own sake, but only for the sake of blessedness. And if this is so, then they are not to be enjoyed. To others, on the contrary, it seems that they are to be enjoyed, because they are to be sought and loved for their own sake. And this they confirm by the authority of Ambrose, who says, on that passage of the Epistle to the Galatians, *But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience*, etc.: "He does not call these 'works' but 'fruits,' because they are to be sought for their own sake." But if they are to be sought for their own sake, then they are to be loved for their own sake. We, however, wishing to remove the apparent conflict of these authorities, say this: that the virtues are to be sought and loved for their own sake, and yet for the sake of blessedness alone. For their own sake indeed they are to be loved, because they delight those who possess them with sincere and holy delight, and beget in them spiritual joy. Nevertheless, one must not come to a stop here, but go further. Let not the step of love pause here, nor let the end of love be here, but let this be referred to that highest Good, to which alone we must cling utterly, because that alone is to be loved for its own sake, and beyond it nothing is to be sought; for that is the supreme end. Therefore Augustine says that we love the virtues for the sake of blessedness alone—not that we do not love them for their own sake, but because the very fact that we love them is referred to that highest Good, to which alone we must cling, in which we must abide, and in which we must place the end of our joy. Hence the virtues are not to be enjoyed. But someone may say: to enjoy is to cling in love to something for its own sake, as has been said; therefore, if the virtues are to be loved for their own sake, they are also to be enjoyed. To which we reply: in that description, where it says *for its own sake*, it must be understood in this way only: that the thing is loved for its own sake *alone*, so that it is not referred to something else, but the end is placed in it—as Augustine shows above, saying: "If you cling fast and abide, placing there the end of your joy, then you are truly and properly said to enjoy. This is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, in the highest and unchangeable Good." Therefore the virtues are to be used, and through them the highest Good is to be enjoyed; and the same we say of the good will. Whence Augustine in Book X of *On the Trinity* says: "The will is that by which we enjoy." So also we enjoy through the virtues, not the virtues themselves—unless perhaps some virtue itself be God, as charity, concerning which we will treat later.

*Epilogue.* Of all, therefore, that has been said—since we have treated specifically of things—the sum is this: that some things are to be enjoyed, others are to be used, and others enjoy and use; and among those which are to be used, there are also certain things through which we enjoy, such as the virtues and the faculties of the soul, which are natural goods. Concerning all these, before we treat of signs, we must speak—and first concerning the things which are to be enjoyed, namely, the holy and undivided Trinity.

### Notes

Translation of Peter Lombard, *Sentences* Lib. I, Dist. I, Caps. I–III (Quaracchi ed., Opera Omnia S. Bonaventurae, Tomus I, pp. 26–28). This is the *littera Magistri*, the text of Lombard on which Bonaventure is commenting. The three chapters are composed almost entirely of quotations from Augustine, primarily *De doctrina christiana* I.2–4, 22, 31–37, and *De Trinitate* X.10–11 and XIII.8; with one quotation from Ambrose on Gal. 5:22.

Key terms (per project glossary): *uti* → to use; *frui* → to enjoy; *perfrui* → to fully enjoy; *res* → thing; *signum* → sign; *ratio* → account/ground (not translated as "reason" in theological contexts); *beata vita* → the blessed life.
