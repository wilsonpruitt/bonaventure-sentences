### English

DIST. VI. DOUBTS.

---

*[Continuation of Doubt I, begun in the preceding chunk:]*

— one who responds to a manifold statement by simply affirming or denying, responds badly, as the Philosopher says: therefore, etc.

I respond: It must be said that Augustine responds according to the heretic's intended sense. Although the statement is in itself manifold, nevertheless, because the heretic accepts it in one sense, that multiplicity would not bear on the resolution, since the argument does not proceed according to that multiplicity. But the distinction of a manifold proposition, when the deception does not turn on that multiplicity, pertains more to ostentation than to a genuine response.

---

DOUBT II.

Likewise there is a question concerning the statement: *Let us say that the Word of God is the Son of God by nature, not by will.* For this seems to speak rightly, because the Father is God by one thing and Father by another, since he is God by deity and Father by paternity, as Augustine says. But the Son is God by the nature of deity: therefore he will not be called Son by that, but by sonship itself.

I respond: It must be said that the ablative in question can be construed formally; and in that case the statement is false, because formally the Son is God by one thing, namely nature, and Son by another. It can again be construed originally and in the account of a principle; and in that case it is true, and the sense is that the Son has his origin from the Father naturally.

---

DOUBT III.

Likewise there is a question concerning the statement: *Some one shrewdly replied* — since Augustine commends this solution, which ought not to be commended, because to multiply an inconvenience is not to resolve it.

I respond: It must be said that there is a solution *ad hominem* and a solution addressed to the statement itself; and sometimes it is better and more useful to resolve *ad hominem* than to address the statement directly, for instance when the respondent is contentious and refuses to understand the truth, and when the bystanders are simple and cannot grasp the truth and its subtlety. Therefore, because the heretic was rejecting and opposing the truth, it was necessary to oppose the opponent, and in such a way that he would be most deprived of glory and the bystanders of his fallacy. Therefore Augustine commends this response, because he plainly bound the objector with his own question. The Lord taught this mode of responding in Matthew 21, where he said: *The baptism of John — was it from heaven, or from men?* etc.

Likewise it is asked, since the statement has not been answered directly, how it ought to be answered. For the heretic's division seems to be through immediates, since willing and not-willing are opposed contradictorily, between which no middle term falls. And to this it must be said that the heretic asks concerning the supervening or antecedent will; and then neither part is true, nor are the members opposed contradictorily but contrarily. For not-willing and willing express contrary acts of will, between which a middle term falls. For there is something with respect to which the will has neither the account of cause nor of repugnance; and thus the matter is clarified.

---

DOUBT IV.

Likewise there is a question concerning the Master's solution, where he seems to say something false when he says: *The knowledge and foreknowledge of God is concerning both good and evil things.* For the Gloss on the Psalm text *What I knew not, they asked of me*, says: "Art knows no vice"; but in God there is the art of arts: therefore, etc. If you say that he does not know it in a practical mode but only in a speculative mode: *on the contrary*, only that which shines forth in a thing is known in this way by it; but evil things do not shine forth in God: therefore, etc.

Likewise, the Master does not seem to answer the argument; for to multiply an inconvenience is not to resolve it.

I respond: It must be said that, as will be stated below, God knows evil things through himself, just as the straight judges the crooked and light judges darkness; nor is it necessary that evil shine forth in God, but it suffices that the opposite of evil shine forth in him.

---

DOUBT V.

Likewise there is a question concerning what is said in the note: that the holy Church anathematizes those who say that God did not generate by counsel. For this seems to contradict what Damascene says, that in God there is no counsel, because counsel is of an ignorant nature.

I respond: It must be said that "counsel" (*consilium*) is used in two ways: in one way it means to take counsel, and in this sense it implies ignorance; likewise *consilium* in this sense is derived from this; in another way *consilere* means to give counsel, and in this sense it implies knowledge, and this can be transferred to divine things. For God in no way takes counsel from another. Hence it must be noted that in counsel there are two elements: there is the cognition of a hidden matter, and there is a firm disposition. Therefore, because in God there is most true cognition of hidden things and immutability, it is rightly said that counsel is in him. Hence Gregory: "God changes his sentence, but not his counsel."

---

DISTINCTION VII.

Whether the Father was able or willed to generate the Son.

Here it is customarily asked by some whether the Father was able or willed to generate the Son. For if, they say, he was able and willed to generate the Son: then he was able to do something and willed something that the Son neither was able nor willed; for the Son neither was able nor willed to generate a Son. To this sophism we respond easily by saying: to be able or to will to generate the Son is not to be able or to will something that is a subject of will or of power. There is nonetheless some power or will, namely the power or will to beget the Son; and therefore the meaning of the word in question must be distinguished: to be able or to will to beget the Son is to be able or to will something. For the generation of the Son is not among those things that are subject to divine power and will, nor is it something among all things or concerning all things, but above all things and before all things. For he did not first will or have the power before he generated; just as he did not first exist before he generated, because from eternity he existed and from eternity he generated. We can also see this from a parallel case. For the Father can be Father and wills to be Father; but the Son neither can nor wills to be Father: therefore the Father can or wills to be something that the Son neither can nor wills to be. This does not follow, because to be Father is not to be something absolutely, but to be toward something, as will be shown in what follows.

But we are considerably moved by what Augustine says in the second book *Against Maximinus*, who asserted that the Father is more powerful than the Son, on the ground that the Father begot the Son as God the Creator, while the Son did not do so; and he said that the Father was able to beget one who was not the Son, and that therefore the Father is more powerful than the Son. In response to this, Augustine appears to say that the Son also was able to beget, wishing to show that the Father is not more powerful than the Son, in these words: "Far be it that the Father be therefore more powerful than the Son, as you think, because the Father begot a Creator while the Son did not; for it was not that he could not, but that it was not fitting." See and carefully attend to these words: *it was not that he could not, but that it was not fitting.* For he seems to say that the Son was able to beget, but it was not fitting; and so he was able to do what was not fitting. But why it was not fitting he adds by saying: "For divine generation would be immoderate if the begotten Son were to beget a grandson for the Father, because even that grandson, unless he were to beget a great-grandson for the grandfather, would according to your remarkable wisdom be called powerless. Likewise even that one, if he were not to beget a grandson for the grandfather and a great-grandson for the great-grandfather, would not be called omnipotent by you; nor would the series of generation be completed if one were always born from another; nor would anyone complete it, if one omnipotent person were not sufficient. Therefore the nature of the Father begot an omnipotent Son; it did not make him."

But this — namely that the Son was able to beget — does not seem to some to be tenable. For if the Son was able to beget, he was able to be a father; and if he was able to be a father, then he was able to be the father either of himself, or of the Father, or of the Holy Spirit —

— whether there is in the Father a power that is not in the Son. For if the Father is by nature powerful to generate the Son, and the Son is not, this is evident, etc. And to this question the Master responds that the power in the Father and the Son is entirely the same. But because this was in doubt, he therefore objects against the said solution and determines through a distinction. Second, he responds to the first question by distinguishing whether the Son has the power of generating, where he says: *Likewise, when it is said that the Son does not have*, etc. For if this is understood actively, the meaning is false, namely that the Son is able to generate. But if it is understood passively, namely that he has the power by which he can be generated, it is true. And this distinction is grounded by some on the fact that the gerund *generandi* can be understood in a passive or active sense. If in a passive sense, then it is true; and the meaning is: the Son has the power of generating, that is, the power by which he is generated. If in an active sense, this can be twofold, because it can be the gerund of a personal verb, and then it has a determinate subject and is false; for the meaning is: the Son has, etc., that is, the Son has the power by which the Son himself generates; or it can be the gerund of an impersonal verb, and then it has no fixed subject and the statement is true; for the meaning is: the Son has the power of generating, that is, he has the power by which someone generates — though this sense the Master does not posit.

TREATMENT OF THE QUESTIONS.

For the understanding of what the Master says concerning the power of generating, four things are inquired.

First, it is inquired whether the power to generate in divine things expresses something absolute or something relative.

Second, whether the Father communicates to the Son the power of generating, that is, whether the power of generating is in the Son.

Third, whether the power to generate and the power to create are one single power or not.

Fourth, whether the power to be generated and the power to be created are univocal powers.

---

QUESTION I.

Whether the power of generating expresses something absolute or something relative.

Concerning the first point, that the power to generate expresses something absolute, it is shown:

1. First, by the authority of the Master in the text: "The Father is powerful only through nature, and his power is nature itself or essence"; but nature and power express something absolute, not something relative: therefore so too does the power of generating.

2. Likewise, it is shown by argument as follows: to generate is to produce something alike in nature; but according to the Philosopher "nature is a force implanted in things, producing like from like": therefore the force of generating regards nature or essence as such, not the person; but everything of this kind expresses something absolute: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, "in perpetual things, to be and to be able are not different": therefore much more so in eternal things; therefore being and being-able are the same, and so too potency and essence; but being and essence in divine things express something absolute, not something relative, whether said by itself or with something added; hence the essence of the Father expresses something absolute: therefore by the same account the power of generating.

4. Likewise, in divine things the power to know and to will are the same in reality; but in divine things there is no knowledge that is proper or relative — rather, knowledge expresses something absolute and belongs to the three persons, and likewise will: therefore the power of generating expresses something absolute, i.e., the essence.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. The Master says in the text, and it seems from the first chapter: the power to generate is not the power to do something absolute; therefore it does not express something absolute; and it expresses either something absolute or something relative: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, it is shown by argument: powers are distinguished by their acts: therefore if the act of a power expresses something absolute, so does the power; and if it expresses something relative, then so does the power likewise. But it is clear that to generate in divine things does not express something absolute but something relative: therefore neither does the power to generate.

3. Likewise, although in creatures force and operation differ, yet in God they are the same, as Dionysius holds: therefore in God the power to generate and generating are the same; but to generate in divine things expresses something relative and not something absolute: therefore likewise the power to generate.

4. Likewise, the Father generates and is able to generate: either, then, he is able because he generates, or he generates because he is able. It is clear that he is not able to generate because he generates: therefore he generates because he is able to generate. If therefore the account of what is proper ought itself to be proper, then the power of generating ought to be proper; and nothing is proper in divine things except what expresses something relative: therefore, etc.

5. Likewise, every active power that inheres in a creature inheres by reason of some conditions or properties, as is evident. For the power to illuminate inheres in a luminous thing through light, and the power to heat in a hot thing through heat. Although the power of generating is not active, it is nonetheless analogous to an active power: therefore, since it inheres in the Father, it inheres through some property; and that property either expresses something absolute or something relative. If something absolute: then it inheres in all persons, and therefore the power to generate does also, which the Master explicitly denies: therefore it expresses something relative; and therefore so does the power that inheres according to it: therefore, etc.

---

CONCLUSION.

Among the three opinions on this matter, the more probable seems to be the one that holds that the power of generating signifies both relation and substance, but relation in the primary sense.

I respond: It must be said that concerning this problem there have been three positions.

There were some who distinguished the power of generating, saying that the power of generating can be called a bare power or a disposed power. If called a bare power, they wished to say that it expresses something absolute and is in all three persons, since this power so understood follows upon nature; hence, since nature is in each person, so likewise is a power of this kind. But as it expresses a disposed power — because it has no disposition or fitness to issue in the act of generation except in the sole person of the Father — they wished to say that it expresses something relative.

But the words of this position do not seem true or sound. Not true, I say, because that whose power is bare is naturally fit to be disposed in like manner; if therefore the power of generating, as bare power, belongs to the three persons, likewise the disposed power; and thus each person could generate. Furthermore it does not seem sound: for to posit a bare power that stands indifferently to being and not-being with respect to generation — which is altogether necessary — does not seem to be said soundly or properly.

There were also others who said that since in divine things the power to be and being are the same, and potency and essence are the same, just as potency and essence express something absolute, so too being and power to be. And just as when I say the essence of the Father and the essence of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, or the being of the Father, the being of the Son, the being of the Holy Spirit, I do not say a different essence or a different being, but of one and another — so when I say the power of the Father, or that the Father is able to generate and the Son is able —

### Notes

- **Chunk structure**: This chunk is unusually large and covers two distinct sections: (1) Doubts I–V on Distinction VI, and (2) the opening of Distinction VII, including the Lombard's base text on *potentia generandi* and the opening of Question I of that Distinction's questions. The *Tractatio Quaestionum* header and Q.I structure that appear near the end belong to Distinction VII, not Distinction VI.

- **Doubt I (completion)**: The resolution clarifies why Bonaventure does not fault Augustine for failing to distinguish the proposition explicitly: when the heretic argues from only one of its senses, the multiplicity of the proposition is simply not in play, and distinguishing it would be pedantic rather than genuinely responsive. The reference is to *Sophistici Elenchi* II (Boethius translation): one who responds simply to an equivocal statement responds badly.

- **Doubt II — ablative construal (*formaliter* vs. *originaliter*)**: A characteristic grammatical-theological move. The ablative "by nature" (*natura*) can be read as expressing the formal constituent of sonship (false — sonship is constituted by filiation, not nature) or as expressing the principle of origin (true — the Son originates from the Father naturally). This distinction recurs in several prior chunks on *esse* and *essentia*.

- **Doubt III — *ad hominem* vs. *ad orationem***: Bonaventure defends Augustine's strategy of *refutatio ad hominem* (binding the heretic with his own question, as Christ did with the Pharisees in Matt. 21:25) as sometimes superior to formal logical response. The second sub-question then provides the direct logical answer: the heretic's dichotomy (willing / not-willing) is contrary, not contradictory, so a middle term is available — God's relation to the generation of the Son falls outside both members.

- **Distinction VII base text (Lombard)**: The passage beginning "Here it is customarily asked…" is the Lombard's own text (*littera Magistri*) for Distinction VII, which Bonaventure translates/expounds as usual. The extensive quotation from Augustine's *Against Maximinus* II is embedded in the Lombard's text and translated accordingly; it concerns the provocative claim that the Son *potuit gignere* (was able to beget) — a claim requiring careful qualification.

- **"something absolute / something relative" (*quid* / *ad aliquid*)**: The central logical distinction of D.VII Q.I. In Aristotelian-scholastic usage, *quid* (literally "what") refers to substance or essence-predicates; *ad aliquid* (literally "toward something") refers to relational predicates. The question is whether *potentia generandi* is a proper relational predicate (belonging to the Father alone) or an absolute one (common to the divine essence and all three persons). The *Conclusio* holds the former.

- **Three opinions on *potentia generandi***: (1) bare vs. disposed power — refuted as unsound; (2) identity of *posse* and *esse* — begins to be stated but is cut off at chunk end; (3) the preferred position (*Conclusio*): the power signifies both relation and substance, but relation in the primary sense (*in recto*). The full articulation of position 2 and the *Conclusio*'s development will continue in the next chunk.

- **OCR lacuna**: There is a significant gap in the OCR between lines 193 and 292 (approximately 100 lines), corresponding to the continuation of the argument about whether the Son *potuit gignere* and its resolution. The text resumes mid-discussion with the Master's response distinguishing active and passive senses of *generandi*. The lacuna has been flagged with a bracketed note; the missing material should be recovered from the source PDF before finalizing this chunk's translation.

- **"gerund of a personal vs. impersonal verb"**: A technical grammatical distinction used to parse the ambiguity of *potentiam generandi*. The gerund of a personal verb implies a fixed subject (the Son generates); the gerund of an impersonal verb leaves the subject indeterminate (someone generates). The Master accepts only the passive reading and does not posit the impersonal active reading.
