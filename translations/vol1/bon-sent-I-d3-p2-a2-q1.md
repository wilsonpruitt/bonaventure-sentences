In this second assignment of the image — which is mind, knowledge, love — the trinity is found with respect to the substance of the soul and with respect to two habits of knowledge and love.

I respond: It must be said that this assignment differs from the preceding one, according to some, because the prior was in the powers, while this one is in the habits. And they respond to the objections through a distinction of mind. For "mind" is diversified according to four modes of reception. It is said in one way from *mene*, which means moon or deficiency — and in this sense it is said of the whole substance of the soul on account of the changes it undergoes. In a second way it is said from "measuring" — and in this sense it stands for the judging power, and this is how Damascene takes it, placing it among the cognitive powers. In a third way it is said from "excelling" — and in this sense it stands for the superior part of reason, and this is how Augustine frequently takes it. In a fourth way it is said from "remembering" — and in this sense it stands for memory both with respect to act and with respect to habit. They say therefore that in the assignment of this trinity "mind" stands for the habit of memory; but in the application, when Augustine says "mind knows itself and loves itself," it stands for the power of remembering.

But this does not seem to be fittingly said, because the application ought to correspond to the assignment. And moreover, since the proper act of mind, as it stands for memory, is to remember, that act ought to be mentioned; but Augustine in this assignment never makes mention of anything except two acts, namely knowing and loving — which are not of memory but of other powers.

It must therefore be responded that that trinity is not in the powers — because love and knowledge do not denote powers; nor is it in habits — because mind cannot stand for a habit, since it is taken as the agent; nor can it be in both powers and habits, because mind cannot stand for one power, since acts of two powers are assigned to it; nor similarly can it stand for several powers, because then there would be no trinity.

It remains, therefore, that one must necessarily hold that this trinity is found with respect to the substance of the soul — by reason of the mind knowing and loving itself — and with respect to habits — by reason of knowledge and love; and so there is a trinity, since the substance is one and the habits are two.

This assignment therefore differs from the preceding one — because the preceding was through uniformity in the powers with comparison to habits and acts, while this one is in substance and habits. It differs further in that the preceding was through the conversion of the soul toward God, while this one is through the conversion of the soul upon itself; and the image in the human being is not found in multiple ways in the same manner, as was said above. It differs also in that the preceding assignment of the image is more proper and fitting than this one. For properly speaking, the image consists in the unity of essence and the trinity of powers, according to which the soul is naturally suited to be sealed by that supreme Trinity with the image of likeness — which consists in grace and the theological virtues. Hence Augustine places this assignment first in his investigation, in order through it to arrive at that other one in which he completes his speculation. Hence this assignment is not proper in the same way as the other; and therefore the Master places it second as something non-principal.

1. To the objection, therefore, that habits are not coeval, etc.: it must be said that habit is threefold. For a power of the soul has a certain habit from acquisition, a certain one from innate disposition, and a third from its own origin. And this is clear, because a habit is that by which a power is ready for act; but a power is ready in these three ways — for example, our affective power has facility for loving another's good through acquired disposition, such as through virtue; for loving its own good through innate disposition; and for loving itself through its natural origin. For since it is inseparably united to itself, it is always disposed toward loving itself. Similarly, since our intellect is always present to itself, it is always disposed toward knowing itself. And so the objection concerning coevity is resolved — for with respect to such habits, coevity holds well.

2. To the objection that there ought to be three habits corresponding to three powers: it must be said that in this trinity there is no place for a habit of memory, because the trinity is found in the soul itself according as it is converted upon itself — and therefore the very substance of the soul takes the place of memory, and the very presence and offering by which the soul always presents itself to its own understanding takes the place of the habit and act of memory. And so it is clear that only the habits of two powers fall there; and therefore there is a trinity.

3. To the last objection concerning the Master's word: it must be said that "mind" there is understood not for the whole substance of the soul. And if it were understood otherwise, the Master's word would not be true. This is clear through Augustine, who, on the occasion of this image, throughout nearly the entire tenth book On the Trinity shows that the soul has an innate knowledge of itself, which is knowledge of its own substance. And furthermore, nothing in the soul knows and loves except the substance: therefore if mind were to stand for one power, it would not have those two acts — namely knowing and loving.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. I, continued objections 2–3):** The chunk opens with the tail of a prior question's objections — arguments 2 and 3 against the identification of the second Augustinian image-assignment (mind/knowledge/love) with a simple trio of habits. Translated seamlessly.
- **Four etymologies of "mens":** Bonaventure catalogs four derivations current in scholastic tradition: (1) from *mene* (moon/defect) = the whole mutable soul; (2) from *metiendo* (measuring) = the judging cognitive faculty (Damascene's usage); (3) from *eminendo* (excelling) = the superior part of reason (Augustine's frequent usage); (4) from *meminisse* (remembering) = memory in act and habit. The solution hinges on which sense is operative in Augustine's formula *mens novit se et diligit*. This taxonomy derives from the *De Spiritu et Anima* c. 11 and Isidore's *Etymologiae* XI.1.
- **The resolution — substance + two habits:** Bonaventure rejects two positions (the trinity is in powers alone; or in habits alone) and proposes that in the second Augustinian assignment, "mind" (*mens*) stands for the *substance* of the soul itself (not a power), while *notitia* (knowledge) and *amor* (love) stand for two consubstantial habits. This yields a trinity of one substance + two habits. The Scholion cites Bonaventure's own anecdote from the Prologue to *II Sentences* where he explicitly defends this interpretation against the charge of contradicting the Master.
- **Difference from the first assignment:** Bonaventure draws three contrasts between the two Augustinian image-assignments:
  (1) The first (memory/understanding/will) is in *powers* compared to habits/acts; the second (mind/knowledge/love) is in *substance* and habits.
  (2) The first is through conversion toward *God* (the most complete image); the second is through conversion of the soul upon *itself*.
  (3) The first is *more proper* — the image properly consists in the unity of essence and trinity of powers naturally suited to receive the seal of Trinitarian likeness through grace. The second is preliminary, as Augustine's own order of investigation confirms.
- **Threefold habit (ab acquisitione / ab innata dispositione / a sui ipsius origine):** The reply to objection 1 introduces a threefold taxonomy of habits to explain how the soul's self-knowledge and self-love can be "habitual" without being acquired: the third kind of habit — arising from the soul's own origin — is coeval with the soul, since the soul is always immediately present to itself and thus always disposed to know and love itself. This is the sense in which *notitia* and *amor* are described as "consubstantial" habits.
- **"The soul always understands itself":** The Scholion (omitted per brief) addresses whether the soul's self-knowledge is actual or merely habitual — a contested question among Bonaventure, Aquinas, Scotus, and Richard of Mediavilla. Bonaventure's position (noted by the Scholion) is that the intellect is always *in first act* ready to know itself, not necessarily *in second act* (actual intellection), which requires attention.
