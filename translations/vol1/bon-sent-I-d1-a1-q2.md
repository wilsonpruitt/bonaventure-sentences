For all things can be taken up into the faculty of the will for approving or rejecting; and this is made clear by Augustine in the book of the *Eighty-three Questions*: "We use things for taking up or rejecting unto health, for enduring unto patience, for ordering unto justice."

Likewise, Augustine in the tenth book of *On the Trinity*: "The will takes up the whole understanding, while I use everything that I understand"; but evil things are understood: therefore it is possible to use evil things.

Yet we use good things in one way and evil things in another. For the ground of ordering in good things is not only on the part of the one ordering, but also on the part of the thing ordered, because good things are by themselves usable and orderable, and this by their nature; but in evil things the ground of ordering is not on the part of the things ordered, but rather on the part of the one ordering; and therefore evil things are not said to be usable.

Hence note that, since to use is to take something up into the faculty of the will, this can occur in four ways: either into the faculty of the will as operating, as is the case with those things that properly pertain to human use; or into the faculty of the will as accepting, as is the love of neighbor and such things; or into the faculty of the will as enduring, as are the evils of punishment; or into the faculty of the will as rejecting, and in this way evils of fault are taken up and ordered. QUESTION III.
Whether only created good is to be used.

Third, having shown whether every created thing and everything other than God is to be used, the question is whether only created good is to be used. And that it is, seems to be shown.

1. "Every good is either an end or ordered to an end"; but only good that is ordered to an end is to be used, because the character of the useful is to lead to an end: therefore since only created good is ordered to an end, only created good is to be used.

2. Likewise, every good is either created or uncreated. But if it is possible to use uncreated good, it is possible to refer it to something else: either to something created or to something uncreated; but not to something uncreated, because there is only one uncreated thing: therefore to something created. But in this way the end is referred to the way and the cause to the effect; but this is misuse, not use: therefore only created good, or the creature, is to be used.

3. Likewise, Augustine in the book of the *Eighty-three Questions* divides the honorable against the useful: therefore nothing useful is honorable or conversely; but God is the honorable through essence: therefore God is not to be used.

4. Likewise, it seems that not only is God or uncreated good not to be used, but that to use him is always a mortal sin, because Augustine in the book of the *Eighty-three Questions* says: "It is the height of perversity to enjoy what is to be used and to use what is to be enjoyed"; but one who uses uncreated good uses what is to be enjoyed: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. It is possible to rightly serve God in view of a reward, because many are good hired servants, as Ambrose says; but it is not possible to serve God except by loving him: therefore it is possible to rightly love God in view of a reward; but one who loves in this way uses God, because he refers him to something else: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, the highest goodness stands in relation to love as the highest truth stands in relation to knowledge; but it is possible to know created truth more clearly without prejudice to the highest truth and without error: therefore similarly it is possible to love another goodness than the highest more ardently without prejudice to the highest goodness and without disorder: therefore it is possible by ordered love to love something else more than God: therefore it is possible to love God for the sake of something else in an ordered way, and so to use him.

3. Likewise, it seems that it is no sin when someone uses God for the sake of his own salvation, because "that whose end is good is itself good"; but the end of this use is good, namely our salvation: therefore the use of God is good: therefore it will not be a sin.

4. Likewise, use of God is either good — and in this case God is to be used; or it is evil — and in this case God is evil, because "that whose use is evil is itself evil"; but if God is good, his use is also good: therefore God is to be used.

CONCLUSIO.

Only created good is to be used, and to use uncreated good is to misuse.

I respond: It must be said that only created good is to be used, because if we use uncreated good, it is always misuse, and such misuse is a mortal sin by reason of perversion toward the end with delight, and by reason of the disorder of the will, which loves what it uses less than that for the sake of which it uses it.

1. To what is therefore objected — that it is possible to rightly serve God in view of a reward — it must be said that that reward is either God himself, as he said to Abraham in Genesis 15: *I am your reward*; and in this case one who looks to the reward does not use, because he does not refer to something else; or that reward is something else, and then one can look to it either as a cause that moves — and in this case he is a bad hired servant; or as a reason that persuades — and in this case he is a good one, and in this way he does not use.

2. To what is objected — that clearer knowledge of a creature does not prejudice the Creator, therefore, etc. — it must be said that the cases are not alike, because the clarity of knowledge is not in our power, but the ardor of affection is in our power; therefore it is required of us that we order this, not the former. Yet a certain act of knowledge is in our power, for instance the act of faith; and if someone assents to the first truth on account of something else, this rightly prejudices the truth, just as loving on account of something else prejudices goodness.

3. To what is objected — that the end of the use of God is good — it must be said that an end is called good in two ways: either with respect to substance or with respect to the intention of ending; and that proposition is understood with respect to the intention of ending, and this consists in right ordering.

4. To the last objection it must be said that that proposition is understood of things that have an ordering to the end, but not of the end itself. — Or it is understood of the natural and proper act of things themselves, which is more frequently elicited from the thing itself, not of the ordering to the end; and in this way the proposition does not apply to the case at hand.

ARTICLE II.
What enjoyment is.

Having treated of use and what is to be used, the question is subsequently raised concerning enjoyment and what is to be enjoyed, and first, what enjoyment is in its essence, namely whether it is an act of the will or of other powers.

QUESTION UNICA.
Whether enjoyment is an act of the will or of other powers.

1. That it is an act of the will seems to be shown by the first definition, which is: *Enjoyment is to cleave in love*, etc.; but love belongs to the will: therefore enjoyment similarly. If you say that love is taken there commonly, according as it is in any power with respect to its act — against this: the same is said in all the other definitions. Whence it is said in the next: "We enjoy things known, in which the will, delighted, comes to rest." But rest belongs to the will. And in another definition similarly: "Enjoyment is to use with joy." But joy properly pertains to the will.

2. Likewise, it is shown by argument, because in enjoyment we are delighted, according as the delightful is a difference of the good, since the good is the honorable, the conferring, and the delightful; but the good is the object of the will: therefore enjoyment belongs to the will alone.

3. Likewise, we enjoy that in which we come to rest; but rest regards the account of the end, "and the end regards the account of the good," and "the good is the object of the will": therefore enjoyment, which orders toward that, similarly.

On the contrary: 1. Every power desires to be united with its object, and once it has it, is delighted, if it knows it, and comes to rest: therefore motion with rest and delight belongs to all the powers: therefore since enjoyment is such, enjoyment will be in all, not only in the will.

2. Likewise, the absence of the beloved thing grieves the lover more than it delights him; for what delights him is that he sees and has it. If therefore the movement of enjoyment is with delight or is delight itself: therefore this is because he sees that in which the delight itself is; but vision regards the cognitive power: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: "This is the highest reward, that we fully enjoy him." But upon that word of the Psalm, *I will show him salvation*, etc., the Gloss says that "vision is the whole reward": therefore if enjoyment is the reward, enjoyment is vision essentially; but vision is in the cognitive power or in reason: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, by faith and hope we tend toward God: either therefore by enjoying or by using; but not by using, since God is not to be used: therefore by enjoying: therefore we enjoy by faith and hope; but faith is a habit of the cognitive reason; but whose is the habit, his is the act: therefore the act of enjoyment is an act of reason; the same seems to be true of the irascible power: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSIO.

Enjoyment taken essentially is an act of the will; taken dispositively it is also an act of the other powers.

I respond: According to some, enjoyment pertains to all the powers; and they say this because all the powers will be rewarded; they also say that in glory it will be more properly of reason, because it stands in a more immediate relation to delight.

But it seems it must be said otherwise, according to Augustine, namely that it is an act of the will itself. For since three definitions of enjoyment are given, all are given by reference to an act of the will, which is to be considered in three ways. In the first way, commonly, insofar as it expresses motion with delight; and in this way Augustine defines it: "Enjoyment is to use with joy." In the second way, insofar as it expresses motion with rest; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: "Enjoyment is to cleave in love to some thing for its own sake"; and in this way it is taken properly. In the third way it is taken insofar as it encompasses both, namely rest and delight; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in the tenth book of *On the Trinity*: "Enjoyment is to rest in things known, with the will delighted on account of them for their own sake," and in this way it is taken most properly.

Because therefore enjoyment according to every acceptation expresses delight or rest or both, and everything of this kind has the account of the good, and this is the object of the will: therefore speaking essentially, enjoyment is an act of the will. But because the will is neither delighted nor at rest except in what it knows either through faith or through vision, and in what it has either through hope or in reality, therefore the acts of the other powers dispose toward this, yet they are not enjoyment itself, speaking essentially.

### Notes

- **Chunk scope.** The text spans the conclusion of Q. II (responses to objections and their resolutions), the full body of Q. III, and the full body of Art. II, Q. Unica (on *frui*) up to the point where the *Respondeo* breaks off mid-sentence at "Yet this question." The translation follows the Latin exactly as supplied.

- **"Honorable / useful"** (*honestum / utile*). Bonaventure follows Augustine's division from the *Eighty-three Questions* Q. 30: the honorable is sought for its own sake, the useful for the sake of another. Rendered consistently as "honorable" and "useful" to preserve the technical opposition.

- **"Good hired servant / bad hired servant"** (*bonus/malus mercenarius*). The distinction drawn from Ambrose: one who serves God in view of reward is good if the reward functions as a persuasive reason (*ratio inducens*), bad if it functions as a moving cause (*causa movens*). The translation preserves the Ambrosian pastoral language.

- **"Through faith or through vision"** (*per fidem vel per speciem*). The Quaracchi apparatus notes the manuscript tradition strongly supports *speciem* (vision/sight) against the Vat. reading *spem* (hope), citing 2 Cor. 5:7 as the source of the locution. Translated accordingly.

- ***Propriissime* / *proprie* / *communiter*** (three modes of *frui*). Bonaventure's standard threefold gradation of terminological precision is preserved in the translation as "most properly," "properly," and "commonly."

- **"Irascible power"** (*irascibilis*). The Augustinian-Aristotelian faculty concerned with the arduous good, here raised as a parallel case to faith alongside reason. Translated as "irascible power" following standard scholastic English.

- **Q. Unica heading.** The article heading (*Articulus II. Quid sit frui*) and question heading (*Quaestio Unica. Utrum frui sit actus voluntatis*) appear in the Latin and are translated as given.
