*[Continuation of the Respondeo of Question II, begun in the preceding chunk:]*

Not only by reason of a defect of presence can something be thought not to exist, but also by reason of a defect of evidence — because it is not evident in itself, or not evident in the way of proof. But the truth of divine being is evident both in itself and in the way of proof. In itself, because just as we know principles insofar as we know the terms, and because the cause of the predicate is contained in the subject and therefore they are self-evident; so likewise in the matter at hand. For God or the highest truth is being itself, than which nothing better can be conceived: therefore it cannot not exist, nor can it be thought not to exist. For the predicate is contained in the subject. Nor does it have evidence only from itself, but also from proof, since every created truth and nature proves and concludes divine being: because if something is a being by participation and from another, then there is a being by essence and not from another. Every right understanding also proves and concludes it, because the cognition of God is impressed on every soul, and every cognition is through him. Every affirmative proposition likewise proves and concludes it; for every such proposition posits something; and with something posited, the true is posited; and with the true posited, truth is posited, which is the cause of every true thing. A negative proposition, however, does not conclude it except sophistically, as they say. Hence from the fact that nothing exists, or that no truth exists, it does not follow nor can it be inferred that truth exists. For the proposition "nothing exists" destroys every truth. And therefore no affirmation follows from it, and the following is false: "if nothing exists, it is true that nothing exists." And if it be said that every proposition implies its own content, this is true — but if nothing exists, there is no proposition and there is nothing. Augustine, moreover, does not make that argument by way of endorsing it, but by way of inquiry.

It must therefore be conceded that the truth of divine being is so great that it cannot with assent be thought not to exist, except by reason of the ignorance of the one thinking, who does not know what is meant by the name of God. And the arguments for this conclusion are to be conceded, although some of them are sophistical.

1. To the objection on the contrary side, from "the pernicious malice has so prevailed, etc.": it must be said that Damascene is speaking of a thinking that arises from blindness, which is evident from the very word he uses, namely "malice."

2. Similarly, to the objection concerning the idol: it must be said that the idolater errs because he does not know what God is; hence he does not think that God does not exist in the universal sense.

3. To the objection that, with God understood not to exist, other things can still be understood: it must be said that Boethius is speaking of an understanding by which we think something *per impossibile*, but without assenting to it.

4. To the objection that God most escapes our grasp: the response is clear, because what most escapes our grasp is *what he is*, but *that he is* is most evident.

5. To the objection asking what it means to say that divine truth cannot be thought not to exist: it must be said that this means no one can believe that God does not exist, so long as he makes use of reason. This does not hold of creatures; because although it is certain that one creature is present to one thing, it is not present to all things, because it does not have a power great enough to offer itself equally to all, as the first truth does.

6. To the objection that what can be expressed can also be thought: it must be said that "thinking" can be taken broadly for any act of the mind, whether erring or not, whether assenting or not — and in this sense that claim is true; or it can be taken for thinking with assent — and in this sense it is false, because one can contradict necessary truths with respect to external utterance, but not with respect to the interior, as the Philosopher says in the *Posterior Analytics*.

ARTICLE II.

On the immutability of God.

Consequently, in second place there is a question concerning the second property of divine being, namely immutability. Concerning which two things are inquired:

First, it is inquired whether immutability is in God.
Second, whether it is a property of God.

---

QUESTION I.

Whether God is immutable.

That immutability is in God is shown in the following manner.

1. Every change is either according to substance or according to accident; but God is not changed according to substance, because everything that changes in that way is corruptible, whereas God or the divine substance, since it has no beginning, is incorruptible: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, neither is he changed according to accident, because in God there is no accident: therefore there is no change in him.

3. Likewise, everything that is changed is first in potency before it is in act, and in such a thing act differs from potency; but God is pure act: therefore he is in no way changed.

### Notes

- **Chunk structure**: This chunk completes Q.II of D.VIII P.I A.I (on whether divine being can be thought not to exist), then transitions to Article II (on immutability), opening Q.I of Article II. The chunk breaks off at the beginning of Richard's argument (argument 4 of the *fundamenta* for Q.I of Article II).

- **Defect of evidence — self-evidence (*per se nota*)**: The completion of the *Respondeo* from the prior chunk argues that divine being is immune to both kinds of defect on the side of the knowable: defect of presence (already treated) and defect of evidence. The self-evidence argument mirrors the structure of Anselm's *Proslogion* 2: the predicate (existence, or more precisely, *non posse non esse*) is contained in the subject (the highest truth, being itself). This is Bonaventure's version of what would later be called the ontological argument, but framed epistemically rather than purely ontologically.

- **Negative propositions and Augustine's argument**: Bonaventure sharply qualifies the argument from Q.I argument 7 (Augustine's *Soliloquies* proof that "no truth exists" is self-refuting). He clarifies that Augustine was using it dialectically (*inquirendo*), not endorsing it as a demonstration. The proposition "nothing exists" is not self-refuting in the strong sense — it simply destroys all truth, including itself, and therefore yields no positive conclusion. This is a careful piece of logical analysis.

- **"exterior utterance vs. interior assent" (*exterius / interius*)**: The resolution of the final *sed contra* (argument 6) draws on the Aristotelian distinction from *Posterior Analytics* I.10: one can verbally contradict first principles (*exterius*) without being able to genuinely assent to their negation (*interius*). Bonaventure applies this to the proposition "God does not exist": it can be *expressed* but not genuinely *assented to* by a mind properly exercising reason.

- **Article II opens**: The transition to Article II on immutability follows immediately after the Q.II scholion. The four opening arguments follow the standard scholastic taxonomy of change: (1) substantial change — excluded because God is incorruptible; (2) accidental change — excluded because God has no accidents; (3) act/potency change — excluded because God is pure act; (4) Richard of St. Victor's argument (incomplete). The *Conclusio* will establish immutability as both a fact about God and a divine property.

- **"pure act" (*purus actus*)**: The Aristotelian-Thomistic formula here appears in Bonaventure's own argument, showing his use of the *actus purus* concept as common scholastic currency at this period, though Bonaventure will integrate it within his distinctive emphasis on divine self-sufficiency and the *fontalis plenitudo*.
