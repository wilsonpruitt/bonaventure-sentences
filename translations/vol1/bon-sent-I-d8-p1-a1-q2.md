*Truth, insofar as it is opposed to falsity, is found also in creatures according to its threefold relation to the subject it informs, to the principle it represents, and to the intellect it activates; but insofar as it is opposed to admixture, it is the exclusive property of God.*

I respond: It must be said that truth has a threefold relation. For it can be related to the subject it informs, to the principle it represents, and to the intellect it activates. In relation to the subject, truth is called the indivision of act and potency. In relation to the principle, truth is called the representation or imitation of supreme unity. In relation to the intellect, truth is called the ground of discerning.

And in all these relations truth can be taken in two ways: in one way, insofar as it is distinguished against falsity; in another way, insofar as it is distinguished against admixture, according to which the true is called pure and unmixed.

Insofar as truth is divided against falsity — which is the privation of indivision, imitation, and expression — since in a creature there is in some manner both indivision and imitation and expression, truth is in this way found not only in the Creator but also in the creature; and thus in this sense it is not assigned as a property of God.

In the other way, insofar as truth is divided against admixture or impurity, it is in this way in God alone. For in God alone there is pure indivision, unmixed with any diversity; in God alone there is pure imitation and likeness, unmixed with any dissimilitude; and in God alone there is the expression of light unmixed with obscurity. In the creature, by contrast, there is indivision along with a diversity of act and potency, and imitation along with dissimilitude; and further in it there is expression along with obscurity. And therefore in this sense truth is a property of divine being; and it is in this sense that the Master and Augustine and Jerome understand it. For they call that being true which has nothing of potentiality, nothing of vanity, nothing of non-being. And therefore in God there is no past and no future, which are in some manner non-beings.

1, 2, 5, 6. And in this way the first two authorities and arguments on the affirmative side proceed.

3. To the objection that there is no truth other than the eternal truth, since it alone causes understanding: it must be said that just as color is the object of sight and the motive of sight — yet not without the act of light — and differs from light itself; so likewise it must be said that created truth, although it cannot move without uncreated truth, is nonetheless in its own way a motive, and is a different truth from that one.

4. and 7. To the objection from Anselm, that all things are true by the first truth: it must be said that "true," by its original imposition, expresses a relation to the exemplar cause, just as "good" expresses a relation to the final cause. For just as something is called good by reason of order, so something is called true by reason of expression; and the ground of expressing belongs to the exemplar. Therefore, just as when it is said of created goods that they are good by uncreated goodness, "goodness" predicates the end in the ablative, not the form — because God's goodness is not the form of any created thing — so similarly, when it is said that all things are true by uncreated truth, the ablative expresses the exemplar formal cause. For all true things are and are naturally fitted to express themselves through the expression of that supreme light; which, if it were to cease to flow in, all other things would cease to be true. Therefore no created truth is true by essence, but by participation; and from this the last point is clear.

To the objection on the contrary side that truth is not a property: the response is clear, because —

4. To the objection that truth is the same as the divine essence: it must be said that the notion of a property in creatures involves three elements: first, that it belongs to it alone; second, that it is the ground of making it known; third, that it differs from that of which it is the property. The first two belong to perfection; the last, however, belongs to imperfection, since it excludes simplicity. And therefore truth is called a property of the divine essence, not because it differs from or inheres in the divine essence as an accident in a subject, but because it belongs to it alone and is the ground of knowing it. And this is why the converse does not hold — essence is not a property of truth — because essence is not the ground of making truth known, as truth is of making essence known.

But if you ask, since essence and truth signify the same thing, why the one is the ground of knowing the other and not conversely: some have said in response that, although essence and truth signify the same thing, truth is more the ground of knowing by reason of what it connotes. But this cannot stand, because truth connotes nothing. It must therefore be said that this is owing to the mode of signifying and understanding.

And it must be noted that these names can be spoken of in two ways: in one way by reason of what they name or signify; in another way by reason of that in which what they name is signified to be posited. Hence it is different to say "sense" and "sense of a human being"; because the first is said as common to the sense of human and beast, the second as proper to the human being. If therefore we take names of this kind in themselves, the account of essence is one thing and the account of truth is another, since essence expresses what a thing is, and truth expresses a condition of being. But when we draw essence and truth up to God, although they are the same, yet by reason of their general significations one is taken as a property of the other; and they are not synonyms there, nor are they synonymous names, but the account of subject and property remains according to the mode of signifying, and remains also according to the mode of understanding; because through essence in a creature we understand essence in the Creator, and through truth in a creature we understand uncreated truth. Hence just as created truth is a property of and ground for knowing created essence, so uncreated truth, according to the account of signifying and understanding, is the ground for knowing and understanding uncreated essence.

*The truth of divine being is such that it cannot be thought not to exist with assent, except by reason of a defect on the part of the knower, who does not know what God is; but on the part of the knowable there can be no defect, either of presence or of evidence, whether in itself or in the way of proof.*

I respond: It must be said that to think something not to exist is twofold. In one way, in the manner of what is false, as I think of the proposition "a man is a donkey"; and this kind of thinking is nothing other than understanding what the proposition says. In this way the truth of divine being can be thought not to exist.

In another way, something is thought not to exist with assent, as I think something not to exist and believe it not to exist; and in this way thinking something not to exist that does exist can arise either from a defect on the part of the knower or from a defect on the part of the knowable.

The defect on the part of the knower is blindness or ignorance, by reason of which, because one does not know the thing, one thinks it not to exist. Now there are two ways of thinking about any existing thing: whether it exists, and what it is.

Our intellect is deficient in its cognition of divine truth with respect to the cognition of *what it is*, but it is not deficient with respect to the cognition of *that it is*. Hence Hugh: "God so tempered his knowledge of himself in the human being from the beginning, that just as he could never be comprehended as to what he is, so he could never be unknown as to the fact that he is." Therefore, since our intellect never fails in its cognition of God as to whether he exists, neither can it be altogether ignorant that he exists, nor think him not to exist.

But since it is deficient in the cognition of *what he is*, it frequently thinks God to be what he is not — as an idol — or not to be what he is — as just. And since one who thinks God not to be what he is, such as just, consequently thinks him not to exist, therefore by reason of the defect of intellect God can be thought not to exist — or the highest truth can — yet not simply or universally, but as a consequence, just as one who denies that blessedness is in God thereby denies that he exists.

And it is in this way that the arguments proving that some intellect does or can think divine being not to exist proceed.

In another way, something can be thought not to exist by reason of a defect on the part of the knowable, and this kind of defect can be twofold: either a defect of presence, or a defect of evidence. A defect of presence occurs because something is not always, not everywhere, not everywhere wholly present. What is not always present, is sometimes present and sometimes not: and therefore it can at some time truly be thought not to exist. Similarly with what is not everywhere present, because by the same account that it can be thought not to exist here, it can be thought not to exist elsewhere. Similarly with what is present in part and absent in part.

### Notes

- **Chunk structure**: This chunk covers two questions of D.VIII, Part I, Article I. Q.I concerns whether truth (*veritas*) is a property (*proprietas*) of God's essence; Q.II concerns whether divine being can be thought not to exist (*cogitari non esse*). Both are only partially present — Q.I opens mid-argument, and Q.II breaks off mid-sentence in the *Respondeo*. The initial arguments for Q.I's affirmative side and Q.II's first *pro* argument are lost to OCR gaps.

- **Distinction VIII's structure**: Unlike prior distinctions, D.VIII is divided into two Parts (*Pars I* and *Pars II*), each with its own article. Part I treats the divine attributes of truth, immutability, and simplicity as properties of essence. This is reflected in the chunk ID (*p.1 a.1 q.1*) and will continue across several chunks.

- **Truth's threefold relation (*triplex comparatio*)**: The *Conclusio* of Q.I turns on this schema: truth as indivision of act and potency (relation to subject), truth as representation of supreme unity (relation to principle), truth as ground of discernment (relation to intellect). These three relations track the three transcendental accounts of truth: ontological (*veritas rei*), exemplar (*veritas in ordine ad causam*), and logical (*veritas in ordine ad intellectum*). The second *Conclusio* then adds the key distinction: against *falsity* (privation), truth is found in creatures too; against *admixture* (impurity), it belongs to God alone.

- **"admixture" (*permixtio*)**: The key term of the second *Conclusio*. God's truth is "pure and unmixed" — his indivision has no shadow of potentiality, his imitation no trace of dissimilitude, his expression no obscurity. This is distinct from the Anselmian argument in Q.II and is properly Bonaventure's own analysis.

- **Property without real distinction**: The reply to objection 4 (truth = essence, so neither can be the property of the other) develops a refined account of *proprietas* in God: the third criterion of property in creatures (that the property differs from its subject) is an imperfection arising from non-simplicity, and therefore does not apply to God. What remains is that truth belongs to essence alone (*soli convenit*) and is the ground of knowing it (*ratio innotescendi*) — two marks of perfection without requiring real distinction.

- **Mode of signifying (*modus significandi*)**: The resolution of why truth is the property of essence and not vice versa, even though they signify the same reality: the names retain their distinct *modi significandi* (truth = condition of being; essence = what-it-is) as derived from creaturely usage, and these modes of signification constitute an asymmetric explanatory relation even when the reality signified is identical. This is a careful piece of semantic theology.

- **Q.II — the Anselmian argument**: Arguments 1–7 for the affirmative all derive from variations of the Anselmian ontological intuition: that the first truth is self-confirming in every cognitive act. Arguments 5–7 are particularly sharp: every assertoric utterance, whether affirmative or negative, implicitly posits the first truth. The *sed contra* deploys empirical counter-instances (the fool of Psalm 13, the idolater) and logical objections.

- **"whether it exists / what it is" (*si est / quid est*)**: The standard distinction in scholastic epistemology between existence-cognition and essence-cognition. Bonaventure's resolution turns on the claim — via Hugh of St. Victor — that while the *quid est* of God is inaccessible to the human intellect, the *si est* (the bare fact of his existence) is naturally and indelibly known. Errors about God are always errors of the *quid est* kind, and these only *consequentially* involve denial of existence.
