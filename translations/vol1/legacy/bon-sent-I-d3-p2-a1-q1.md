For the retention of a species disposes one toward understanding, and understanding disposes one toward loving, if what is understood is good.

1. And from this the solution to the objection that in these powers there is no distinction by origin is clear.

2. To the objection that image is found according to exterior disposition: it must be said that there is an image of a corporeal and sensible thing — and this, since it offers itself to cognition through external features, has an image that represents according to exterior disposition. There is also an image of a spiritual thing, which is most interior to each thing and is known according as the power is gathered back to interior things; and this has an image that represents with respect to interior dispositions.

3. To the objection that memory concerns sensible things: it must be said that memory is taken in three ways — in one way as receptive and retentive of sensible and past things; in another way as retentive of past things, whether sensible or intelligible; and in a third way as retentive of species, abstracting from every temporal difference — namely of innate species. And in this third way it is part of the image; but the objection runs against the other first two ways. In the first way memory follows sense; in the second way it follows understanding and will; in the third way it precedes and corresponds to the Father.

4. To the objection that memory is not reformed: it must be said that on the contrary memory is reformed, both with respect to the state of the way and with respect to the state of the homeland — in the first by hope; and this is clear through Augustine's exposition on that word of Matthew twenty-two: "You shall love the Lord your God with your whole heart — that is, with intellect without error; with your whole soul — that is, with will without contradiction; with your whole mind — that is, with memory without forgetfulness." In the second state it is reformed with respect to retention; hence Bernard says that "God will be for the understanding a fullness of light, for the will a multitude of peace, for memory a continuity of eternity." Nor is it incongruous that memory, though it is first, is reformed through the last gift; because the order of reformation and deformation begins from the later, while the order of formation begins from the higher — and therefore reformation begins from the will and extends to memory.

5. To the objection that the image is in the power of knowing: it must be said that likeness denotes something gratuitous, and is therefore said to be in the will or in love through appropriation. The image, however, does not denote what is gratuitous, and is therefore said to be in the power of knowing.

Or alternatively, concerning the image: because the proper image is in the Son, and understanding — which is in the cognitive power — is appropriated to the Son, the image is therefore said to be in the power of knowing.

6. To the objection that the will is not equal to understanding: it must be said that the will, as it is taken commonly for willing and not-willing — both of which are acts of the will — is well equalized, insofar as it is said to be common to both, as Augustine says and is held in the text: that the will grasps other things when I use, etc. For whatever we remember or understand, we take into the capacity of the will for choosing or rejecting; and this is according to what it denotes as the common act of the will. But according to the special act, which is willing alone, it is not equalized; and the objection runs concerning this, because willing in this sense does not comprehend the whole will, just as remembering comprehends the whole memory and understanding the whole understanding.

QUESTION II.

Whether the image is found in these powers through their comparison to God.

Second, a question is asked concerning the powers in comparison to their object — namely whether the image is found in them through their comparison to God. And it seems that it is.

1. "The soul is an image precisely insofar as it is capable of God and can be a participant in Him," as Augustine says in the fourteenth book On the Trinity; but it is capable insofar as it pertains to the superior part: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, in the same work: "The image of that nature than which nothing is better is to be sought and found in us where our nature has nothing better"; but this is the superior part: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the same point seems evident by reason, because an image is so called insofar as it leads to the prototype; therefore since that prototype is God, the image is not found in these powers except insofar as they lead to God; but through these powers the human being is led to God, when through them he is converted toward Him: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, God is the object of the theological virtues, in which the image of reformation consists: therefore, since the object of both images is the same — because one leads toward and perfects the other — if God is the object of one, He is also the object of the other.

On the contrary: 1. Augustine in the ninth book On the Trinity assigns the image in mind, knowledge, and love, according as the soul remembers itself, understands itself, loves itself: therefore it seems that the image is found through the conversion of the soul upon itself.

2. Likewise, Augustine in the twelfth book On the Trinity, chapter four: "When we seek the Trinity in the nature of the human mind, we seek it in the whole mind, not separating the action of temporal things from the contemplation of eternal things so as to seek some third thing": therefore the trinity of the image is found according to the action of temporal things, and thus through conversion toward lower things.

3. Likewise, according as the soul is converted upon lower things or upon itself, in it there is equality and order and origin and everything that concurs to the account of image.

4. Likewise, the image is in sinners who are turned away from God, and even in those who cannot return at all, such as the damned: therefore the account of image is not found according to conversion toward God.

5. Likewise, for the perfect account of image it is necessary that the knower and the known be equalized, or the one expressing and the one expressed — for these two represent the Father and the Son; but in conversion toward God there is no such equalization: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSION.

The image is primarily and principally in the powers of the soul insofar as they are converted toward God; secondarily in them insofar as they are converted toward the soul itself; but insofar as they are converted toward lower things, the image is not in them — but the vestige of the Trinity.

I respond: For the understanding of the foregoing, three things must be presupposed in the account of image: first, the image is found according to express conformation to the thing imaged; second, that what is conformed to the image is consequently conformed to the original — hence one who sees the image of Peter consequently also sees Peter; third, that the soul according to its powers is rendered conformed to those things toward which it is converted, whether according to knowledge or according to love.

Since therefore, when the soul is converted toward God it is conformed to Him, and the image is found according to conformity: therefore the image of God consists in these powers insofar as they have God as their object.

Again, since the soul is the image of God, and what is conformed to the image is also conformed to the original, therefore the soul, insofar as it is converted upon itself, does not depart from conformity; and therefore the image consists in these powers insofar as they have the soul itself as their object.

But when it is converted toward lower creatures, it is conformed to them — in which there is not the image of God, but the vestige. Therefore the powers of the soul, insofar as they have lower things as their objects, depart from the account of image, because they depart from express conformity.

Although therefore in these powers, insofar as they are converted toward lower things, one can find a trinity and some conformity — as Augustine shows — yet since they fall short of express conformity, the account of image is not found in them. Hence Augustine investigates the trinity in all the powers of the soul — not because the image is in them, but in order to exclude them from the account of image. Hence, seeking the whole trinity in the whole soul, he seeks it in the superior and inferior part of reason and in sense. And this is what he says in that authority: "When we seek the Trinity in the nature of the human mind, we seek it in the whole" — he does not say "we seek the image." Hence Augustine says in the twelfth book On the Trinity, chapter four: "Although a trinity can be found in the inferior part of reason, the image cannot be found there."

It must be conceded, therefore, that the image consists in these powers insofar as they are converted toward the soul, but first and principally — as the first arguments show — insofar as they are converted toward God. Hence Augustine assigns the image in two ways: the first is in mind, knowledge, and love, according as the mind knows and loves itself; the second is in memory, understanding, and will. And at the end of his book he shows that the most complete account of image is found in comparison to God.

1. 2. 3. 4. To the objection concerning the aversion of sinners: it must be said that we do not speak of the image in these powers according to actual conversion, but according to aptitudinal conversion, which never abandons the powers — just as a human being is called capable of walking even if his feet are cut off, though he does not walk.

5. To the objection that in the image it is necessary for the knower and the known to be equalized: it must be said that it is not necessary for the known to be equalized to the knower by the equalization of thing to thing, but under the account of the knowable. Hence the known is in the understanding to the extent that it is represented by memory. That it should be simply equal is not required; yet it is equalized insofar as the soul is converted upon itself. Hence the account of image has something more in the conversion toward God, and something more in the conversion of the soul upon itself. In the conversion toward God it has more, because it has more of the account of beauty and conformity; in the conversion toward itself it has more of the account of consubstantiality and equality.

QUESTION III.

Whether memory, understanding, and will are the same in essence as the soul.

Third, a question is asked concerning the comparison of these powers to the soul or subject — namely whether they are the same in essence as the soul. And it seems they are.

1. Augustine says in the tenth book On the Trinity: "These three — memory, understanding, and will — are one mind, one life, one essence, and therefore one substance."

2. Likewise, Bernard in the Sermons on the Canticles: "I see three things in the soul — memory, understanding, and will — and these three are the soul itself." If you say that this is said causally, this amounts to nothing, because Augustine in On the Spirit and the Soul says that the soul "is, as it were, certain things that are its own — such as its powers — and certain things that are not its own — such as its virtues"; which if it were said causally, both could be so said.

3. Likewise, the Philosopher seems to want to say the same thing, because he says that the principle of being and the principle of operating are the same: therefore since the principle of being is the substantial form itself, the principle of operating will also be it; but the principle of operating is the power: therefore the power is the principle of being; but there is no principle of being except the substantial form in the human being: therefore power and substantial form are the same in substance.

4. Likewise, it is shown by reason: just as prime matter is naturally suited to receive all things in truth, so the soul according to likeness; but the potency of prime matter with respect to the forms to be received does not differ in essence from matter itself: therefore similarly it seems that the potency of the soul does not differ from the soul itself. Proof: for if it differed in essence, it would be either substance or accident. Not accident, because it precedes every form and every accident; if substance, then it is either matter or form. Furthermore, if it differed, there would have to be some matter capable of it.

5. Likewise, an accidental form is not simpler than a substantial form; but the power of operating does not differ from an accidental form — for the power of heating does not differ in essence from heat, nor the power of illuminating from light itself: therefore similarly it seems that the power of the soul does not differ from the soul itself.

6. Likewise, what is an accident is not substantial to any substance; but these powers are substantial: therefore they are not accidents of the soul: therefore they are substances — clearly none other than the soul itself. Proof of the minor: because in the human being the rational, sensible, and vegetative soul does not express a diversity of substances but of powers: therefore it is clear that in the human being these differences — vegetative, sensible, rational — are derived from the powers; but such differences are substantial: therefore the powers also are substantial: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. Augustine in the fifteenth book On the Trinity assigns the difference of the created image from the Trinity — that is, the uncreated one — namely that in that Trinity the one having is what is had, whereas here the one having is not what is had: therefore if the soul has three powers, it is not essentially those powers.

2. Likewise, Dionysius says that in every created thing these three differ: substance, power, and operation: therefore in the soul also substance and powers differ.

3. Likewise, Boethius: "In every created thing that by which it is and that which it is differ" — or "what it is and its being": therefore similarly, and indeed much more so, that which it can do and that by which it can do.

4. Likewise, it is shown by reason: those that differ in genus differ in essence, and one is not predicated of the other essentially; but the powers and the soul are such, because the soul is in the genus of substance while its powers are in the second species of quality — namely natural power or impotence: therefore, etc.

5. Likewise, those things of which one is outside the other differ essentially and substantially; but power goes out beyond substance, because it operates upon an object that is outside; but it is impossible for it to operate where it is not; if therefore power is where it operates, and it operates outside the substance of each thing: therefore it goes out beyond substance: therefore, etc.

6. Likewise, for this there is another argument: if the soul and its powers were the same in essence, then one would be multiplied only according to the multiplication of the other; and so, since there is only one soul, it would have only one power; but this is false: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSION.

The powers of the soul are substantial and are in the same genus by reduction as the soul; they are not, however, altogether the same in essence as the soul itself.

I respond: For the understanding of what has been said, it must be noted that natural power is spoken of in two ways. In one way, as it denotes the mode of the existence of natural power in a subject — according to which the subject is said to be easy or difficult at doing something; and in this sense natural power denotes a mode of quality and is generally in the second species of quality, as is evident when one speaks of a runner and a boxer, each of which denotes a facility that follows from the mode of existence of the power of walking or of resisting or acting in a subject. In another way natural power is said to be a power naturally proceeding from a subject. And this can be of two kinds. For some power proceeds from a substance along with an accident, such as the power of heating — for fire does not heat through its substance alone but through heat; and this power is not of a different genus than the quality from which it proceeds; hence the power of heating is in the same genus as heat. In another way, natural power is that which naturally and immediately proceeds from the substance, such as the power of generating with respect to the induction of the ultimate form; and this indeed is not of a different genus than the substance, but is reduced to the genus of substance as a substantial difference.

This is how it must be understood in the case of the soul's powers. For in one way the soul's powers can be named according to the first mode — as they denote a facility, which denotes the mode of existence of the power in a subject, such as cleverness and slowness; and these are in the second species of quality. In another way the powers can be named insofar as they denote the order of substance to act, which is mediated by some accidental property, such as the power of syllogizing — which is in the soul when it has the habit of syllogizing; and this is in the same genus as the science of syllogizing, namely in the first species of quality. Again, one can name the powers of the soul as they immediately proceed from the substance — as in these three: memory, understanding, and will. And this is clear, because with every accident set aside, once it is understood that the soul is a spiritual substance, by the very fact that it is present to itself and joined to itself, it has the power to remember and understand and love itself. Hence these powers are consubstantial to the soul and are in the same genus by reduction as the soul. Yet since they proceed from the soul — for a power comports itself in the manner of what proceeds — they are not altogether the same in essence, nor yet do they differ so as to belong to a different genus, but they are in the same genus by reduction.

And a sufficiently clear example can be given in a thing and its likeness. For a thing does not have so great an identity with its likeness that they are numerically one, nor so great a diversity that they differ in genus. For the likeness of Martin is not so distant from Martin as to differ entirely from him. And so the likeness of a thing is in the same genus by reduction as that of which it is a likeness. Because it proceeds, it differs — but it does not pass into another genus. And I speak of likeness according to the account of likeness, not of intention — that is, as it proceeds from the subject and does not withdraw, like a radiance from a light. The arguments proving that the soul is not its powers through essence are therefore to be conceded.

1. and 2. To the objection therefore from Augustine and Bernard that the soul is its powers: it must be said that there is no predication of accident of subject there, nor of the same thing of itself through essence, but a substantial or essential predication.

For this it must be noted that "essential" is said in four ways. In the first way, essential denotes that which expresses the entire essence of a thing, such as the individual species. In the second way, essential denotes what belongs to the essence and constitution of a thing, such as matter and form. In the third way, essential denotes what a thing cannot be or be understood to be without — such as those things in which the account of vestige is found, namely unity, truth, and goodness. ### Notes

- **Opening (Q. I, continued objections 3–6):** The chunk opens with the tail of the sixth objection from the prior chunk's Question I. Objections 3–6 concern memory (temporal, hence not suitable for the image), theological virtues (not in memory), will (book *On the Spirit and the Soul* assigns image only to cognitive power), and equality (will is not equally activated by all objects). Translated seamlessly.
- **"Memory in three senses" (memoria tripliciter):** Bonaventure's key distinction resolving the Aristotelian objection: (1) receptive memory of past sensible things (follows sense); (2) retentive memory of intelligibles as past (follows understanding and will); (3) retentive of innate species, abstracting from time (precedes, corresponds to the Father). Only the third sense belongs to the image. This threefold distinction is a Bonaventurian original within the scholastic tradition.
- **"Order of reformation / order of formation":** A subtle distinction in reply 4: deformation (sin) and reformation (grace) begin from the will (the last in the psychological order), since sin is first a disorder of love; but formation (natural constitution of the image) begins from memory (the first/deepest power). This is why hope reforms memory: both involve "retention" or "holding" (*tentio*). Bernard is cited (*In Cant.* Serm. II.5).
- **Question II — three conclusions:** Bonaventure gives a graduated answer: (1) the image is *primarily* in the powers insofar as they are converted toward God (the most complete image); (2) *secondarily* insofar as they are converted toward the soul itself (Augustine's first assignment, *De Trin.* IX); (3) *not at all* insofar as they are converted toward lower creatures (there only the vestige is found). This is the framework of the *Itinerarium* I–IV.
- **"Aptitudinal conversion" (conversio aptitudinalis):** Key term in reply 4 to the objection from sinners and the damned: the image is not destroyed by turning away from God in act (*conversio actualis*) because it consists in an aptitude or capacity that remains even in the damned. The image can be deformed but not destroyed. Illustrated by the analogy of one who is "capable of walking" even with amputated feet.
- **Question III — powers and the soul's essence:** Bonaventure's *Conclusio* — the powers are neither purely identical to the soul (as Augustine and Bernard's language might suggest) nor accidental qualities distinct in genus from it. They are *consubstantial* to the soul, in the same genus by reduction (*per reductionem*), proceeding from the soul as a radiance proceeds from light. The four senses of "essential" are a key technical passage: only in the weakest (fourth) sense are the powers "essential" to the soul — not constituting but required for perfect being.
