QUESTION III.

Whether the generation of the Son is according to the account of exemplarity.

Finally, it is inquired whether the generation of the Son from the Father is according to the account of exemplarity. And that it is, seems to be shown in the following manner.

1. On the text of the Psalm, *God has spoken once*, the Gloss for Augustine says: "that is, he begot the Son, in whom he disposed all things"; but the eternal disposition expresses the account of an exemplar: therefore if the Son proceeds as the Word, he proceeds by the mode of exemplarity.

2. Likewise, the generation of the Son from the Father is analogous to the production of knowledge from the mind; but knowledge proceeds from the mind according to the account of an exemplar, because the exemplar is the ground of knowing: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, what is image in the product corresponds to exemplar in the producer; for when the product expressly represents, it is called an image. Similarly, when the producer expressly represents, it is called an exemplar: therefore, since the Son proceeds as image, by the same account he proceeds by the mode of exemplarity.

4. Likewise, every cognitive principle of a thing to be produced produces according to the account of exemplifying; but the Father is the cognitive principle of the Son: therefore he produces the Son according to the account of an exemplar. If you say that this does not suffice, and that it is further necessary that the exemplar and the exemplified differ in form and nature: this amounts to nothing, because if a man, in generating a man, could generate such a one as he conceives, then that generation would be not only according to nature but also according to an exemplar. But the Father produced the Son wholly as he knew and willed: therefore, etc. If you say that even this does not suffice, but that it is further necessary that the exemplar precede: it is objected that if God had created the world from eternity — per impossibile — the world would nonetheless have been produced according to the account of exemplarity.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. Among lower things, agents acting by nature and agents acting by an exemplar are divided as opposites, just as nature and intellect are: therefore what is produced according to nature is not produced according to the account of an exemplar; but the Son is produced by the Father according to nature: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, everything that produces something according to the account of exemplarity produces according to the account of will; but the Father, as has been shown above, does not produce the Son through will: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, there is no form of a form, and therefore no exemplar of an exemplar; but the Son is the art and exemplar of all things: therefore he has no exemplar in the Father: therefore he does not proceed according to the account of exemplarity.

4. Likewise, what is in another according to truth is not in that other according to an exemplar; but the Son is in the Father according to truth: therefore not according to an exemplar; but what is not in another according to an exemplar does not proceed according to the account of an exemplar: therefore, etc.

---

*The Son proceeds from the Father as the ground of exemplifying, not as an exemplified thing from an exemplar.*

I respond: It must be said that, just as to proceed by the mode of will and liberality is twofold — for in one way that which is not liberality itself but which is made or given from liberality proceeds by the mode of liberality, and thus creatures proceed from God; and in another way that which is the ground of liberality, such as love, proceeds by this mode, and thus the Holy Spirit proceeds as love, which is the gift in which all gifts are given — so likewise to proceed by the mode of exemplarity is twofold. In one way, as an exemplified thing properly speaking; and thus the creature proceeds from God as an exemplified thing from an exemplar, and in this way the exemplar imports formal causality with respect to the exemplified. In the other way, something is said to proceed by the mode of exemplarity as the ground of exemplifying. And it is in this latter way that the Son himself appears to proceed, who is called the Word of the Father, through whom the Father not only speaks himself but also disposes all other things. Hence the Son is called by Augustine in the sixth book *On the Trinity* "the art full of all living reasons." And from this arises what is customarily said: that one who denies that ideas exist denies that the Son of God exists. And this mode of proceeding according to exemplarity is not repugnant to natural procession; on the contrary, it cannot but be natural. The other mode, however, is repugnant to natural procession; for it proceeds according to the command of the will, such that the producer and the produced differ as cause and caused, and the one is not in the other according to truth.

Hence the arguments adduced in support of this side proceed according to this way and conclude truly according to it; but according to the other way none of them concludes — indeed, all of them are to be answered by denial.

Those arguments, however, that are objected on the contrary side all proceed according to the other way, because they speak of proceeding by the mode of exemplarity in the sense of being an exemplified thing; which is evident, because they are drawn from the account of eternal disposition, of cognition, and of image. The last argument, however, does conclude that it proceeds by the mode of exemplarity as an exemplified thing, and for that reason it is to be answered by denial.

4. To the objection that every principle of a thing to be produced, if it has cognition, produces through an exemplar: it must be said that this is false, unless the account of cognition precedes in such a way that it has pre-cognition at least according to the account of cause with respect to effect; and this ordering requires essential or substantial diversity. For the exemplified, precisely as exemplified, is not in the exemplifying agent according to truth, but according to likeness — which likeness, since it is the ground of knowing and of exemplifying, is called an exemplar. The Son therefore proceeds according to the account of exemplarity, not as an exemplified thing through an exemplar, but as the very exemplar or ground of exemplifying all other things. And if you object that the exemplar is common to the whole Trinity, the Master of Auxerre responds to this in his question on the archetypal world or on ideas, that the idea or archetypal world is not merely appropriated to the Son, but is also proper to him.

Or alternatively it can be said that insofar as exemplar expresses the account of knowing, it is in this way common to the whole Trinity and appropriated to the Son, as wisdom is. But insofar as it further expresses the account of emanating, it is in this way proper to the Son; and it is in this sense that it is imported by the name Word, as will be more fully apparent below.

### Notes

- **"ground of exemplifying" (*ratio exemplandi*)**: The *Conclusio* hinges on the distinction between the Son as *exemplatum* (exemplified thing, as creatures are relative to God) and the Son as *ratio exemplandi* (the very ground or principle by which other things are exemplified). The Son is not a copy of a prior divine idea; he *is* the living art through which the Father conceives and disposes all things. *Ratio* here carries its full scholastic sense of formal ground or principle; "ground of exemplifying" is preferred over "reason for exemplifying" to capture the ontological rather than merely logical force.

- **"the art full of all living reasons" (*ars plena omnium rationum viventium*)**: Augustine, *De Trinitate* VI.10.11. The phrase is a touchstone for Bonaventure's exemplarism throughout the *Commentary*. "Art" (*ars*) here means the divine intellect as the seat of the eternal ideas (*rationes*) of all created things; the Son as Word is identified with this art.

- **"one who denies that ideas exist denies that the Son of God exists"**: A formula recurring in Bonaventurian and Franciscan theology, expressing the intimate link between the doctrine of divine exemplar ideas and the eternal generation of the Son. It is not directly quoted from a source here but treated as a *consuetum dici* (customary saying).

- **"Master of Auxerre" (*Altissiodorensis*)**: William of Auxerre (d. 1231), author of the *Summa Aurea*. The Quaracchi editors note the reference is to a passage in *Summa* I treating the archetypal world and ideas, distinguishing what is *appropriated* to the Son (insofar as it pertains to wisdom) from what is *proper* to him (insofar as he is Image and Word). The alternative solution at the end of the *Respondeo* (appropriation vs. proper predication of exemplar) reflects this distinction.

- **"per impossibile"**: Technical scholastic phrase for a counterfactual hypothesis introduced for the sake of argument; retained in Latin as is conventional in English philosophical writing, or rendered "per impossibile" without translation.

- **"denial" (*interemptio*)**: A technical term in scholastic disputation for the move of simply denying the premise of an opposing argument (as opposed to distinguishing or conceding it). Rendered "denial" consistently; the Latin *interemptio* (literally "cutting off") carries the sense of a direct refutation that removes the argument at its root.

- **Doubt I**: The chunk ends mid-sentence in Doubt I, which concerns Augustine's statement that the Father generates neither by will nor by necessity — a division that appears insufficiently exhaustive (why not "naturally / necessarily"?). The resolution will appear in the next chunk.
