5. Likewise, let the argument of Augustine be made, leading to an impossibility. If the Son has the power or ability to generate, suppose that he does generate; I ask the same of the second likewise; and if there is no stopping at the first, it is necessary to posit infinite sons, because by the same account that one does not stop at the first, neither does one stop at any: therefore, if there is a stopping point — since the contrary is repugnant to divine completeness — but by the same account that one stops at any point, one stops at the first: therefore the first does not have the power of generating.

6. Likewise, let the argument of Richard be made: if the Son were to generate another son, then since a begotten son is more closely related to the father than to the grandfather, accordingly there would not be there a supreme and equal connection: therefore neither a supreme and equal love: therefore neither perfect blessedness — all of which pious faith rejects as supremely impossible.

CONCLUSION.

*The Father does not communicate the power of generating to the Son, which is proven by two arguments.*

I respond: The opinion of some is that in the Son there is the power of generating, but he does not generate — and this not because he cannot, but because it does not befit him. And they posit a parallel in the production or creation of creatures. God is able to produce infinite creatures; but that he does not produce them is not owing to powerlessness but because it does not befit him; and so likewise in the matter at hand.

But on the contrary: suppose then that the power which is in the Son for generating is actualized; it is clear that the Son will generate an eternal God: therefore something will be able to begin to have being and to have eternal being; but this is impossible and unintelligible.

Therefore it must be said that the Father does not communicate to the Son the power to generate, for two reasons. One is that he cannot; the other is that he ought not.

The reason why he cannot is this: the fecundity for generating is in the Father because he is the principle, and he is the principle because he is the first. But it is impossible that what is first communicate its primacy to another. For there is a contradiction in terms in saying that a produced person is first. Therefore, since the fecundity for generating inhered in the Father by reason of his primacy, he cannot communicate it to the Son; and I believe this to be the proper reason for this, as will be apparent below in Distinction Twenty-Seven, where it will be treated why the Father generates.

Likewise, he ought not communicate it, because in the emanation of the divine persons there must be attended to both original distinction and most full communication: original distinction, because it is necessary that things which communicate in nature be distinguished either through matter or through origin. To be distinguished through matter is impossible for the persons; because where there is this mode of distinguishing, there is composition and variation and multiplication of forms, all of which are repugnant to God, who is simple and invariable and truly one. And therefore it is necessary that they be distinguished through origin or relation: therefore through generating and being generated: therefore if the Son is able to generate, he can also fail to be distinguished. If you say that there is still a distinction, because one is only Father, another only Son, and another Father and Son: then I object to you that the second son could generate by the same account as the first; and then there would be two of whom each would be both father and son.

1. To the objection on the contrary side from the authority of Hilary, that the Son has in himself a nature fitted to beget: it must be said that this expression is improper, and one must not argue from improper expressions but rather explain their impropriety, as the Master explained above. For the sense is: he has in himself the nature of the one who begets, that is, the nature of the Father.

2. To the objection that "it was not that he could not": it must be said that the Master explains this well, namely that it means he was not thereby powerless. Hence, according to the art, a distinction must be made when it is said that the Son could not generate: for this can be taken privatively, and then the sense is that he could not, that is, he was powerless; and then power is denied while aptitude is left, as it is said of one who has been maimed that he cannot walk, because he is by nature apt for walking yet cannot walk — this cannot be said of the Son in this mode, because he has no such aptitude for this. And it is in this sense that Augustine speaks. In another way, "not to be able" is taken negatively; and in this sense it must be conceded that the Son could not generate.

3. To the objection that the Father communicates to the Son the spirative power: the response is clear, because since the Son is generated, he cannot have primacy with respect to generation; but since he is not spirated, he is inspiratable; and therefore he was able to have primacy and fecundity with respect to that; and thus it is clear that the argument does not hold.

4. To the objection that the Son is the most express image, etc.: it must be said that the Son is called the most express image because he represents in all those things in which an image is naturally fitted to represent; but an image is so fitted to represent that it is nonetheless not itself the thing imaged — otherwise it would not be an image. But if the Son were to represent in the act of generating, he would already be the Father: and therefore it is neither intelligible nor possible for the Son to generate or to imitate the Father in generating.

5. To the objection concerning light and radiance: it must be said that the cases are not parallel, because light does not perfectly multiply itself in the first radiance, and therefore in many it accomplishes what it cannot in one.

6. Likewise in the case of a carnal son: hence one father generates several sons. Yet this argument is not the principal one, but rather the one stated above, because in lower things fecundity is not by reason of primacy but by reason of perfection. In God, however, because he is most perfect and first, it is not merely by reason of perfection — for then it would be in all persons and there could be no stopping point — but by reason of primacy. Likewise these arguments do not proceed by analogy but rather more by disanalogy.

### Notes

- **Chunk structure**: This chunk completes the *sed contra* of Q.II (arguments 3–6) and then gives the full *Respondeo* and replies. The *Conclusio* ("the Father does not communicate the power of generating to the Son") is established by two independent reasons: (1) he *cannot* — grounded in the logic of primacy; (2) he *ought not* — grounded in the requirements of original distinction and full communication. Only the first reason is fully articulated before the OCR lacuna; the conclusion of the second reason picks up at "that he would communicate that power."

- **"primacy" (*primitas*)**: A key technical term in Bonaventure's Trinitarian theology. The Father's fecundity for generating is inseparable from his being the *primum* — the unoriginated principle. Primacy cannot be communicated because the very notion of a "produced first" is self-contradictory (*oppositio in adiecto*). This ground is called the "proper reason" for why the Father alone generates, to be developed fully in D.27. Rendered "primacy" throughout rather than "being-first" to preserve the nominal form.

- **"original distinction" (*originalis distinctio*)**: The technical principle that divine persons must be distinguished by their relations of origin (generation, spiration), since material or formal distinction is inapplicable to a simple, invariable being. This is contrasted with "most full communication" (*plenissima communicatio*) — the two requirements must both be satisfied, and communicating the power of generating would violate the first.

- **"inspiratable" (*inspirabilis*)**: A coined scholastic term meaning "capable of being spirated" — i.e., able to be the terminus *a quo* of spiration rather than its product. Since the Son is not spirated (unlike the Holy Spirit), he can be a principle of spiration together with the Father. The argument is: the Son's position in the order of origin permits fecundity with respect to spiration but not with respect to generation, precisely because he is himself generated.

- **Privative vs. negative "cannot" (*non posse privative / negative*)**: A standard logical distinction applied here with theological precision. Taken *privatively*, "the Son cannot generate" would imply that the Son is *deprived* of a capacity he ought to have — which would be a deficiency. Taken *negatively*, it simply denies the capacity without implying any lack or imperfection. Bonaventure grants only the negative sense; Augustine's statement in *Against Maximinus* is read in the privative sense and reinterpreted.

- **Reply to argument 4 (image)**: The resolution draws on the formal definition of image: an image represents its original *without being identical to it*. If the Son generated, he would become a second Father, collapsing the distinction between image and original. The argument shows why the analogical structure of image-and-original actually *excludes* the Son from generating, contrary to what argument 4 claimed.

- **Reply to argument 6 (carnal generation)**: Bonaventure notes that the analogy from carnal generation fails in an important respect — in lower beings, fecundity is grounded in *perfection* (a sufficiently developed nature), not in *primacy*. Since in God the two coincide only in the Father (who is both most perfect and first), the analogy cannot be straightforwardly extended. The reply also notes that a father in created generation does generate multiple sons, which shows the analogy breaks down structurally.

- **OCR lacuna**: A brief gap appears in the text between the second reason's development and the conclusion "that he would communicate that power to the Son." The transition is recoverable in sense — the missing material likely completed the argument from *plenissima communicatio* — but should be verified against the source PDF.
