CONCLUSION.

The name "God," since it has the nature both of a common term and of a singular term, can properly suppose both for the nature and for the person.

I respond: It must be said that there are customarily two opinions on this matter.

One holds that the name "God" properly signifies the nature and supposes for the person — sometimes for one, as when it is said "God generates"; sometimes for two, as when it is said "God spirates"; sometimes for three, as when it is said "God is the Trinity." And the account given for this is that since it has many supposits, it has the nature of a common term; but a common term properly supposes for an individual, and not properly for the nature or form, unless it is drawn there — as when one says "man is a species." They say the same in the matter at hand. They further say that because all the supposits are in a unique supposition in that name, it can therefore suppose simultaneously for many persons — although this cannot be done by the term "man" or another common term; and in this way they resolve the objections.

The other opinion is that it properly supposes for the nature, as it signifies, and does not suppose for a person unless drawn by another — as when it is said "God generates"; and those who hold this say that when it is said "God is the Trinity," it there stands for the nature, not for the person; and one nature is three persons — therefore the expression is true. And the account of this position is that the name "God" has the nature of a discrete term, since it does not have a plural; therefore it signifies and properly supposes the same thing; since therefore it signifies the nature, it properly supposes for the nature.

But it is better to say both — because it has the nature of a common term and of a discrete term: of a common term by reason of the plurality of supposits, of a discrete term by reason of the non-multipliable form — and that it properly supposes both for the nature and for the person. Yet the arguments for each side conclude correctly in their respective way. But this cannot be found in any other term, and therefore neither can a similar mode of suppositing.

DOUBTS CONCERNING THE MASTER'S TEXT.

DOUBT I.

In this part a question arises concerning what he says: "God from God, Light from Light" — because since the preposition "from" (*de*) denotes transition and thus diversity and distinction, it seems that by equal reasoning and from equivalent terms the following are true: "God is other than God," "God is distinguished from God."

I respond: It must be said that there are two ways of importing distinction or diversity — namely as a mode, or as a thing; or as exercised, or as conceived. Since therefore prepositions import distinction as exercised, and distinction exists in divine things with respect to supposits, "from" (*de*) causes the term to stand for diverse supposits. Since therefore it posits the mode around that in which it exercises distinction — and such are the persons — the expression is true. But since the name "another" imports distinction as conceived, and similarly the verb "to distinguish" — these therefore simply posit distinction with respect to the term by reason of its form, and therefore they are false.

DOUBT II.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning the response that the Master gives at: "But what is further added — therefore He begot Himself as God, etc." — because the Master resolves it by denying the conclusion, and he seems to resolve it incorrectly. For since "same" and "diverse" sufficiently divide being, it seems to follow necessarily: He begot God — therefore either Himself or another.

I respond: It must be said that the Master, by upholding the first premise and denying the conclusion, indicates that the conclusion does not follow from the premises: and that it does not follow, he shows by adducing a counter-instance against that disjunction.

To the objection that "same" and "diverse" sufficiently divide being: it must be said that this is simply false. For a part is neither altogether the same as the whole nor simply diverse from it. Yet it has truth according to the same respect. Hence "if not the same as another, then diverse" is true according to that respect according to which it is not the same. But the Son is not the same as the Father in person, and therefore He is another in person; yet it does not follow that He is "another God" — because otherness in essence would be signified.

DOUBT III.

### Notes

- **Question IV conclusion — "God" supposes both for nature and person:** Bonaventure surveys two standard scholastic positions and proposes a third as superior. Position 1 (common in his tradition): "God" signifies nature, supposes for person(s) — with the unique feature that it can suppose simultaneously for multiple divine persons because all share a single supposition. Position 2: "God" properly supposes nature (because it lacks a plural, behaving like a discrete/singular term). Bonaventure's *via media*: "God" uniquely partakes of both modes — it is like a common term (multiple supposits) and like a singular term (non-multipliable form). Therefore it can properly suppose both for the nature and for one or more persons. This explains both "God generates" (for the Father) and "God is the Trinity" (for the nature or all three persons simultaneously) without requiring either position alone to carry all the cases.
- **"Unique supposition":** The phrase *in unica suppositione* in Position 1 expresses the point that the three persons, though numerically distinct, do not stand to the name "God" as three separate instances of a common form (as Peter, Paul, and John stand to "man"), but share one numerically identical deity. This is what allows "God" to suppose for all three simultaneously — something impossible for "man" (which cannot suppose for both Peter and Paul at once).
- **Doubt I — prepositions vs. nouns in importing distinction:** A key semantic distinction that resolves apparent inconsistencies in Trinitarian language. Prepositions like "from" (*de*, *ex*, *a*) import distinction *as exercised* — they simply operate within the real relational structure of divine persons without explicitly predicating otherness as a conceptual content. Names like *alius* ("another") and verbs like *distinguitur* ("is distinguished") import distinction *as conceived* — they place it explicitly as the signified content, thereby predicating otherness of the form (essence), which is false. Hence "God from God" is true while "God is other than God" is false.
- **Doubt II — "same and diverse" as exhaustive:** Bonaventure's elegant rebuttal of the disjunction argument (*se vel alium*): the Aristotelian principle that every being is either same or diverse (*Metaphysics* X) holds *within a given respect*, not absolutely. The Son is other than the Father in person, not in essence — so "other" holds in one respect and does not hold in another. This is not a violation of the exhaustive division but its proper application: otherness is always relative to a respect.
- **Doubt III — appositional and mediate construction (Priscian):** The Master's distinction — that "God the Father" can be read either appositionally/immediately (where "Father" qualifies "God" as one compound term, yielding "the Father-God") or mediately (where "God" and "is Father" are separate predications) — is here defended by appeal to Priscian's grammar of the understood copula. The text ends mid-argument as the application of the Priscianic rule is being developed.
