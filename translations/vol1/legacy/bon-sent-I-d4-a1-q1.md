### English

DIST. IV. THE SINGLE ARTICLE.

On the comparison of the name "God" to generation, and on its co-signification and supposition.

QUESTION I.

Whether the expression "God begot God" is to be conceded.

Concerning the first — that the expression "God begot God" is to be conceded:

1. It seems so from what is said in the Creed: "God from God" — but this is only through generation: therefore God is generated from God: therefore the expression is to be conceded: "God begot God."

2. Likewise, generation is with respect to something like in nature, hence "a human being generates a human being"; therefore if in divine things there is generation, something like in nature is produced: therefore since the Father is God, He generates only God: therefore this is true: "God generates God."

3. Likewise, whatever the Son has, He has either from Himself or from another; but He has deity, and not from Himself — for then He would be unbegotten: therefore He has it from another; but He has it only through generation, and He has deity only from one who has deity, and one who has deity is God: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. The name "God" signifies essence or substance, since it is a substantial term; but this is not conceded — on the contrary, it is false: "Essence generates essence": therefore similarly this: "God begot God."

2. Likewise, the name "God" either supposes for every person or determinately for some one. If determinately for some one: then its signification is restricted by something — and there is nothing other than this verb "begot" or "generates" that could restrict it. But the rule is that a term placed in the predicate does not restrict the term on the side of the subject by reason of signification: therefore it stands for every person. Therefore the expression does not seem to be true insofar as it is taken for the person of the Son: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the term "God," so far as it is concerned, supposes equally well for the Son as for the Father: therefore since it is the Son's not to generate, just as it is the Father's to generate, if "God generates" is true — for the Father — then by the same reasoning "God does not generate" is true — for the Son; if therefore the latter is not to be conceded, neither is the former.

4. Likewise, contradictorily opposed propositions are true of anything in disjunction, because of anything either the affirmation or the negation holds: therefore if God begot God, it was either the God who is the Father or the God who is not the Father. If the God who is the Father, then the begotten is the begetter; if the God who is not the Father — but what is implied can be inferred simply, as when it is said "the human being who does not run is disputing, therefore the human being does not run": therefore similarly, if He begot the God who is not the Father, God is not the Father; but if He is not the Father, He does not generate: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSION.

It is correctly said that God begot God — which is proved by four logical rules.

I respond: It must be said that the aforesaid expression, with the unity of essence preserved, is accepted by both masters and saints. For its understanding, four rules must be noted.

The first is that an abstract name is imposed upon a form and from a form — as "whiteness" is imposed upon whiteness itself and from the form of whiteness; but a concrete name is imposed from a form, but not upon the form, but upon the supposit — as "white" is imposed from the form of whiteness, but not upon the form, but upon the supposit, such as upon some white thing, a man or a swan.

The second rule is that a term having a multitude of supposits, taken without distribution, stands for that for which it renders the expression true — as when it is said "a man runs," the expression is true for the one who is running, if some one is running.

The third rule is that for a term having a non-multipliable form it makes no difference whether negation is placed before or after it. Hence it makes no difference to say "Peter does not run" and "not-Peter runs."

The fourth rule is this: a relative pronoun refers back to its antecedent under the same mode of supposition under which the antecedent preceded the relative — unless it makes a simple relation.

1. From the first rule the response to the first objection is clear. For since the term "God" is concrete — even though it is imposed from an essential form, namely deity — it is nonetheless imposed upon a person or supposit, just as "white" is imposed from the whiteness of a white thing; and therefore it supposes and renders the expression true for a person, not for the form. But the name "essence" or "deity" is abstract, and is therefore imposed upon a form and from a form, like "whiteness," and therefore it signifies and supposes essence. And therefore this is false: "Essence generates essence," because generation belongs to the supposit, not to the form; but this is true: "God begot God" — for the person.

2. From the second rule the response to the second objection is clear. For although the signification of a term is not restricted, it nonetheless stands for that for which the expression is true — as "a man runs" stands for Peter or John, if for one of them the expression is true; but the term is not restricted to that one. And this is clear, because if distribution is added, it will confuse that term for all; but if it were restricted, it would confuse only for those to whom the restriction extended. Hence it is one thing for a term to be restricted to one, and another for it to render an expression true for one.

3. From the third rule the solution to the third objection is clear. For although "God generates God" is true for the Father, yet "God does not generate God" is not true for the Son. For since the term "God" denotes a non-multipliable form, it makes no difference to place the negation before or after it; and therefore since a prefixed negation removes the predicate altogether from the subject, so too a postfixed negation removes the predicate entirely from the term "God" when it is said "God does not generate" — because it removes it from every supposit. And therefore it has a different mode of supposition in the affirmative than in the negative: in the affirmative the expression was true for the Father; but in the negative there can be no truth, because the negation removes the predicate entirely.

4. From the fourth rule the fourth objection is clear. For since a relative pronoun has entirely the supposition of its antecedent, and the term "God" in the predicate supposes for the God who is begotten, the relative pronoun refers back for that one. And thus the sense is: "God begot God — who, the begotten God, is the Father, or is not the Father"; and this is true for the negative — nor is it permissible to infer "therefore God is not the Father," because the supposition is changed; on the contrary there is a fallacy of figure of speech there.

QUESTION II.

Whether the expression "God generates another God" can be admitted.

Second, it is asked whether the unity of essence admits this expression: "God generates another God." And it seems it does not.

1. Anselm in On the Procession of the Holy Spirit: "When we say 'God from God,' we do not understand another God from another God, but the same God from Himself."

2. Likewise, if He begot another God: but where there is one and another, there are two: therefore if He begot another, there are two gods.

3. Likewise, "another" denotes otherness in general: therefore since a general term is specified by what is adjoined, the name "God" specifies it; but if it specifies it, it specifies it by reason of form: therefore otherness in form is denoted: therefore such an expression is not to be admitted, since there is no otherness in form there.

On the contrary: 1. Generation implies distinction; but distinction implies some otherness: therefore so does generation; therefore if this is true — "God begot God" — then this follows consequently: "God is distinguished from God" or "He begot another God." If you say this does not follow, because "to generate" implies distinction as a mode, while "to be distinguished" or "to be another" implies it as a thing — on the contrary: from a consequence as a mode there follows a conclusion as a thing; hence if "if a human being is, an animal is" is true, from "human being" there follows "animal." And furthermore, it is established that the distinction of generation is not only on the part of the one understanding, but also on the part of the thing: therefore to that distinction as exercised there corresponds a real distinction.

2. Likewise, the affirmative is false: "The begotten God is the generating God": therefore the negative is true: "The begotten God is not the generating God." But as the affirmative signifies identity, so the negative signifies diversity: therefore just as the generating and the begotten God can be compared to one another through a negation, so through otherness: therefore this is true: "God begot another God."

3. Likewise, "the Father or God begot another" — this is established as true: therefore either another God or another non-God; but not another non-God: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, "another" is a term of masculine gender; but a term of masculine gender stands for a person in partitive terms: therefore "another" denotes personal otherness; but this is true: "God begot another God in person": therefore this is simply true: "God begot another God."

CONCLUSION.

In the strict sense of the words, the expression "God begot another God" is false.

1. I respond: It must be said that this expression is customarily distinguished: "God begot another God" — because "another" can be taken adjectively, and in this sense it posits otherness with respect to the form of the term "God," and in this sense the expression is false; it can also be taken substantively, as if it were substantivized; and then it is an appositional construction, like "animal man," and the sense in this way is: "God begot another God" — that is, He begot another who is God; and in this sense the expression is true.

But although this distinction has its place in theological expressions by reason of a certain proper mode of speaking, nevertheless insofar as one would proceed according to the force of the expression, it would not need to be distinguished — because an adjective joined to a substantive, as "white man," is not said to be substantivized, nor is there said to be an appositional construction there, especially since such a construction applies to the less common with respect to the more common. Hence since the name "another" is an adjective having a substantive joined to it, in the aforesaid expression it posits otherness with respect to it by reason of supposit and form.

And therefore, if we wish to proceed technically, such an expression must be judged false. For this reason, for the understanding of the aforesaid expression, a general rule must be noted: there is no place for distinction where the union does not arise from diverse causes — for example, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are united in the name "God" not from diverse causes or by reason of diverse things, but by reason of one deity or essence. A union from diverse causes is when in "human being," Peter and John are united by reason of diverse humanities, because the humanity of Peter is one and that of John is another.

And according to this rule, from the opposite side, another rule must be taken: there is no place for complete union where simultaneously with the union there is distinction — as the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are united in one deity or essence, but have distinction by reason of the plurality of persons.

According to this, it must be observed that in divine things certain terms import distinction only, certain others complete union, and certain others occupy a middle position. Since therefore the generating God and the begotten God, although they are personally distinct, are united in deity from the same cause — namely one deity — terms importing distinction simply are not admitted. Hence this is not admitted: "God is distinguished from God" — similarly: "God begot another God." Likewise, by reason of the distinction, terms importing complete union in supposit and form are not admitted: hence this is not admitted: "God begot Himself." But those that occupy a middle position are admitted, such as the verb "generates" — because it denotes distinction in person together with unity of essence. Similarly this is conceded: "The Father generates another self." Hence Augustine to Maximin: "The Father begot another self"; and similarly in the commentary On John: "The Father sending the Son sent another self." And from this it is clear that from a simple verb there does not follow a term importing distinction; and so the first point is clear.

2. To the second objection concerning negation: I readily concede that there is otherness; but it does not follow that there can be said to be otherness in the essence or form of deity. Hence it does not follow: "The begotten is other than the generating one, therefore another God" — because the supposition of the term "God" is changed.

### Notes

- **Four logical rules:** Bonaventure structures the entire first question around four rules drawn from medieval terminist logic (Peter of Spain's *Summulae Logicales* and related tradition): (1) abstract vs. concrete names — abstracts impose on forms, concretes impose on supposits; (2) a common term without distribution stands for that for which it renders an expression true; (3) for a term with a non-multipliable form (like "God" — only one divine essence), negation placed before or after makes no difference; (4) a relative pronoun refers back under the same mode of supposition as its antecedent. The Scholion (omitted) provides a substantial glossary of the relevant logical vocabulary.
- **"Deus" as concrete term:** The pivot of Question I. "God" (*Deus*) is a concrete name imposed *from* the essential form (deity) but *upon* the supposit (a person). Hence it supposes for a person, not for the essence — making "God begot God" true (for the person of the Father generating the person of the Son), while "Essence generates essence" is false (since generation belongs to supposits, not forms). This distinction between abstract/concrete and their differing suppositions is the foundation of the entire Trinitarian language analysis.
- **Non-multipliable form:** The third rule applies because the divine essence is numerically singular — there is only one deity, unlike "humanity" (which is numerically diverse in Peter and John). Hence "God does not generate" is not merely true for the Son (while "God generates" is true for the Father) — the negation removes the predicate from every supposit simultaneously. A valid exception for Trinitarian predication.
- **Question II — "another God":** The *Conclusio* is that this expression is false in the strict grammatical/logical sense. The customary theological distinction (taking "another" substantively as appositional) is pastoral accommodation rather than strict linguistic analysis. Bonaventure provides two complementary rules: (a) no distinction-term (*alius Deus*, *distinguitur a Deo*) is admitted when the union is from a single cause; (b) no complete-union-term (*genuit se*) is admitted when there is simultaneously distinction. Only middle-position terms (*generat*, *alterum se*) are admitted, as they express distinction in person with unity in essence.
- **Augustine's "alterum se":** The phrase *genuit de se alterum se* (from the Letter to Maximin) is the patristic warrant for the middle-position formulation. "Another self" (*alter se*) captures personal distinction (another) with essential identity (self) — exactly the middle that Bonaventure's analysis requires.
