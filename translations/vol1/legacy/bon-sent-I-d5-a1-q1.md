1. To the first objection, therefore — that essence and person do not have diverse properties: it must be said that diversity of account is twofold in divine things. In one way, through having diverse properties; and this induces distinction, and in this sense they differ in account and one is not predicated of the other — as Father and Son. In another way, through having a property and not having it; and this does not induce distinction nor cause one not to be predicated of the other; but it does cause something to be said of one that is not said of the other — as is clear in the case of Peter and "man": Peter is an individual, "man" is not; and yet "man" is truly predicated of Peter.

To the objection that the supreme opposition is that of affirmation and negation: it must be said that this is true where the negation posits nothing — as between "something" and "nothing"; but where it posits extremes, the opposition can be minimal and arises from however small a difference whether of thing or of account; and so it does not suffice for distinction.

2. To the second objection — that a predicate supposes for the subject: it can be said in one way that — just as in lower things there is predication according to substance, as "man is an animal," and predication according to account, as "animal is a genus"; and the predicate supposes for that of which it is predicated according to substance; but once predication according to substance is changed to predication according to account, there is a fallacy of accident — as the inference does not hold: "animal is a genus, man is an animal, therefore man is a genus" — so similarly they say in divine things: since there are both thing and account there, predication according to substance exists there; and with that preserved, whatever is said of the predicate is also said of the subject; but once predication according to substance is changed to predication according to account, there is a fallacy of accident; and such is the case here: "the Father is the divine essence, the Father is the Father, therefore the divine essence is the Father."

But this analogy does not seem fitting, because in divine things account is predicated of essence — hence this is true: "essence is paternity" and "essence is generation"; which indeed is not found in lower things. And therefore it must be said differently: in divine things there is a twofold mode of predication — by identity and by inherence. By identity — as when it is said "essence is the Father"; by inherence or denomination — as adjectives and verbs do. Predication by identity exists in divine things by reason of supreme simplicity, which does not allow a person to be less simple than the essence; and since this does not exist in creatures, predication by complete identity is not found in them, except when the same is asserted of itself — as when it is said "humanity is humanity"; but every proper predication is by inherence, because nothing is altogether simple — hence this is false: "humanity is animality." In predication by inherence, however, the term signifies one thing and supposes for another, because it signifies the common form and supposes for the inferior; and in such predication it is true that what is predicated of another supposes for it. But in predication by identity the term signifies and supposes the same thing. Hence in this case the sense is that what is signified by the term "Father" is the same as what is signified by the term "essence," and therefore paternity is essence; and because the name "essence" does not signify a person, it therefore does not suppose for it, since essence is never predicated of a person except by predication by identity.

3. To the third objection concerning proper attribute (*passio*), subject, and similar things: it must be said that in divine things certain terms are substantial — they enclose within themselves the thing around which they posit the form imported by them — such as the name "Father"; and such terms can be predicated by identity, as when it is said "essence is the Father" — that is, the one who is the Father. Certain others are entirely in adjacency — such as verbs and names retained adjectively, like "generates," "begotten," and "born"; and these posit their thing around those of which they are predicated, and are therefore predicated only by inherence. And therefore "to generate" posits the distinction it imports around the essence when said of it; and therefore this is false: "the essence generates." Yet this is true: "the essence is generation." And when one passes from predication by identity to predication by inherence, there can be a fallacy of accident there. Therefore what is said — "of whatever the subject is predicated, its proper attribute is also predicated" — this has a counter-instance where the proper attribute is foreign to that of which the subject is predicated — as the intention "species," even though it is a proper attribute of "man," and "man" is said of Peter, is not said of Peter that it is a species.

And if you object that in divine things the account of what is foreign does not obtain, because accident does not occur there: it must be said that even though there is no foreignness or diversity with respect to thing, there is nonetheless foreignness with respect to account or mode of predication — which is threefold in divine things, as will be made clear below. Hence just as there is a fallacy of accident here: "essence is a person, but a person is distinguished — therefore so is essence," so also in the matter at hand.

4. To the last objection — concerning the expression "essence is the Father of the Son": Praepositivus distinguishes that "Father" can posit its thing or relation imported by it around the subject itself — which is the essence — and then the expression is false, for the sense is that the essence is referred to the Son. Or it can enclose within itself the thing of its substantive, so that the sense is: "essence is the Father of the Son" — that is, the essence is that one who is referred to the Son; and in this way the expression is true, and the argument does not hold: "therefore the Son is the Son of the essence." On the contrary, there is a fallacy of accident there. For just as the following does not hold: "the Son is referred to the one who is the Father, and that one is the essence, therefore He is referred to or distinguished from the essence" — so neither does it hold in the matter at hand.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. I replies 1–4):** The chunk opens with Bonaventure's replies to the four *contra* objections of Question I (whether essence generates). These replies complete the *Conclusio* section — that "essence generates" is an entirely improper expression and must be denied. Translated seamlessly.
- **Twofold diversity of account in divine things (reply 1):** Bonaventure's key distinction: (a) diversity through having *different* properties (paternity, filiation, passive spiration) → induces real distinction, so Father is not predicated of Son; (b) diversity through one having a property and the other not → does not induce real distinction, so the terms can still be mutually predicated. The analogy with "Peter is an individual / man is not" illustrates: "man" is truly predicated of Peter despite not sharing "individual" with him. This prevents the argument that because essence lacks the property of generating (which the Father has), essence and Father must be more distinct than Father and Son.
- **Twofold predication: by identity / by inherence (reply 2):** Bonaventure's most important structural contribution of this question. He rejects Praepositivus's distinction (predication according to substance vs. according to account) as insufficient because in divine things account is itself predicated of essence ("essence is generation" is true). He replaces it with: *predication by identity* (abstract terms: "essence is paternity") — the term signifies and supposes the same thing; *predication by inherence/denomination* (concrete terms, verbs, adjectives: "God generates") — the term signifies one thing (form) and supposes for another (supposit). The inference rule "what is said of the predicate is said of the subject" holds within one mode but yields a fallacy of accident when modes are mixed.
- **Twofold specification of terms in divine things (reply 3):** Further refinement: (a) substantial terms that enclose their thing (*Pater*, *Filius*, *Spiritus Sanctus*) — these can be predicated by identity ("essence is the Father"); (b) adjectival terms entirely in adjacency (*generat*, *genitus*, *natus*) — these are predicated only by inherence and posit distinction around the essence. Hence "essence is generation" (identity) is true; "essence generates" (inherence) is false.
- **Reply 4 — Praepositivus's distinction:** Bonaventure attributes to Praepositivus (Peter of Corbeil, d. 1222, Chancellor of Paris) a further distinction for the expression "essence is the Father of the Son": "Father" can be read with its relational content posited around the essence (false — essence is related to the Son) or with that content enclosed within the term's substantial content (true — the essence is the one who is related to the Son). This is sound but does not help the objector's inference ("therefore the Son is Son of the essence") — a fallacy of accident occurs when one moves from the identity-mode to the inherence-mode.
- **Question II begins:** The second question of Article I opens — whether "generated from the substance of the Father" is a proper expression. This question concerns the abstract term as *terminus a quo* (principle) of generation, rather than as *terminus ad quem* (term) or *principium quod* (agent).
