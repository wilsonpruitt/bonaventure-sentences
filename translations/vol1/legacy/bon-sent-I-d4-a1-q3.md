DIST. IV. THE SINGLE ARTICLE. QUESTION III. (continued from prior chunk)

3. To the third objection — either another God or another non-God: I say that the division is not exhaustive, because there is no contradiction unless the negation is taken with respect to the whole. For in the expression "He begot another God," two things are said — namely that He begot God, and that the other is another in deity; and therefore for the contradiction to be properly derived, it is necessary that the negation be borne over the whole. Hence just as the following does not hold when pointing to a black-robed monk who is white by nature: "He is either a white monk or not a white monk" — because both are false — similarly this is to be understood in the matter at hand. Nor does this hold: "He is God and He is other: therefore He is another God" — on the contrary, this is a fallacy of accident, as in the inference: "He is good and he is a lute-player: therefore he is a good lute-player."

4. To the last objection: it must be said that "another," although it is of masculine gender, nonetheless — because it posits something with respect to the substantive — draws its supposition from the substantive; and therefore it is not taken personally except insofar as it is substantivized, just as the name "one" — hence just as when "one God" is said, "one" denotes substantial unity, so when "another God" is said, "another" denotes substantial otherness. And the reason for this comes not only from the fact that it is an adjective, but because it is a general term that is specified by what is adjoined.

QUESTION III.

Whether it can be said grammatically according to the rules of grammar: "several gods."

Third, it is asked whether the name "God" grammatically signifies the plural number — or whether it can properly be said "several gods." And it seems it can:

1. Because according to the Philosopher, concepts are the same for all, even though the words differ; but modes of signifying follow modes of understanding: therefore since among the Hebrews it is properly said *Elohim* — which is equivalent to "gods" — the same can be said among us.

2. Likewise, as the Philosopher holds, what is true presupposes what is grammatically well-formed; hence "of Cato it is or it is not" signifies neither truth nor falsehood; but this is true: "there are not several gods": therefore it is grammatically well-formed: therefore so is this: "several gods exist" — because negation does not remove ill-formedness.

3. Likewise, just as "God" belongs to one alone, so "principle of creatures" belongs to one alone; but although it is falsely said "several principles," it is nonetheless said in a grammatically well-formed way: therefore by equal reasoning we can say "several gods."

4. Likewise, the name "phoenix" has only one individual; yet it is grammatically well-formed to say "several phoenixes." If you say that the individual is multiplied through succession of time — I raise the same point against you concerning the name "sun," which is multiplied at no time; and yet it is grammatically well-formed to say "several suns": therefore also "several gods."

On the contrary: 1. Every name that has a plural is a common appellative name — for proper names are not pluralized, since one does not say "several Peters" or "several Johns"; but the name "God" is not an appellative name, because it does not signify a multipliable form: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, the name "God" is properly the proper name of the divine nature; but no such name is pluralized: therefore, etc.

CONCLUSION.

The name "God," properly speaking, does not have a plural.

I respond: For the understanding of what has been said, it must be noted that the name "God" is used in three ways — namely, by titular use, by adoptive use, and by nature. In the first two ways it is pluralized; hence the Apostle in the first letter to the Corinthians eight: "For indeed there are many gods and many lords"; but in the third way it is not, because in this sense the name "God" signifies the divine nature together with conditions, the collection of which it is impossible to find in another; and therefore just as a proper name, according to the rules of the art, does not have a plural, so neither does this name "God."

1. To the objection, therefore, that among the Hebrews it has a plural — namely *Elohim*: it must be said that modes of signifying do not only follow the diverse general modes of understanding, but also modes of expression. And since the Hebrews have articles and modes of expression that are diverse from ours — which we do not have — they therefore can have it, but we cannot.

2. To the objection that what is true presupposes what is grammatically well-formed: it must be said that there are two kinds of grammatical ill-formedness. One arises from a discohesion of accidents — as in "of Cato it is"; the other arises from a discohesion of concepts — as when one says "several Peters." The first ill-formedness removes truth and falsehood; the second ill-formedness has falsity conjoined with it; and therefore, since through negation falsity is removed, and the saints prefer to speak truly rather than to preserve propriety of speech and speak less truly, they therefore deny several gods. It could however be said that although the word "gods" (*dii*) is not a word that signifies according to the rules of the art and according to original imposition, it is nonetheless a word that signifies by accommodation of usage — as *olli* for *illi*; and therefore the affirmative generates a false understanding, and the negative a true one, even if not grammatically.

3. and 4. To the objection that "principle of creatures" belongs to one alone, and similarly "phoenix": it must be said that to belong to one alone is threefold. For either a name is imposed from an unmultipliable form, as in the case of proper names — and in this sense it removes plurality both in reality and in co-signification. Or it is imposed from a form that is naturally suited to be communicated, although it is not communicated because of a particular determination — as when one says "principle of creatures." Or it is imposed from a form limited because of defect of matter — as "phoenix"; and such a term removes plurality according to reality, but not according to co-signification, because such a form can be co-signified as capable of being multiplied through individuals.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. II replies 3–4):** The chunk opens with the final two replies to the objections of Question II. Reply 3 invokes the logical point that "either another God or another non-God" is not a genuine exhaustive disjunction — the contradiction must bear over the whole composite expression, not over partial elements. The analogy of the "white monk who is black by clothing" illustrates the failure of the disjunction. Reply 4 turns on the grammar of *alius* as an adjective that draws its supposition from the substantive it modifies — hence "another God" (*alius Deus*) imports substantial otherness, not personal otherness.
- **Question III — plural "gods":** A grammatical/logical question about whether "God" (*Deus*) can properly take a plural form. Bonaventure's *Conclusio* distinguishes three senses of "God": (1) titular (*nuncupative*) — pagan gods, false idols; (2) adoptive (*adoptive*) — those who participate in divine grace; (3) by nature (*naturaliter*) — God properly so called. Only the third cannot be pluralized, because it signifies the divine nature together with conditions (simplicity, infinity, aseity, etc.) that are by definition singular and cannot be instantiated in another.
- **Two kinds of grammatical ill-formedness:** A subtle logical distinction in reply 2. *Discohesion of accidents* (as in "of Cato it is") makes an expression neither true nor false — it is simply grammatically ill-formed in a way that removes the truth-functional dimension. *Discohesion of concepts* (as in "several Peters") generates a necessarily false expression — not merely ill-formed. The negation of the first type produces neither truth nor falsehood; but the negation of "several gods" produces truth, because "gods" in the false/improper sense is being denied.
- **"Olli" for "illi":** A technical example from Latin grammar — an archaic or dialectal form used to illustrate that customary usage can give semantic force to a word that is not properly formed according to grammatical rules. The point is that "dii" (*gods*) can be used meaningfully by accommodation, even if it lacks strict grammatical legitimacy in theological discourse.
- **Three types of names that belong to one alone:** A neat logical taxonomy in reply 3–4: (a) unmultipliable form → proper name, removes plurality both really and in co-signification (like "Peter"); (b) multipliable form, contingently uncommunicated → false but grammatically well-formed plural (like "principle of creatures," where there is only one but the form could in principle be communicated); (c) multipliable form, limited by defect of matter → removes real plurality but not co-signification (like "phoenix" or "sun," whose form could in principle be multiplied). "God" in the proper sense falls under (a).
- **Question IV begins:** The fourth and final question of the article — whether "God" supposes for a person or for the nature — opens. The chunk ends after the statement of objection 2. This question is crucial for Trinitarian predication: if "God" supposes for the essence, then "God generates" would be false (as "Essence generates" is false); if for a person, then the affirmations of prior questions are well-founded. Bonaventure's answer (not yet in this chunk) will be that "God" supposes for the person by its primary mode of supposition, though it signifies the essence — a distinction parallel to the abstract/concrete analysis of Question I.
