DIST. III. PART I. THE SINGLE ARTICLE. QUESTION I.

CONCLUSION.

God, supremely knowable in Himself, would also be supremely knowable by us, if there were no defect on the part of our intellect.

It must be said that God in Himself, as supreme light, is supremely knowable; and as a light that supremely fulfills our intellect, He would be, so far as He Himself is concerned, supremely knowable even by us, unless there were some defect on the part of the knowing power — a defect that is not perfectly removed except by the God-likeness of glory. The arguments that God is knowable by creatures and even most clearly knowable, so far as He Himself is concerned, unless something impedes or is lacking on the part of the intellect, are therefore to be conceded — as will be shown afterward.

1. To the objections raised on the contrary: it must be said that there is knowledge by comprehension and knowledge by apprehension. Knowledge by apprehension consists in the manifestation of the truth of the thing known; but knowledge by comprehension consists in the inclusion of totality.

For the first kind of knowledge, a proportion of fittingness is required; and such proportion exists in the soul with respect to God, because "the soul is in some way all things" — through its assimilation to all things, since it is naturally suited to know all things, and is most capable of God through assimilation, because it is the image and likeness of God. For the second kind of knowledge — knowledge by comprehension — a proportion of equality and equivalence is required; and this does not exist in the soul with respect to God, because the soul is finite while God is infinite; and therefore the soul does not have this proportion. It is of this second kind that Dionysius speaks, and the objection runs against it — but not against the other.

2. To the objection concerning the distance between the intelligible and the sensible: it must be said that distance can be measured according to the account of being and according to the account of the knowable. In the first way there is greater distance; in the second there is not, because both — namely God and the soul — are intelligible. This is not so in the case of intellect and sense, because the sense is a determined power, but the intellect is not.

3. To the objection that the infinite cannot be grasped by the finite: some say that to grasp the infinite is twofold — namely with respect to essence, and in this way the infinite is grasped; and with respect to power, and in this way it is not grasped — just as a point is entirely reached according to substance by a line, but not totally according to power. But this solution does not seem to resolve the difficulty, because in God essence and power are the same, and each is infinite.

Therefore it must be said that infinite is twofold: one kind that stands in opposition to the simple, and such an infinite is not grasped by the finite — this is infinite mass; another kind that has infinity together with simplicity, such as God; and such an infinite, because it is simple, is wholly everywhere, and because it is infinite, is in no thing in such a way that it is not also outside that thing. This is how the cognition of God must be understood. And therefore it does not follow that if He is known wholly, He is comprehended — because the intellect does not include His totality, just as no creature includes His immensity.

4. To the objection that one who judges has power, etc.: it must be said that to judge about something is twofold: in the first way, by discerning whether it is or is not — and in this way judgment belongs to every intellect that knows with respect to every object; in another way, by approving or disapproving whether it ought to be so — and in this way one does not judge about truth, but according to it judges about other things, as Augustine says in On True Religion: "The judge does not judge about the law, but according to it judges about other things." And concerning this mode it is true what Augustine says, that "no one judges about that truth, yet no one judges without it." And it is of this second mode that what is objected holds true — that the one who judges has power over what is judged; but of the first mode it is not true that it has power over it — it can nonetheless be directed toward it as toward an object with the assistance of that truth.

5. To the last objection concerning information: it must be said that God is present to the soul itself and to every intellect through truth; therefore it is not necessary for a likeness to be abstracted from Him by which He may be known. Nevertheless, when He is known by the intellect, the intellect is informed by a certain knowledge, which is as it were a likeness — not abstracted, but impressed — inferior to God, because it is in an inferior nature, yet superior to the soul, because it makes the soul better. And this is what Augustine says in the ninth book On the Trinity, chapter eleven: "Just as when we learn bodies through the senses of the body, some likeness of them is formed in our mind: so when we know God, some likeness of God is formed; yet that knowledge is inferior, because it is in an inferior nature."

QUESTION II.

Whether God is knowable through creatures.

Second, it is asked whether God is knowable through creatures. And it seems He is not.

1. Because the way to error is not the way to knowledge; but knowledge through creatures is a way to error: therefore, etc. Proof of the minor premise: from Wisdom fourteen: "The creatures of God are a snare and a trap and a stumbling block for the feet of the foolish." Furthermore, Augustine in On Free Will speaks of those who are occupied in creatures: "Turning their back to you, they are fixed in their bodily work as in their own shadow."

2. Likewise, darkness or a dark thing is not a way to knowing what is luminous or light; but the creature is darkness, while God is light: therefore God is not knowable through creatures.

3. Likewise, the middle term through which something is known or proved of an extreme must share in something with that whose knowledge it serves; but Creator and creature have nothing in common: therefore God is not known through creatures.

4. Likewise, every middle term through which one ascends to an extreme is distant from it by a finite number of degrees; but every creature, however noble, is distant from God by infinite degrees — for however much it may be doubled, it will never arrive at His nobility: therefore through creatures one does not ascend to knowledge of God.

On the contrary: 1. From Wisdom thirteen: "From the greatness of the beauty and of the creature the Creator of these things can be seen and known." Hence Isidore in On the Highest Good: "From the beauty of the bounded creature, God causes His own beauty — which cannot be bounded — to be understood."

2. Likewise, it is shown by reason thus: it is possible to know not only the effect through the cause, but also the cause through the effect: therefore if God acts as cause according to His own nobility, and the creature is the effect, God can be known through creatures.

3. Likewise, the sensible is a way of knowing the intelligible; but the creature is sensible and God is intelligible: therefore through the creature one arrives at knowledge of the Creator.

4. Likewise, it is possible to know like through like; but every creature is like God — either as a vestige or as an image: therefore through every creature it is possible to know God.

There is therefore a question as to what the difference is between vestige and image; and since the vestige is in every creature, it is asked why the image is not similarly in every creature, and in what respect the vestige is to be discerned.

CONCLUSION.

God can be known through creatures by the natural light of reason.

I respond: It must be said that, because the cause shines forth in the effect and the wisdom of the craftsman is manifested in his work, God — who is the craftsman and cause of creatures — is known through them.

And for this there are two reasons: one from fittingness and the other from need. From fittingness, because every creature leads more toward God than toward anything else; from need, because since God, as supremely spiritual light, cannot be known in His spirituality by the intellect, which is as it were material, the soul needs to know Him through creatures.

1. To the objection that knowledge of creatures is a way to error: it must be said that knowing creatures is twofold — either with respect to their special properties and those that belong to imperfection, or with respect to their general conditions, which belong to completion. If with respect to the special properties of imperfection: either by attributing them to God, or by removing them. In the first way it is a way of error; in the second way it is a way of knowledge — and in this way God is known through removal.

But if creatures are known with respect to the conditions of perfection, this can happen in two ways — as a painting can be known in two ways: either as a painting, or as an image. Hence one either rests in the beauty of the creature, or through it one tends toward something else. In the first way it is a way of deviation; hence Augustine in On Free Will: "Woe to those who love your signs instead of you, and wander in your vestiges and abandon you who are their guide." In the second way — insofar as it is a way toward something else — it is a principle of knowing through supereminence, because every noble property in a creature is to be attributed to God in the highest degree; and in this way that is clear.

2. To the objection that a dark medium is not a way of knowing light: it must be said that there is a well-disposed eye and a bleary eye. For the well-disposed eye this is true, but not for the bleary one, for which a covering cloud or a reflecting earth serves as a medium for seeing the sun — so it is for our intellect, which is like the eye of an owl with respect to the most manifest things of nature.

3. ### Notes

- **Opening (Q. I conclusion and replies):** The chunk opens with Bonaventure's *Conclusio* for Question I and replies to the five objections. The *Conclusio* is the resolution of the opening question (whether God is knowable by creatures): God is supremely knowable in Himself; the defect is on our side, fully overcome only by the *deiformitas gloriae* of beatific vision.
- **"Knowledge by apprehension / by comprehension" (cognitio per apprehensionem / per comprehensionem):** A key Bonaventurian distinction drawn from Augustine (*De Videndo Deo* IX.21). Apprehension requires only proportio convenientiae (fittingness); comprehension requires proportio aequalitatis (equality) — the latter impossible for any finite intellect with respect to an infinite God. Dionysius's claim that God cannot be understood applies only to comprehension. Rendered with these technical terms.
- **"The soul is in some way all things" (quodam modo est anima omnia):** The Aristotelian formula from *De Anima* III.8, expressing the intellect's openness to all intelligible form — the ground of the soul's proportio convenientiae with God.
- **Infinite of mass vs. infinite with simplicity:** Bonaventure's second solution to objection 3 distinguishes *infinitum molis* (quantitative infinity, which is opposed to simplicity and cannot be grasped by a finite intellect) from *infinitum cum simplicitate* (God's infinity, which is simple and therefore wholly present everywhere without being bounded). This is a recurring distinction throughout the Trinitarian questions. The point: God can be wholly known without being comprehended, just as God is wholly everywhere without being bounded.
- **"The judge does not judge about the law" (iudex non iudicat de lege):** Augustine, *De Vera Religione* XXXI.58. Bonaventure uses this to distinguish discernment (*discernendo*) — which requires no superiority over the object — from approval/disapproval (*approbando*), which does. The intellect knows God by the first mode; only God employs the second with respect to eternal truth.
- **Reply 5: "not abstracted but impressed" (non abstracta sed impressa):** Bonaventure's careful account of how God is known without species abstraction — the soul receives an *impressed* likeness (not derived from God by abstraction, as from a sensible object, but imprinted by God's presence). This is the basis for the Scholion's long defense against ontologist readings (Malebranche, etc.): divine illumination means participation in a created light that reflects uncreated truth, not direct vision of God.
- **Question II begins:** The second question of the article — whether God is knowable *through* creatures (as opposed to *by* a creature, Question I) — opens with four objections and four *contra* arguments. The chunk ends mid-reply to objection 4. Key discussions: the analogy of the bleary eye (*oculus lippus*) for our intellect's need for creaturely mediation; the analogy/univocation distinction for the objection about lack of community between Creator and creature; the painting/image distinction (one may rest in the creature as in a painting, or pass through it as through an image toward God).
- **"Like the eye of an owl" (sicut oculus noctuae):** Aristotle, *Metaphysics* II.1 — the intellect is to the most manifest things as the owl's eye is to daylight. Bonaventure applies this to show why the soul, despite its natural kinship with God, still needs the mediation of creatures as a cloud or earth mediates the sun's light for a diseased eye.
