DOUBT IV.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says when he appropriates truth to the Son, saying: "Most perfect beauty is understood as the Son, namely the truth of the Father." But on the contrary, Augustine in the Soliloquy: "Truth is that which is"; but being is not appropriated to any particular person: therefore neither is truth.

Likewise, it seems that he wrongly appropriates order to the Holy Spirit, because in the preceding chapter he says that from order one understands the wise one; but wisdom is appropriated to the Son: therefore order too.

I respond: It must be said that truth can be considered in two ways, just as color can. For in one way color is considered with respect to that in which it exists — and in this way it is defined in the book On Sense and the Sensed: "Color is the limit of the transparent in a bounded body." In another way it is considered in comparison to the sight that it moves — and in this way it is defined in the book On the Soul: "Color is that which moves sight in accordance with the act of the transparent."

Similarly, truth can be considered in comparison to that in which it exists — and in this way what is true is "that which is"; in another way through comparison to the intellect that it moves — and in this way truth, as the Philosopher says in the second book of the Metaphysics, "is the end of speculative understanding." According to the first mode, Anselm says: "The truth of the Father is the essence of the Father." With respect to the second mode, Hilary says that "truth is the declarative of being." And since the Son proceeds as the Word — to whom the account of declaring is appropriated — the account of exemplar is therefore appropriated to Him, and consequently the account of truth in the second mode of truth. Augustine's definition, however, takes it in the first mode.

To the objection concerning order: it must be said that there is an order of things in the universe — and this is appropriated to wisdom; and there is an order of things toward an end — and this is appropriated to goodness; and so it is clear that there is no contradiction.

TREATMENT OF THE QUESTIONS.

ARTICLE I.

On the first assignment of the image.

Next, for the understanding of this second part of the present distinction — the division of which was set out above — with respect to the first assignment of the image, which is through memory, understanding, and will, three things are asked.

The first is whether the account of image is found in these three.

The second is concerning the comparison of these to their object.

The third is concerning their comparison to the soul or subject.

QUESTION I.

Whether the account of image is found in memory, understanding, and will.

That it is found in all three together seems evident:

1. For the account of image requires an express conformation in distinction; but in divine things distinction is found with respect to the three persons: therefore in the created image it is found most of all with respect to three powers.

2. Likewise, the account of image consists in perfect capacity, because according to Augustine "the mind is an image precisely insofar as it can be capable of God and a participant in Him"; but God is not fully grasped by the soul unless He is loved, nor is He loved unless He is understood, nor is He understood unless He is held present to the soul; but the first is through will, the second through understanding, the third through memory: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, knowledge or understanding is appropriated to the Son, and will to the Holy Spirit: therefore either the assignment of the image will be truncated and diminished, or it is necessary to posit a power corresponding to the Father.

On the contrary: 1. For the being of an image, representation in distinction of origin and order is required; but in these powers there is no such distinction — because they are created simultaneously with the soul, and one is not from another, nor is one after another: therefore in them the account of image is not to be found.

2. Likewise, an image is a representation according to exterior disposition, as is evident; but these three powers are most interior to the soul itself: therefore the account of image is not in them.

3. Likewise, that memory in particular does not belong to the account of image seems evident — because since the image belongs essentially to the soul and exists in it according to the rational power, yet memory, as the Philosopher says, belongs to sensible things, since it is found in brutes; hence it differs from recollection, because recollection is not found in brutes: therefore it seems that memory does not belong to the image. ### Notes

- **Opening (Doubt III continued):** The chunk opens completing Bonaventure's response to Doubt III — the question about the different enumerations of the Trinitarian vestige in creatures. He provides a systematic taxonomy of trinities found in creatures: (a) in itself: matter/form/composition, and number/weight/measure (Wisdom 11:21); (b) in comparison to other creatures: substance/power/operation (Dionysius) and quo constat/congruit/discernitur (Augustine); (c) in comparison to God: mode/form/order (referred), and unity/truth/goodness (referred and assimilated). The vestige in the strict sense belongs to the last category.
- **Correspondence of trinities:** Bonaventure's summary correspondence: unity ↔ mode ↔ efficient cause; truth ↔ form (*species*) ↔ exemplar cause; goodness ↔ order ↔ final cause. This threefold structure underlies the entire vestige theology and the *Itinerarium* I–II.
- **Doubt IV — appropriation of truth and order:** Two distinct objections: (1) Augustine's definition of truth as "that which is" (*id quod est*) makes truth coextensive with being, which is common to all persons; (2) the Lombard says order reveals the "wise one" (Wisdom appropriated to the Son), yet also appropriates order to the Holy Spirit via goodness. Bonaventure's double response: (1) truth has two senses — ontological (truth = being, common to all; Anselm: "the truth of the Father is the essence of the Father") and relational/epistemological (truth as the end of understanding; Hilary: "truth is the declarative of being" — this second sense is appropriated to the Son as Word). (2) Order likewise has two senses: the ordering of things among themselves (wisdom, Son) vs. the ordering of things toward an end (goodness, Holy Spirit).
- **Part II of Distinction III begins:** The chunk transitions to the commentary on Part II of Distinction III — the "near likenesses" (the image in the soul's powers). Article I, Question I asks whether the image is properly found in the triad of memory/understanding/will. Three *pro* arguments and three *contra* arguments are presented. The chunk ends mid-argument in the third *contra* (concerning memory and whether it belongs to the rational image, given Aristotle's claim that memory is found even in brutes). The *Conclusio* and replies are in the next chunk.
- **"Image requires representation in distinction of origin and order":** The first *contra* invokes a stricter definition of image than mere likeness — an image must represent its original through a relation of origin (one term proceeding from another) and order (one after another). The three faculties of the soul, created simultaneously with the soul and none proceeding from another, fail to meet this criterion. This sets up Bonaventure's sophisticated response about what kind of "origin" and "order" is required.
- **Memory and brutes (Aristotle, *De Memoria et Reminiscentia*):** The third *contra* distinguishes *memoria* (found even in animals) from *reminiscentia* (recollection, rational only). If memory is a sensory faculty belonging to brutes, it cannot be the image of the divine Trinity in the rational soul. Bonaventure's response (not yet translated) will distinguish Augustinian "memory" (the soul's presence to itself and to God) from Aristotelian "memory" (retention of past sensory impressions).
