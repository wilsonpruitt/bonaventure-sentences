DIST. III. PART II. ARTICLE II. QUESTION II.

QUESTION II.

Whether mind, knowledge, and love have order, equality, and consubstantiality.

Second, a question is asked concerning these things in comparison to one another, according to the threefold comparison that Augustine makes — namely of order, equality, and consubstantiality — which the Master relates in the text. There is order among these, because the mind is the parent, knowledge is the offspring, and the third is love proceeding from both. There is also equality there, because the mind knows itself only as much as it is, and loves itself only as much as it knows itself. There is also consubstantiality; hence Augustine in the ninth book On the Trinity: "We are admonished, if we can see even this much, that these things exist in the soul substantially, not as in a subject like color in a body or any quality or quantity; for whatever is such does not exceed the substance in which it is. But the mind, with the love by which it loves itself, can love something else" — and thus he holds that love is consubstantial to the mind.

1. But an objection is raised against this: First, it seems that there is no order or origin in these things. For they are either taken as innate habits or as acquired ones. If as innate, there is no order, because they exist simultaneously with the soul itself; if as acquired, then love precedes knowledge — for no one acquires or studies to learn anything unless he loves to know. Hence Augustine at the end of the ninth book On the Trinity: "The offspring of the mind is preceded by desire, by which, through seeking and finding what we wish to know, the offspring is born — which is knowledge itself." Therefore either there is no order, or it is not the order that is claimed.

2. Likewise, it seems that there is no equality there. For either knowledge and love are taken in comparison to lower things or in comparison to the soul. If in comparison to lower things, it is manifest that there is no equality — for we know many things that we do not love; if in comparison to the soul, then the equality is either with respect to intensity or with respect to extension. With respect to extension it is not there — that is clear because only one object is there; therefore it would be with respect to intensity; but that this is false seems evident, because since we know that the soul is less than God and greater than the body, it sometimes happens that we love it more than God and less than the body — and thus the quantity of love does not follow the quantity of knowledge.

3. Likewise, it seems that there is no consubstantiality there, because love and knowledge are habits and are qualities: therefore they seem to differ essentially from the mind itself.

4. Likewise, Augustine's argument is that they are not in the soul like accidents, because they extend outward; but this amounts to nothing, because accidents extend outward — as heat does in heating and color in modifying sight. Furthermore, a human being knows certain knowable things through acquired science, which is an accident, and it likewise extends outward.

CONCLUSION.

Mind, knowledge, and love have order, equality, and consubstantiality.

I respond: It must be said that, as Augustine assigns, there is order, equality, and consubstantiality in these things.

The order is found in these connatural habits of the soul in comparison to acts, just as order is posited in faith, hope, and charity, even though they are infused simultaneously.

1. And so the objection on the contrary is clear: because the order is not in the habits themselves considered absolutely, but through relation to acts.

Similarly, there is equality according to the conversion of the soul upon itself and the perfection of the aforesaid habits. Hence Augustine says in the ninth book On the Trinity that there is no equality in these habits except according as they are perfect.

2. And so the solution to the objection concerning love is clear: because that love is not perfect love but lustful and disordered. — Or it can be said that they are equal according as they are connatural; for one is as ready and easy to know oneself as to love oneself; but this does not hold of acquired habits, whether bad or good, and the claim is not understood of those.

Similarly, the third thing is present there — namely consubstantiality — because, as was said above, love and knowledge connatural to the soul exist according as the soul is converted upon itself; and so they add nothing whatsoever above the powers themselves. For by the fact that the soul is present to itself it has knowledge; by the fact that it is one with itself it has the habit of love; and therefore, just as the powers are consubstantial to the soul — as was seen above — so too are habits of this kind. Hence even if they seem to denote the mode of a habit or quality, in reality they add nothing above the powers.

3. And so the response to the objection is clear: that they are not qualities in this sense.

4. To the objection concerning Augustine's argument: it must be said that that argument does not conclude principally that love or knowledge are in the soul substantially; and this is because then by equal reasoning it could be said and objected of every love. But it concludes consequently, which is clear as follows. For when love extends itself outside its subject by loving another, this is by virtue of substance — just as it has no existence of its own except through substance. If therefore love and knowledge extend themselves through substantial power, and these are understanding and will; and the love by which the soul loves itself is the same as the will itself; and similarly the knowledge by which it knows is nothing other than understanding: it remains therefore that love and knowledge with respect to themselves are consubstantial to the mind itself.

QUESTION III.

Whether the trinity of the image — which consists in mind, knowledge, and love — necessarily leads to knowledge of the three divine persons.

Third and finally, it is asked whether this trinity of the image — of mind, knowledge, and love — necessarily leads to knowledge of the Trinity with respect to the persons. And it seems that it does:

1. Because in this trinity of the image there is relation; but there is relation only with respect to persons: etc.

2. Likewise, in this trinity there is distinction, because knowledge is not love; but in God there is no distinction except of persons: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, in this trinity there is the origin of one being born from one, and of a third from both: therefore since these are proper to the persons, it is clear, etc.

4. Likewise, in this trinity love is the third — which is proper to the Holy Spirit and which is toward another: therefore it seems that it necessarily leads to the Trinity of persons.

On the contrary: 1. This trinity is understood in creatures without personal distinction: therefore it can also be understood in God; but this is false: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, knowledge and love are in each of the persons; but through what is in all of them one does not arrive at knowledge of personal distinction: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, granted that there were only one person, He would still know and love Himself: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, the philosophers knew this trinity, and yet they did not know the Trinity of persons: therefore this does not necessarily lead to that.

CONCLUSION.

Reason alone does not ascend from this trinity of mind, knowledge, and love to knowledge of the Trinity.

I respond: It must be said that through this trinity one can ascend to knowledge of the Trinity, by attributing to that supreme Trinity what is in this trinity. But this can happen in two ways. For either these three can be attributed to God according to substance — so that by "mind" we understand mind in God, and by "knowledge" in the soul we understand knowledge in God, and so with the third; and in this way it does not lead to knowledge of the Trinity except with respect to appropriated attributes — and this is how the philosophers understood it.

Or these three can be referred to God by reason of properties — which are order, origin, distinction, and relation; and in this way they lead to knowledge of the Trinity with respect to proper attributes.

But to posit or understand these in God — faith can do this, but not reason; and thus perfect knowledge of the image is not had except by faith. Hence it must be conceded that the image, perfectly known as image, leads to knowledge of the Trinity; but not absolutely. And from this both sides of the question are clear.

1. and 2. To the objection concerning love: it must be said that love can denote complacency — and in this sense it is common; or it can denote connection or communion or gift — and in this sense it has the account of a person.

DOUBTS CONCERNING THE TEXT OF PART II.

DOUBT I.

In this part a question arises concerning what he says: "The image of God remains." On the contrary: Psalm [72]: "Lord, in the city you will reduce their image to nothing."

I respond: "Image" is said in two ways — with respect to substantial being, and this has regard to the trinity of powers and their order and equality, and in this sense it always remains; in another way insofar as it adds well-being above mere being, such as beauty and honor; and this can be lost, because "the human being, when he was in honor, did not understand."

DOUBT II.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "But memory, however, is said in relation to something, etc." — therefore memory, understanding, and will are in the category of relation.

Likewise, it is asked why mind is said to be "in relation to itself" more than memory or understanding.

I respond: It must be said that to be said "in relation to something" is twofold — either properly and through itself, as father and son; or by reason of something annexed, because it has an annexed respect and inclination; and in this way memory is said to be in relation to something, because it has an innate respect toward the memorable, and similarly understanding toward the intelligible and will toward the willing; and these objects have a respect toward one another. And so the objection is clear.

To the question concerning mind: it must be said that "mind" is named from an essential act. For this it must be understood that *that by which it is* gives the soul the most generic being, and in this sense it is called essence; or insofar as it gives generic being, and in this sense it is called life — because the soul is in the genus of the living; or insofar as it gives spiritual being, and in this sense it is called mind. For "mind" is said only of what lives with intellectual life. — Or: the soul in itself is called essence, as act of the body it is called life, as perfectible by God it is called mind.

DOUBT III.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: that three persons are not "of one God" — because this seems to say something false, because if they are of one essence, and essence is God, therefore they are of one God. If you say that the inference does not hold, I ask: why is it not conceded that three persons are "of one God"? If it is because the genitive case denotes diversity, then since essence is not diverse from person, one cannot say "three persons are of one essence."

I respond: It must be said that the genitive is used in three ways — sometimes in the account of possession, as when it is said "the ox of Peter or of John"; sometimes by virtue of declaration of essence, as "a woman of outstanding form"; sometimes intransitively, as "creature of salt." A general term is used intransitively with a specific one — and in this way one can say "substance or person of God"; by virtue of declaration of essence, a name importing form in the manner of form — and in this way three persons are said to be "of one essence." Because therefore when one says "three persons are of one God," "God" signifies neither in the manner of form nor in the manner of specifier, it is understood in the manner of possessor or principiator — and therefore the expression is simply false.

DOUBT IV.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says — that the image is "for the most part unlike." For it seems [that since] an image is an expressed likeness, if it is for the most part unlike, it is not an image.

I respond: It must be said that expression is either simple or in a genus. If therefore we speak of expression simply, then I say that the rational soul is not greatly like God; but if we speak in the genus of creatures — because it approaches as much as created nature can — in this sense it is said to be greatly like God and an expressed likeness of God.

DOUBT V.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "If there were two, either each would be insufficient, or one would be superfluous" — because according to this line of reasoning it could be shown that there is only one person.

I respond: It must be said that among the persons there can be no superfluity, because in them there is one sufficiency; hence if one were superfluous, all would be. But there cannot be any insufficiency there either, because three have nothing more than one. But if there were two essences, there would be two sufficiencies — if each were per se sufficient; but if one together with the other, each would be insufficient: and so it is clear that the case of two essences is not like that of two persons.

DOUBT VI.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says — that the rational man understood that God has wisdom that was begotten from Him, because he understood that He is not a foolish thing: therefore it seems from this that the Father is wise through begotten wisdom.

I respond: It must be said that this inference is not to be understood immediately — namely: because He is not a foolish thing, therefore He has begotten wisdom. But it must be understood thus: God is a spiritual substance: therefore He is naturally suited to know; therefore if He does not have wisdom, He is a foolish thing; but He is not a foolish thing; therefore He has wisdom; but there is no wisdom without a word, and there is no word unless it proceeds from a mind and is thus generated: therefore from the first principle, if He has wisdom, it is necessary that wisdom be begotten. And all these inferences must be understood as immediate.

DISTINCTION IV.

Whether God the Father begot Himself as God.

Here arises a quite necessary question. For it is established and irrefragably true that God the Father begot the Son. Therefore it is asked whether it must be conceded that God begot God. For if God begot God, it seems that He begot either Himself as God or another. But if He begot another God, there is not only one God; if He begot Himself as God, some thing begot itself.

To which, in response, we say that it is to be conceded — soundly and in a Catholic manner — that one begot one and that God begot God, because God the Father begot God the Son. In the Creed too it is written: "Light from Light, true God from true God." But what is further added — therefore He begot Himself as God, or another God — neither is to be conceded, we say. That He did not beget another God is manifest, because there is only one God. That He did not beget Himself is shown by Augustine in the first book On the Trinity, saying: "Those who think it to be within God's power to beget Himself err all the more, because not only is God not so constituted, but neither is any spiritual or corporeal creature. For there is nothing that begets itself in order to exist" — and therefore it must not be believed or said that God begot Himself.

But the garrulous reasoners press on, saying: if God the Father begot God, He begot either the God who is God the Father, or the God who is not God the Father. If He begot the God who is not God the Father: therefore there is a God who is not God the Father — therefore there is not only one God. If He begot the God who is God the Father: therefore He begot Himself.

To which we respond by distinguishing this proposition as they present it: if God the Father begot God, either the God who is God the Father or the God who is not God the Father. For this can be understood in a sound and in a perverse sense; and therefore it must be responded as follows: God the Father begot the God who is the Father Himself — this we say is false; and we concede the other — namely, He begot the God who is not the Father. Yet He did not beget another God, nor is the one who was begotten any other God than the Father; rather He is one God with the Father. But if it is further added: He begot the God who is not God the Father — here we distinguish, because this can be understood in two ways: He begot the God who is not God the Father — namely God the Son, who as Son is not the Father, who is God: this sense is true. But if it is understood thus: He begot the God who is not God the Father — that is, who is not the God who is the Father: this sense is false. For one and the same God is Father and Son and Holy Spirit; and conversely, the Father and Son and Holy Spirit are one God.

CHAPTER II.

Whether the Trinity is predicated of one God just as one God is predicated of three persons.

But certain adversaries of the truth concede that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, or three persons, are one God and one substance — but they are unwilling to concede that one God or one substance is three persons, saying that the divine substance is predicated of three persons, but that three persons are not predicated of the divine substance. The Catholic faith, however, holds and proclaims both that three persons are one God, one substance or essence or divine nature, and that one God or the divine essence is three persons. Hence Augustine in the first book On the Trinity says: "God Himself, the Trinity, is rightly understood — blessed and alone powerful." Behold how expressly he said "God Himself the Trinity," in order to show both that God Himself is the Trinity and that the Trinity is God Himself. Likewise in the same work: "In those words of the Apostle, where, speaking of the coming of Christ, he says: He will show forth the blessed and only powerful, the King of kings and Lord of lords, who alone has immortality, etc. — there neither the Father is properly named, nor the Son, nor the Holy Spirit, but the blessed and sole powerful — that is, the one and only true God, who is the Trinity itself." Behold, here too he says openly that the one and only true God is the Trinity itself; and if one God is the Trinity, therefore one God is three persons. Likewise in the fifth book On the Trinity: "We say that God alone is the Trinity itself." Likewise in the book called the Enchiridion to Laurence, chapter nine: "It is enough for a Christian to believe that the cause of all created things, visible or invisible, is none other than the goodness of the Creator, who is the one true God, and that there is no nature that is not either He Himself or from Him, and that He is the Trinity — the Father, namely, and the Son and the Holy Spirit." Likewise Augustine in the sermon On Faith: "We believe that one God is the one Trinity of the divine name." Likewise in the sixth book On the Trinity: "We say that God alone is the Trinity itself." Behold, by these and many other authorities it is evidently shown that it must be said and believed that one God is the Trinity and one substance is three persons; just as conversely the Trinity is said to be one God and three persons are said to be one substance.

Now let us return to the question posed above, where it was asked whether God the Father begot Himself as God, or another God. To which we say that neither is to be conceded. Yet Augustine says in the Letter to Maximus that God the Father begot another Himself, in these words: "The Father, in order to have a Son from Himself, did not diminish Himself, but so begot from Himself another self, that He remained whole in Himself and was in the Son as great as He is and as one." This can be understood thus — namely, He begot from Himself another than Himself, yet not another God, but another person; or He begot another self — that is, He begot another who is what He Himself is. For even though the Father is other than the Son, He is not something other than the Son — rather they are one.

COMMENTARY ON DISTINCTION IV.

Questions arising from the comparison of generation to the concrete essential term — the name "God."

"Here there arises a quite necessary question," etc.

DIVISION OF THE TEXT.

In the preceding distinction the Master proved the Trinity and Unity through fitting likenesses and arguments. In the present distinction the second part is set forth, in which he resolves the doubts that arise. A doubt arises from this: that in divine things there is both a trinity and a unity, and thus something distinguishing and something distinguished, and something indistinct — as are the substantial terms. The doubt therefore arises from the comparison of the distinguishing property to the substantial term. This part has two sections. In the first he raises the doubt from the comparison of the distinguishing property to substance or essence; in the second to its power, at distinction six: "Furthermore, a question is usually raised."

Likewise, the first part has two sections, because substance can be signified in concrete form — as through the name "God" — or in abstract form — as through the name "essence." First, therefore, he raises the question from the comparison of generation to the name "God"; second, to the name "essence," at distinction five: "After these things it is asked whether it must be conceded," etc.

This distinction has four sections, corresponding to the four things touched upon there. In the first, taking as given that "God begot God" is true, he asks about "He begot Himself or another" — which he resolves by eliminating both horns. In the second he asks about "He begot a God who is God the Father, or who is not God the Father" — and resolves it by making a distinction on the side of the predicate, at: "But the garrulous reasoners press on." In the third he asks about "God is the Trinity" and proves with many authorities that it is true, on the occasion of the foregoing, at: "But certain adversaries of the truth." In the fourth he returns to his original matter — namely the first question asked — and adds to the first solution that, although "He begot Himself" or "another" cannot be conceded separately, they can nonetheless be conceded jointly, at: "Now let us return to the question posed above."

TREATMENT OF THE QUESTIONS.

For the understanding of what the Master touches upon in the present distinction, four things are asked.

First, whether this expression is to be conceded in divine things: "God begot God."

Second, whether the unity of essence admits this expression: "God begot another God," or "God is other than God."

Third, a question is asked about the co-signification of the name "God" — namely whether grammatically we can say "several gods."

Fourth and finally, a question is asked about the supposition of the name "God" — whether it supposes for a person or for the essence.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. II, Article II):** The chunk opens with the remaining objections (2–3) of Question II and the full *Conclusio*, replies, and scholion. Translated seamlessly from the prior chunk.
- **"Order in these habits according to acts, not absolutely":** The key to Bonaventure's resolution of objection 1. Order is not found in mind/knowledge/love as simultaneously existing habits taken in themselves, but in the orientation of their acts: memory's act (retention of self) disposes toward understanding's act (self-knowledge), which in turn disposes toward love's act (self-love when what is known is good). An analogy with faith/hope/charity (simultaneously infused, yet ordered in their acts) clarifies the point.
- **"Consubstantiality" qualified:** Objections 3–4 challenge the claim that knowledge and love are "substantial" to the mind. Bonaventure's resolution: they add nothing above the powers themselves; the love by which the soul loves itself *is* the will itself, and the knowledge by which it knows itself *is* understanding itself. "Consubstantiality" therefore does not mean absolute real identity, but the same mode of consubstantiality established in the prior question — the powers belong to the same genus as the soul by reduction.
- **Question III — image and Trinity:** The final question of Article II reaches the theological apex: does the trinity of the image *necessarily* demonstrate the divine Trinity? Bonaventure's graduated *Conclusio*: (1) referred to God according to substance → leads only to appropriated attributes (what philosophers could know); (2) referred to God according to relational properties (order, origin, distinction, relation) → leads to proper Trinitarian attributes; but (3) only faith can make this second attribution. The image, *perfectly known as image*, leads to the Trinity — but this perfect knowledge is itself a gift of faith.
- **Distinction III, Part II Doubts (I–VI):** The remaining doubts concern the Master's text of Part II. Doubt I: the permanent vs. corruptible image (substantial being vs. well-being). Doubt II: whether the soul's powers are in the category of relation (they are only secondarily relational, by annexed respect); and the etymology of "mens" as naming the spiritual act of being. Doubt III: the grammar of the genitive case — "three persons of one essence" is sound because "essence" functions as a formal specifier, but "three persons of one God" is false because "God" in this construction denotes a possessor. Doubt IV: "expressed likeness" is relative to the genus of creatures, not absolute. Doubt V: the superfluity argument applies to essences but not to persons (a single sufficiency is in all persons). Doubt VI: the inference from "not a foolish thing" to "has begotten wisdom" requires several intermediate steps that must be made explicit.
- **Distinction IV begins:** The chunk concludes with the Lombard's text of Distinction IV and Bonaventure's *Divisio Textus* and four-question overview. The Lombard's text concerns whether "God begot God" implies self-begetting or polytheism, and whether "God is the Trinity" can be said as well as "the Trinity is God." Both are standard 12th-century Trinitarian dialectical puzzles inherited from Abelard and Gilbert of Poitiers.
