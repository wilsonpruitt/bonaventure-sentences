DOUBT IV.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "one God is three persons." But on the contrary: whatever is predicated of one and the same thing is predicated of one another: therefore if one God is Father and Son, then the Father is the Son. If you say this is true when things are predicated of one singular, but not when predicated of one common term — on the contrary: nothing is subjected to two things in a unique supposition, even if it is common to them. Hence this is false: "a man is Socrates and Plato": therefore similarly in the matter at hand.

I respond: It must be said that, as is clear, the supposition of the name "God" is otherwise than the supposition of any other term. For since it has the nature of a common term and of a discrete term, it stands simultaneously for many as for one: and therefore it does not follow: "God is Father and Son, therefore God the Father is the Son" or conversely. Similarly, neither is it permissible to infer from this that the Father is the Son. And it must be noted that such predication is by identity: therefore the supposit is truly predicated of the formal term.

DOUBT V.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what the Master says: "one and only true God is the Trinity" — because this seems to contradict what he says below, namely that "Trinity" is a collective name and "one and only" are partitive and discretive names: therefore just as this is false: "one and only man is every man," so is this.

I respond: It must be said that the name "Trinity" is collective of persons; but "one and only," added to the term "God," do not denote the discretion of a person, but of the nature from others. Hence "one and only God" denotes one and only nature; and since in divine things nature and the thing of the nature or supposit are the same, the Trinity is therefore predicated of God by predication of identity. Nor is it similar in the case of "every man" and "one and only man." For the term "man" is a term that can be confused and multiplied, and therefore this is true: "every man is a man" — nor does it stand simultaneously for many unless confused; and therefore this is false: "a man is every man," because it is true neither by reason of supposition nor by identity, nor is nature and the thing of nature the same in a human being.

DOUBT VI.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "It is enough for a Christian to believe," etc. For he seems to say something false, because either he means "enough" with respect to faith — and in that case it is false, because many other things must be believed; or "enough" with respect to knowledge — and that similarly is false, because from this one never sufficiently knows the cause of a thing.

I respond: It must be said that "enough" is understood with respect to knowledge — not just any knowledge, but that necessary for salvation.

DOUBT VII.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "The Father begot another self." For it seems to combine those two poorly, because "self" denotes complete identity and "another" denotes diversity — and so they are opposed, and so they mutually imply opposites. If you say that one diminishes from the other — I ask: what, and from what?

I respond: It must be said that direct opposition is always understood with respect to the same thing. Since therefore in divine things there is simultaneously identity in nature and otherness in supposit, and this without opposition — the name of identity and the name of otherness are joined in speech without opposition — indeed, for the expression of a singular mode.

DOUBT VIII.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "The Father, in order to have a Son, did not diminish Himself" — because this seems to say something false; for the following argument holds: whoever generates one son such that he cannot generate further diminishes his power; but this is so in the Father: therefore, etc.

I respond: That is true if generating another were a matter of power; but that, once one has been begotten, He could generate another — this denotes imperfection of the power in generating, because from this it would be shown that He did not give everything to the one.

DOUBT IX.

Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "He begot from Himself another self, but not another god, but another person" — whether "other" (*alterum*) or "another" (*alium*) is said more properly; and it seems "other" (*alterum*), because it denotes a lesser diversity — for Socrates is called "other" from himself; but in divine things the diversity is minimal. But on the contrary: accidental differences cause something to be called "other" (*alterum*); but in divine things there is no accident: therefore it ought not to be called "other."

Likewise, it seems that neither is well said. For if substantial differences cause something to be called "another" (*aliud*), and accidental ones "other" (*alterum*), since neither obtains in divine things, neither seems to be properly said.

I respond: It must be said that since the Father differs from the Son both in supposit and in property, He can be called "another" (*alius*) and He can be called "other" (*alter*). But since that property does not accrue accidentally to the person, it is more properly said "another" (*alius*). And because "another" (*alius*) refers to the supposit and "another thing" (*aliud*) to the essence: therefore, even if "another" (*alius*) in the masculine is admitted there, nevertheless "another" (*aliud*) in the neuter is not.

DISTINCTION V.

Whether the divine essence begot the Son, or was begotten by the Father, or whether the Son was born from it, or the Holy Spirit proceeded from it.

After these things it is asked whether it must be conceded that the Father begot the divine essence, or that the divine essence begot the Son, or that essence begot essence, or whether the divine essence neither begot nor was begotten at all.

To which, agreeing with the Catholic writers, we say that neither did the Father beget the divine essence, nor did the divine essence beget the Son, nor did the divine essence beget essence. By "essence" here we understand the divine nature, which is common to three persons and wholly in each individually. Therefore it must not be said that:

1. The Father begot the divine essence — because if the Father were said to have begotten the divine essence, the divine essence would be said relatively toward the Father or would be placed for a relative term. But if it were said relatively or placed for a relative term, it would not indicate essence. For as Augustine says in the fifth book On the Trinity: "What is said relatively does not indicate substance."

Likewise, since God the Father is the divine essence, if He were its begetter, He would be the begetter of the very thing that He Himself is; and so the same thing would have begotten itself — which Augustine denies, as we showed above.

Likewise, if the Father is the begetter of the divine essence, since He Himself is the divine essence and is God — therefore by the act of generating He both is and is God. Thus it would not be through the thing generated that the Father is God, but through the act of generating that the Father both is and is God. And if this is so, not the begotten gives the begetter the cause of being and being God, but the begetter gives the begotten this cause. By similar reasoning Augustine proves in the seventh book On the Trinity "that the Father is not wise through the wisdom He begot — because if He is wise through it, He is it; for in that being is the same as being wise. But if being is the same as being wise there, the Father is not wise through the wisdom He begot. For what else do we mean when we say 'being and being wise are the same there,' except that He is through that whereby He is wise? Therefore that which is the cause of His being wise is also the cause of His being. If therefore the wisdom He begot is the cause of His being wise, it is also the cause of His being. But no one would say that the cause of the Father's being is the wisdom begotten by the Father; for what is more insane? So therefore if the Father begot the essence by which He is, the essence He begot would be the cause of His being." Therefore He did not beget the essence by which He is. "For in that simplicity," says Augustine, "since being wise is not other than being, wisdom is the same as essence there"; and therefore what is said of wisdom, the same we say of essence. Therefore just as He did not beget the wisdom by which He is wise, so neither the essence by which He is. For as He is wise through wisdom and powerful through power, so He is through essence; and wisdom and power are the same as essence. It is clear from the foregoing, then, that the Father did not beget the divine essence.

But to this there seems to be contrary what Augustine says in the one book On Faith and the Symbol, chapter three: "God, when He begot the Word, begot that which He Himself is — not from nothing, nor from some already created and fashioned matter, but from Himself that which He Himself is." Likewise: "God begot God" — that is, the same from Himself. And here Augustine in the book On Faith to Peter says: "So believe Christ the Son of God, one person of the Trinity, to be true God, that you do not doubt His divinity to have been born from the nature of the Father." Here he seems to say that the nature of the Son is born from the nature of the Father. The same also in the fifteenth book On the Trinity says: "The Son is said to be counsel from counsel and will from will, just as substance from substance, wisdom from wisdom." And here he seems to say that substance is begotten from substance and wisdom from wisdom. But we determine this thus: "wisdom from wisdom and substance from substance" means the Son, who is wisdom, who is substance, is from the Father, who is the same substance and wisdom; and the Son, who is divinity, is born from the Father who is the divine nature. And to say it more expressly: we say the Son-wisdom is from the Father-wisdom, and we say the Son-substance is begotten from and by the Father-substance. That this is how Augustine must be understood, he shows in the seventh book On the Trinity, saying: "The Father Himself is wisdom; and the Son is called the wisdom of the Father, in the way He is called the Light of the Father — that is, as light from light; and so let wisdom from wisdom be understood; and each is one wisdom and one essence." Likewise: "Therefore Christ is called the power and wisdom of God, because from the Father — who is power and wisdom — He Himself also is power and wisdom, just as He is light from the Father who is light, and He is the fount of life with God the Father who is the fount of life. The Son therefore is wisdom from the Father-wisdom, as the Son is light from the Father-light, and God the Son from God the Father, so that each individually is light and each individually is God and each individually is wisdom, and together one light, one God, one wisdom." Behold, with these words Augustine manifestly discloses from what sense those words and similar ones are to be taken — namely when it is said "substance from substance" or "substance begot substance."

But to this there also seems to be contrary what Hilary says in the fourth book On the Trinity: "The Son has nothing that is not born; and the admiration of the honor of the begotten one is in the honor of the one who begets." Since therefore the Son has essence — for the whole divine essence is in him — it seems that the divine essence itself is born. Likewise in the fifth book he says: "The birth of God cannot fail to hold the nature from which it has proceeded; for what subsists as other than God does not subsist from any other source than God." Behold, here he says that the birth of God proceeded from the nature — and so it seems from these words and the preceding ones that the nature of God both has begotten and has been begotten. What he says more openly in the ninth book On the Trinity: "We profess that the only-begotten God, remaining in the form of God, remained in the nature of the human being, and we do not pour back the unity of the servant form into the nature of the divine unity, nor again do we preach the Father in the Son through corporeal insinuation — but we confess that the nature born of the same genus had in itself naturally the nature of the one begetting it, which nature, remaining in the form of the generating nature, accepted the form of the nature and infirmity of the body. For the nature of God had not failed to exist, but the nature of God — remaining in itself — had assumed the lowliness of earthly birth, exercising the power of its own kind in the manner of the assumed lowliness." Behold, here he openly says that the nature begot, that the nature was begotten, and that the nature assumed a nature — which is denied by most. Likewise in the same work: "Is it an injury to the only-begotten God that the unbegottable God should be His Father, when the only-begotten birth subsists from the unbegottable God in the only-begotten nature?" Behold, here too he speaks of an only-begotten nature.

But since he wishes these words to be understood rightly, he himself says in the fourth book: "The meaning of words is to be taken from the reasons that prompted saying them, because things are not subject to speech, but speech to things." These words therefore can be understood thus: the Son has nothing except what is born — that is, He has nothing as God except what He received by being born; and in being born He had the subsisting nature of the Father in Himself. Hence the same Hilary adds in the fifth book: "The begotten one has the same nature as the one who begat — yet so that the one who is born is not the one who begat (for how could He be the Father, if He were begotten?), but the one who is begotten subsists in those very things in which the one who begot is wholly present; because the one who is begotten is not from any other source. And therefore what subsists in one from one is not referred to another. And so in the generation of the Son, the immutable God generating the immutable God follows, as it were, His own nature; and from the immutable God the perfect birth of the immutable God does not abandon its own nature. Let us therefore understand the nature of God as subsisting in that one, when God is in God; and there is no other God beyond the one who is God, because that God Himself and in Him is God."

The nature of God the Father is therefore taught to be truly present in God the Son, when the God who is God is understood to be in Him. For there is one in one, and one from one.

It is also said, and is frequently read in sacred Scripture, that the Father begot the Son from His own substance. Hence Augustine in the book On Faith to Peter says: "God the Father, born of no God, begot from His own nature, without beginning, God the Son, equal to Himself and coeternal with the same divinity with which He Himself is naturally eternal." Behold, here Augustine says that the Son was begotten from the nature of the Father. But there is one nature of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit. If therefore the Son was begotten from the nature of the Father, He was begotten from the nature of the Son and of the Holy Spirit — indeed from the nature of the three persons. The same Augustine also in the fifteenth book On the Trinity says that Christ is the Son of the substance of the Father and begotten from the substance of the Father, explaining that word of the Apostle speaking of God the Father: "Who delivered us from the power of darkness and transferred us into the kingdom of the Son of His love" — "By what is said 'the Son of His love,'" he says, "let nothing else be understood than His beloved Son, than the Son of His substance. For the love of the Father, which is in His ineffably simple nature, is nothing other than that nature and substance itself — as we have often said and do not tire of repeating — and therefore the Son of His love is none other than the one who was begotten from His substance." Behold, here Augustine openly says that the Son was begotten from the substance of the Father and is the Son of the substance of the Father. The same Augustine also in the second book Against the Heretic Maximin asserts that the substance of God begot the Son and that the Son was begotten from the substance of the Father, saying: "Full of carnal thoughts, you do not think that the substance of God begets the Son from itself unless it suffers what the substance of flesh suffers when it begets. You err, not knowing the Scriptures nor the power of God. For you do not at all conceive of the true Son of God if you deny that He was born from the substance of the Father. For He was not the son of a man and then made the Son of God by God's gift — born of God by grace, not by nature. Or perhaps, even though He was not the son of a man, He was nevertheless already some sort of creature and was converted into the Son of God by God's changing Him? But none of this is so: therefore He was born either from nothing or from some substance. But lest we believe you think the Son of God is from nothing, you affirmed that you do not say that the Son of God is from nothing. From some substance, therefore, He is; and if not from the substance of the Father, tell us from which; but you will not find it. Then let it not weary you to confess with us that the only-begotten Son of God, Jesus Christ, is from the substance of the Father." Likewise in the same work: "We both read that we may be in His true Son, Jesus Christ. Tell us therefore whether this true Son of God — distinguished by some property from those who are sons by grace — is from no substance or from some? 'I do not say from none, nor will I say from nothing,' you say: therefore He is from some substance. I ask: from which? If He is not from the substance of the Father, seek another. If you find no other, acknowledge the substance of the Father, and confess the Son to be consubstantial with the Father." Likewise in the same work: "I confess that God the Father begot entirely incorruptibly, but that He begot what He Himself is. I also say what must be said often: either the Son of God was born from some substance or from none; if from none, then from nothing — which you no longer say; if truly from some substance, but not from the substance of the Father, He is not a true Son; if however from the substance of the Father, then Father and Son are of one and the same substance. But you do not want the Son to have been begotten from the substance of the Father; and yet you concede that He is neither from nothing nor from any matter, but from the Father — and you do not see how necessary it is that what is not from nothing nor from any other thing, but from God, cannot be except from the substance of God, and must be that which God is, from whom He is — that is, God born from God; because no other previously existed, but the coeternal nature is from God."

By these words introduced, it seems indicated that the divine substance begot the Son and that the Son was begotten from the substance of the Father and that the coeternal nature is from God and that the Father begot what He Himself is. But what He Himself is, is the divine essence; and so it could be thought that the divine essence begot. These words greatly move us, and how they are to be understood I would rather hear from others than transmit myself. Nevertheless, speaking without prejudging or rashness, from this sense can these words be taken: "the coeternal nature is from God" — that is, the Son who is coeternal with the Father is from the Father, such that He is of the same nature with Him or of the same nature. This sense is confirmed by Augustine, who there subjoins and as if explaining what he had said — for after saying "the coeternal nature is from God," he added: "The Son is not other than that from which He is — that is, the Father and Son are of one and the same substance." Then, more openly, he discloses that such an understanding is to be had from the said words, saying in the same book Against Maximin: "This Trinity is of one and the same substance — because the Son is not from some matter or from nothing, but from the one from whom He was begotten. Likewise the Holy Spirit is not from some matter or from nothing, but is from the one from whom He proceeds." With these words he clearly shows the reason for saying that the Son is from the substance of the Father — namely because He is begotten from the Father, being of the same substance with Him; and the Holy Spirit is from the substance of the Father and of the Son — because He proceeds from both, being of the same substance.

Behold, it is shown from those words that the Son and the Holy Spirit are not from nothing, but from something, yet not from any matter. Hence Hilary also in the twelfth book On the Trinity says: "The only-begotten God, since He was born, testifies the Father as his author; since He was born from one who remains, He was not born from nothing; and since He was born before time, He surpasses all understanding by being born." Here it is openly said that the Son was not born from nothing. Similarly the Holy Spirit is not to be said to be or to proceed from nothing — "because the Son was born from the substance of the Father" — that is, He is from the Father, with whom He is of the same substance and is the same substance. From this sense also must be taken the expression: "The Father begot what He Himself is" — that is, the Son, who is what the Father is. And that this is how it must be understood, Augustine discloses, saying in the first book Against Maximin: "The Father begot what He is; otherwise the Son is not true, if the Son is not what the Father is." Likewise: "the substance of God begot the Son" — that is, the Father who is substance begot the Son, who is the same substance and of the same substance. That this is how it is to be understood, Augustine shows, saying to Maximin: "Just as you say 'Spirit begot Spirit,' so say 'Spirit of the same nature or substance begot Spirit.' Likewise, just as you say 'God begot God,' so say 'God of the same nature or substance begot God.' If you believe and say this, you will be accused of nothing further in this matter." For with these words he discloses how the aforesaid are to be understood. Similarly: "The Son was born from the substance of the Father, or the Father begot the Son from His own nature or essence" — that is, from Himself by nature and essence He begot the Son of the same essence and nature, and who is the same essence and nature. Similarly expound that expression: "The Son of the substance of the Father" — that is, the Son of the Father-substance — that is, who is substance, with whom the Son also is the same substance, because the Son is consubstantial with the Father. And this sense is aided by the words of Augustine, who says in the seventh book On the Trinity: "We say three persons of the same essence, or we say three persons are one essence. But we do not say three persons from the same essence, as if what is essence were one thing and what is person were another." With these words he shows that it is not to be said that a person is from essence, except in the aforesaid sense. This sense is also confirmed from what the same author says in the fifteenth book On the Trinity: "As our knowledge is to the knowledge of God, so also our word — which is born from our knowledge — is unlike that Word of God which was born from the essence of the Father. And this is as if one were to say: from the knowledge of the Father, from the wisdom of the Father, or — which is more expressive — from the Father who is essence, from the Father who is knowledge, from the Father who is wisdom."

CHAPTER III.

Why the Word of the Father is called the natural Son.

Hence it is that the only-begotten of God alone is called the natural Son — because He is of the same nature and is the same nature as the Father. Hence Hilary in the fifth book On the Trinity, speaking of Christ, says: "He is Son by nature, because He has the same nature as the one who begot Him."

DIVISION OF THE TEXT.

In the preceding distinction the Master raised the question about the comparison of generation to the concrete essential term — the name "God." In the present distinction he raises the question about the comparison of generation to the abstract essential term — which is "essence." And this part is divided into two parts.

In the first part he raises the question from the comparison of generation to essence in the account of a term — namely whether essence is generated; in the second, in the account of a principle — whether essence generates, etc., at: "Likewise it is not to be said that the divine essence begot the Son," etc.

Likewise, the first part has four sections. In the first he proposes the problems — namely whether the essence is generated or generates. In the second he adduces three arguments proving that the essence is not generated by the Father, at: "Therefore it is not to be said that the Father begot," etc. In the third he adduces arguments on the contrary side, at: "But to this there seems to be contrary," etc. In the fourth and last he resolves and expounds, at: "To which we respond."

"Likewise it is not to be said." This is the second part of this distinction, in which the Master determines the second part of the question — which is about the comparison of generation to essence in the account of a principle — and this part has three sections. In the first he determines this question, saying that essence does not generate; and he confirms this through an argument leading to the impossible. In the second he adduces authorities that are directly contrary to the aforesaid solution, at: "What appears to be contrary to what has been said." In the third he adduces authorities from which a contrariety can be elicited, at: "It is also said, and frequently," etc.

Likewise, the second part has four sections. In the first it adduces the contrary authorities of Augustine. In the second it expounds the adduced authorities, at: "But we determine this thus." In the third, against the aforesaid expositions, it adduces other authorities of Hilary in which a more express contrariety is noted, at: "But to this there also seems to be contrary." In the fourth it explains those authorities and confirms its explanation through the words of Hilary, at: "But since he wishes these words to be understood rightly."

"It is also said, and frequently in sacred Scripture it is read." This is the third part, in which he objects against the solution through authorities from which the contrariety of the aforesaid solution is elicited — because in these it is not said that the essence generates or is generated, but that from the essence or substance of the Father the Son is generated — and this part has four sections. In the first he adduces the contrary authorities. In the second he expounds those adduced, at: "By these words introduced it seems indicated" — where he resumes the aforesaid exposition. In the third he draws from those opinions a certain corollary, at: "It is also shown from those words," etc. In the fourth and last he returns to his matter and shows his exposition to be good, through the authority of Augustine, at: "And that this is how it must be understood," etc.

### Notes

- **Opening (Doubt III continued):** The chunk opens with the completion of Bonaventure's response to Doubt III — the Priscian-based distinction between immediate/appositional and mediate construction of "God the Father." Then Praepositivus's alternative solution (using the confused vs. determinate supposition of the relative pronoun) is presented and assessed. The "moderns'" solution via simple/personal relation of the relative pronoun is given as the cleaner resolution.
- **Doubt IV — "One God is three persons":** Resolution using the unique supposition of "God" — because it partakes of both common and discrete/singular modes, it can stand simultaneously for many persons without yielding the illegitimate inference that Father is Son. Predication here is *per identitatem* (by identity), not *per inhaerentiam* (by inherence as accident in subject).
- **Doubt V — "Trinity" as collective vs. "one and only" as partitive:** Resolution: "one and only God" discretes the nature from others, not any person from another; and in God nature = the thing of nature = supposit, so the Trinity is truly predicated of God by identity.
- **Doubts VI–IX:** Brief responses. Doubt VII is philosophically notable — "another self" (*alterum se*) is not contradictory because identity-in-nature and otherness-in-supposit coexist without opposition in God, since opposition requires sameness of respect. Doubt IX distinguishes *alius/alia* (personal otherness, masculine/feminine) from *aliud* (essential otherness, neuter): the Son is *alius* (another person) but not *aliud* (another thing in essence).
- **Distinction V — the Lombard's base text:** A substantial *catena* from Augustine (*De Fide et Symbolo*, *De Trinitate*, *Contra Maximinum*, *De Fide ad Petrum*) and Hilary (*De Trinitate* IV, V, IX, XII) on whether "essence" can be subject or object of generation. The Lombard's solution: "Son from the substance of the Father" means the Son who is *from* the Father *is* that same substance — not that essence generates or is generated as a term of the generative act. Augustine's key gloss (*Against Maximin*): "the Trinity is of one substance because the Son is not from any matter or from nothing, but from the one from whom He was begotten."
- **Distinction V Division of Text and Question I opening:** Bonaventure's *Divisio Textus* maps the Lombard's complex structure (two main parts, each with four sub-parts) and introduces the four questions of Article I. Question I (whether essence generates) opens with three arguments *contra*; the chunk ends mid-argument 3.
