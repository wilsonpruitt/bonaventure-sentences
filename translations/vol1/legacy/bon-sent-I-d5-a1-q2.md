And for the understanding of what has been said, it must be noted that beyond the aforesaid three ways in which the preposition "from" (*de*) is taken, it can also be taken in a fourth way — namely substantially — so as to denote substantial agreement between the extremes; but this it never denotes purely, since it is a preposition and imports some relation and respect to the extremes. For if it were held only substantially, then just as it is said "the son from the substance of the father," so it would be said "the father from the substance of the son" — which is absurd.

Therefore it must be noted that "substantially" is customarily taken in three ways. Sometimes it is taken substantially and partially — as when it is said that the parts are from the whole or from the substance of the whole, as a jugful of wine from the cask. Sometimes it is taken substantially and ordinally — as when it is said "the body of Christ is made from bread"; for there is order there, because the substance of the bread does not remain in the body of Christ, just as morning does not remain in noon; but furthermore substantially, because the substance of bread passes into the substance of the body of Christ. Sometimes it is taken substantially and originally — as when it is said "the Son from the substance of the Father." By reason of the ablative it is held substantially; by reason of the genitive, originally: and therefore it imports some distinction of the Son with respect to the Father — not with respect to His substance — and thus the preposition holds there both general and special signification.

QUESTION I.

Whether substance or essence is generated.

That substance is generated is argued thus:

1. In lower things generation is a movement toward substance; what is of movement belongs to imperfection, what is toward substance belongs to perfection; if therefore what belongs to perfection is to be transferred to divine things: therefore generation also terminates at essence.

2. Likewise, generation in divine things terminates: either therefore at substance or at accident, because every being is either substance or accident; but in divine things it does not terminate at accident, since there is no accident there: therefore it terminates at substance. ### Notes

- **Opening (Q. II arguments 1–4 *pro* and four *contra*):** The chunk opens with the four arguments for the proposition "the Son is generated from the substance of the Father," followed by four objections *contra*. All translated.
- **The four-way analysis of "from" (*de*):** The heart of the *Conclusio*. Bonaventure notes that the preposition *de* has three established senses (material, ordinal, original) — and then adds a fourth: *substantialiter*. But this fourth sense never operates in pure isolation; it always combines with another. Three compound modes: (a) substantially + partially (part from a whole — wine from a cask); (b) substantially + ordinally (Eucharistic transubstantiation — body of Christ from bread); (c) substantially + originally (Son from the substance of the Father — ablative gives the substantial consubstantiality, genitive gives the relational origin). Mode (c) is precisely what Trinitarian usage requires: the Son is from the Father *as* consubstantial with Him *and* having origin from Him.
- **Transitive prepositions (reply 1–2):** The standard scholastic grammar point (Priscian, Scotus on speculative grammar): prepositions like *de* are called "transitive" because they join two grammatically distinct terms — but "transitive" in grammar does not necessarily mean "ontologically diverse." Oblique cases are transitively construed without implying real distinction of the referents (as "I see myself" has a reflexive structure despite formal transitivity).
- ***De* vs. *a* (reply 3):** Bonaventure's nuanced point: *a* (*from*) denotes the active principle exclusively (the Father as active source), while *de* (*from/out of*) denotes consubstantiality together with origin. Hence "the Son from (*de*) the substance of the Father" says both that He shares the Father's substance and that He has His origin from the Father — something *a* alone cannot express. The expression is thus not merely permissible but theologically richer than *a*.
- **Article II begins:** The second major article (generation relative to essence as *terminus*, not as *principium*) opens. Its two questions — (1) whether substance/essence is generated; (2) whether it is communicated through generation — address the *term* of the generative act, whereas Article I concerned the *principle*. The chunk ends mid-argument 3 of Question I.
