### English

DIST. VI. SOLE ARTICLE, QUESTION II.

---

QUESTION II.

Whether the generation in divine things is according to the account of will.

Second, it is inquired whether the generation of the Son is according to the account of will. And that it is not, is shown first by authorities, second by arguments.

First, it is shown by the authority of Hilary, who says in the book *On the Synods*: "Will brought substance to all creatures, but nature gave it to the Son."

2. Likewise, Augustine in the fifteenth book *On the Trinity* reproves Eunomius, who held that the Son of God is the Son of will: therefore, if he reproves rightly, the generation of the Son is not according to the condition of will.

3. Likewise, it is shown by arguments as follows: Generation, of itself, is a natural procession or one proceeding by the mode of nature; but this is a different mode of producing than by will: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, the Son is the wisdom of the Father: therefore if he proceeds according to the account of will, then will is prior to wisdom; but this is unfitting, because cognition according to the natural order of understanding precedes affection or will: therefore, etc.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. In every ordered nature, what is posited naturally is subject to the will, or at least conformed to the will, as is evident in the first man; but in God the nature is most perfectly ordered: therefore nothing is from nature or through nature that is not also through will: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, the mode of proceeding in the created image and in the uncreated Trinity is analogous; but in the image, knowledge arises from the mind through the mediation of will: therefore the Son too proceeds from the Father through the mediation of will or by will. The minor premise is shown by Augustine in the ninth book *On the Trinity*: "An appetite precedes the birth of the mind, through which, as we find by seeking what we will to know, the offspring is born, namely knowledge itself."

3. Likewise, where there is greater communication, there is greater liberality; but the Father communicates more to the Son than to all creatures: therefore there is greater liberality there: therefore, since creatures proceed by the mode of liberality, much more so the Son, and so on.

4. Likewise, in creatures in the act of generation, both nature and will move simultaneously, and nothing is thereby detracted from the offspring: therefore if the Father produces according to his whole self more so than a creature does, then much more so through both nature and will.

5. Likewise, for this there is the authority of Hilary in the third book *On the Trinity*: "Before all time the Father begot the Son, imparting to his nativity all that he is as God through love"; if through love: therefore through will.

6. Likewise, Origen, speaking of the divine mind, says: "Bringing forth a shoot of will, he becomes the Father of the Word": therefore it seems that the Son is generated by the Father through will and that he is the Son of will.

---

*The generation of the Son is according to will in the account of approving, but not in the account of producing, except insofar as will is a principle conjoined to nature, and indeed in such a way that nature produces principally, with will accompanying.*

I respond: It must be said that will can be considered in two ways with respect to what is willed: either in the account of approving and loving, and in this way it stands with respect to every good, whether necessary or contingent, whether created or uncreated, whether from another — which is what is meant when some things are said to be from nature and others from will — or as conjoined.

If as a distinct principle, then will is distinguished in two ways, namely as supervening and as antecedent. The supervening will is that by which one passes from not-willing to willing; this is not in God, neither with respect to God nor with respect to creatures. For God has no new will either with respect to himself or with respect to another. The antecedent will precedes the effect in causality and duration; this indeed is in God, but not with respect to God, but only with respect to creatures; for all the divine persons exist simultaneously.

In another way, as will is considered as a principle conjoined to nature, it can be twofold; for then nature and will together are the principle; and either nature is the producing principle principally, with will accompanying, or conversely; for both cannot be the principal principle simultaneously.

If will is the principal principle, with nature accompanying, thus is the procession of the Holy Spirit, who proceeds by the mode of love, yet alike in nature. But if nature is the primary principle, with will accompanying, thus is the generation of the Son, who is produced as wholly alike and by the mode of nature, and nonetheless as beloved; and therefore it is said that the Father is well pleased in him, and in Colossians 1 the Son is called the Son of love.

1. To the objection on the contrary side, that generation is by the mode of will or according to the account of will: it must be said that this is to be understood either of the approving will, as the two authorities of Hilary and Origen show, or of the accompanying will, as the argument placed first indicates.

2. To the objection concerning the likeness of the image: it must be said that in this respect the created image is unlike the uncreated Trinity, as Augustine says in the fifteenth book *On the Trinity*, because there the word is not born through inquiry, as it can be born in us; whence this argument tends rather to the opposite of what is proposed than to the proposed conclusion.

3. To the objection that there is greater communication in the generation of the Son, etc.: it must be said that greater or lesser communication does not make the mode of emanating be according to liberality or according to the mode of will, but rather the mode or ground of communicating does. And because the Father communicates to creatures through will, such that his will is the producing cause, but to the Son through the fecundity of nature: therefore the Son is not said to be produced through will in the same manner as creatures.

4. To the objection that in human generation nature and will move simultaneously: it must be said that this is owing to a deficiency rather than to completeness; because a father cannot generate by himself, but only through conjunction with another who is distant, which comes about at the command of the will; but God has no need of the assistance of another; and therefore the cases are not parallel.

### Notes

- **Structural position**: This question is the second of three in the Sole Article of Distinction 6, following the question on necessity (Q.1) and preceding the question on exemplarity (Q.3). The chunk opens with cross-references to other doctors (Alexander of Hales, Scotus, Thomas, Albert, etc.) that belong to the scholion of Q.1; these have been omitted as critical apparatus.

- **"approving will" vs. "accompanying will" (*voluntas approbans* / *voluntas concomitans*)**: The *Conclusio* turns on this distinction. The will approves and loves every good (necessary or contingent); the will accompanies but does not principally cause the generation of the Son. This is distinguished from will as the *principal* productive principle, which would assimilate the Son's procession to that of creatures or of the Spirit.

- **"supervening will" (*voluntas accedens*)**: The will *de novo* arising, passing from non-willing to willing — categorically excluded from God due to divine immutability. Rendered "supervening" to capture the technical sense of *accedens* (something newly added or coming upon).

- **"antecedent will" (*voluntas antecedens*)**: Precedes the effect in causality and duration. Present in God but only with respect to creatures, not with respect to the divine persons themselves (who are simultaneous). The anecdotal disputed question quoted in the Scholion adds "accompanying and accepting will" (*voluntas concomitans et acceptans*) as the third mode, explicitly applicable to God with respect to both himself and creatures.

- **Holy Spirit vs. Son — mode of procession**: The *Respondeo* articulates a formal distinction that will be treated at length in D.10–11 and D.26: the Spirit proceeds *per modum amoris* (will as principal principle, nature accompanying), the Son *per modum naturae* (nature as principal principle, will accompanying). This asymmetry is foundational to Bonaventure's Trinitarian theology.

- **Colossians 1 reference**: "Son of love" (*Filius caritatis*) — Col. 1:13 (*in regnum Filii caritatis suae*). Rendered "Son of love" to match the Vulgate phrasing and Bonaventure's usage.

- **Reply to argument 2 (the image analogy)**: Bonaventure's response is notable: the Augustinian analogy of the mind producing knowledge through desire (cited in the *sed contra*) actually cuts *against* applying a will-mediated model to the Son's generation, because in God there is no discursive inquiry. The direction of the argument is inverted.

I. tr. 7. q. 30. ni. 3. a. 2.* etc.) that are clearly the tail end of the Q.1 scholion apparatus; these have been omitted. Minor OCR noise (*soimio* for *solutio*, *homine* for *hominibus* in context) corrected silently.
