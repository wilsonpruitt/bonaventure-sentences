QUESTION III.

Whether the power of generating and the power of creating are one single power.

Third, it is inquired whether the power to generate and the power to create are one single power; and that they are not one, seems to be shown as follows.

1. Augustine: "The Father is Father by one thing and God by another": therefore he is generating by one thing and creating by another; but the power of generating is that by which he generates, and the power of creating is that by which he creates: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, powers are distinguished by their acts and acts by their objects; therefore if generation is terminated at God and creation at a creature, and these are altogether different: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, powers are multiplied according to subjects, since in many powerful agents there are many powers; but the power of creating — or the power to create — is in the Son, whereas the power to generate is not in him: therefore they are not one single power.

4. Likewise, whatever things are such that one can be understood without the other being understood, and conversely, are not one; but the power to generate can be understood while the power to create is set aside, and conversely the power to create while the power to generate is set aside: therefore, etc.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. Of one altogether simple powerful agent, there is only one single power; but the Father is altogether simple: therefore he has only one single power; but he has the power to generate and the power to create: therefore these two are one single power.

2. Likewise, if the power to generate and the power to create were different, since the power to generate regards a person and the power to create regards nature: therefore nature and person would be two distinct things: therefore neither would nature be predicated of person nor conversely, which is altogether false.

3. Likewise, if there is a distinction between the power to generate and the power to create, there is an order between them. I ask, therefore, what is prior according to the account of understanding. And that the power of generating is prior seems to follow, because it is with respect to the eternal, and the eternal is before the temporal. But on the contrary: the understanding of what is common is prior to the understanding of what is proper; but the power of creating expresses what is common to the three persons, while the power of generating expresses what is proper to the Father: therefore, etc.

---

*The power of generating and the power of creating differ not in reality but only according to the account of understanding.*

I respond: It must be said that the power to generate and the power to create are one single power, yet expressed in two ways. For it is one being in reality to be Father and to be God, yet differing according to the account of understanding; because the latter is absolute, when I say "to be God," and the former is relative, when I say "to be Father." And the same is to be understood concerning power. Hence, just as essence and person are one in reality, yet there is a difference of account in understanding and in expression, so likewise must it be said of the power of generating and the power of creating.

And just as there is there a difference not in reality but according to account, so too there is there an order according to the account of understanding. Hence they have one order and another according to different comparisons. For comparing the power to create and the power to generate to that of which they are powers: since the power to create belongs to nature and the power to generate belongs to the person, and the understanding of what is common is prior to the understanding of what is proper, thus without dispute the power of creating is prior according to the account of understanding. But comparing them to that at which they are terminated: since the power of creating regards the temporal and the power of generating regards the eternal, the power of generating is prior according to the account of understanding relative to the power of creating. And thus the objections concerning order are answered.

Therefore the arguments showing that the power to generate and the power to create do not differ in reality or in essence are to be conceded.

### Notes

- **Chunk structure**: Q.III is complete except for a lacuna in reply to objection 1 (the reply to "alio est Pater, alio est Deus" is missing from the OCR — the text jumps from the concession statement directly to reply 3). Q.IV begins but its *sed contra* is cut off at the end of the chunk; the opening arguments for the negative position also appear to be missing their first item (the OCR begins at argument 2).

- **One power, two expressions (*unum posse, dupliciter dictum*)**: The *Conclusio* applies the standard essence/person distinction to the domain of power: just as being Father and being God are one reality expressed under two accounts (*rationes*), so generating and creating are one power considered under a personal and an essential description respectively. This is a direct application of the principle established in Q.I.

- **Order of the two powers**: Bonaventure's resolution of the third *sed contra* argument (which asks which power is prior) is elegant: the answer depends on the direction of comparison. Relative to their *subjects* (nature vs. person), the power of creating is conceptually prior because the common (essence) is understood before the proper (paternity). Relative to their *termini* (temporal vs. eternal), the power of generating is conceptually prior because the eternal precedes the temporal. Neither order is real; both are *secundum rationem intelligendi*.

- **Q.III reply 3 — proper vs. improper subject**: The Scholion elaborates this reply: the *proximate and adequate subject* of the creative power is the divine essence alone; the divine persons are only *remote* subjects. Therefore the axiom "powers are multiplied by subjects" does not apply here.

- **Q.III reply 4 — signified vs. connoted (*significatum / connotatum*)**: A standard logical distinction. What is *connoted* (the object or terminus — creature vs. divine person) can be understood separately; but the *principal signification* (the single divine power as such) cannot be split, any more than person can be understood in isolation from essence.

- **Q.IV context**: The question shifts from active to passive power — not "can the Father generate?" but "is the capacity *to be generated* and the capacity *to be created* the same kind of capacity?" This is a more abstract question about whether passive potency is univocal across the Trinitarian and creaturely orders. The *sed contra* argument from *res* (common to *fruibile* and *utibile*) draws on the Bonaventurian teaching from D.1 on the enjoyment/use distinction.
