I respond: It must be said that generation, by its common account, terminates at substance. But it must be noted that substance is said in two ways: primary substance — which is the individual, hypostasis, or person — and secondary substance — which is what is common. I say therefore that the term of generation can be spoken of in two ways: either with respect to production, or with respect to intention. With respect to production — since it concerns the singular — generation terminates at primary substance; and with respect to intention, at the common nature, because the nature producing this human being intends to give him the common form.

But since in creatures the common form is numbered in supposits, therefore in them the common form is produced and corrupted; and therefore in creatures generation terminates not only according to intention but also according to production at the common substance; and therefore the universal is generated in the singular, because it is there numbered. But since in divine things the substance is not numbered and no new being accrues to it: therefore generation according to production terminates only at primary substance, which is the person — for person, according to Boethius, "is the individual substance of a rational nature." And since only that is properly said to be generated at which generation terminates according to the account of producing: therefore the person is generated, not the substance or essence. And therefore this is not admitted: "the essence is generated."

And thus all the objections on both sides are clear — for they proceed along different lines.

QUESTION II.

Whether the divine substance or essence is communicated through generation.

Finally it is asked whether substance is communicated through generation; and that it is, is shown thus:

1. A thing is communicated through that by which it comes to be in many; but substance is in many persons and this is only through generation and through procession: therefore it is communicated through generation.

2. Likewise, a cause is that by whose positing the thing is posited and by whose removal it is removed: but by positing emanation in divine things, commonness and distinction are posited; by removing generation or emanation, the essence would be in only one — as a hypostasis is: therefore generation is the account of communicating the essence.

3. Likewise, what is given to someone and does not cease to be held by the giver — through that by which it is given, it is communicated; but the substance is given by the Father to the Son through generation and does not cease to be held by the Father when given to the Son: therefore it is communicated through generation.

4. Likewise, in lower things generation is the account of communicating substance or nature; but in divine things the nature is far more communicable, because it is simpler: therefore in divine things the substance or essence is communicated through generation.

On the contrary: 1. Generation is the principle of distinguishing; but the principle of distinguishing and the principle of communicating are not the same: therefore through generation there is no account of communicating in divine things: therefore through generation nothing is communicated.

2. Likewise, what causes something to be in a certain thing is not the account of communicating but of appropriating and individuating; but generation causes the substance to be in a certain person — namely in the person of the Son: therefore it is not the account of communicating, but of appropriating.

3. Likewise, everything that is communicated through generation is common through generation. If therefore substance is communicated through generation, then with generation removed it will not be common — therefore it is proper; but what is proper cannot be communicated through generation: therefore neither can substance.

4. Likewise, what is communicated through generation is given and received through generation; and everything of that kind, if it is not accidental to generation, is generated; but substance does not accrue accidentally to generation: therefore, etc.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. I, arguments 3–4 *pro* and *contra* 1–4):** The chunk opens with the final two arguments *for* the generation of substance, then four arguments *against*. Translated seamlessly.
- **Primary / secondary substance (Aristotelian framework):** Bonaventure deploys Aristotle's distinction (*Categories*) between primary substance (the individual, supposit, person) and secondary substance (the common nature, genus/species). Generation terminates *totally* at primary substance (with respect to production) and *formally* at secondary substance (with respect to intention) — but only in creatures does the secondary substance actually begin to exist *de novo*. In God, the essence never begins to exist anew when the Son is generated; therefore generation terminates only at primary substance in divine things.
- **"God is generated" but not "essence is generated":** The resolution turns on the concrete/abstract distinction from Distinction IV. "God" (*Deus*) is a concrete term signifying substance in concretion — it supposes for a person. Therefore "God generates God" and "God is generated" are true. "Essence" (*essentia*) is an abstract term that signifies and supposes the common nature — it is never generated, because no new being accrues to the essence; only a new person (new primary substance) comes to be.
- **Question II — communication of essence through generation:** The four arguments *pro* argue that since the essence is in multiple persons and this is *via* generation, generation must be the account of communicating the essence. The four *contra* argue that generation is the principle of *distinction*, not communication, and that what is communicated through generation must be generated — but essence is not generated. Bonaventure's *Conclusio* (begun but not completed in the chunk) will distinguish two senses of "common" (communicable vs. actually communicated) and argue that generation is the account of the essence's actual communication while not making the essence a *term* of generation.
- **Boethius citation:** "A person is the individual substance of a rational nature" (*De Persona et duabus naturis* III) — the standard scholastic definition of person, used to anchor the conclusion that generation terminates at person, not essence.
- **Against Joachim of Fiore:** The Scholion notes that both conclusions (essence does not generate; essence is not generated) are directed against the heresy of Joachim, condemned at Lateran IV (1215), who had argued that Peter Lombard's teaching implied a *quaternitas* (Father, Son, Spirit, and the common essence as a fourth entity). Bonaventure's careful distinction between primary and secondary substance, and between generation as productive term vs. intentional term, is designed to uphold both the unity of essence and the genuine reality of Trinitarian distinction.
