And indeed the potency of matter is less removed from matter than active power is from substance; for the potency of matter is a passive potency, which denotes an ordering toward another with privation, while active power denotes an ordering with a positive act — and therefore the potency of matter adds less beyond matter than active power adds beyond substance. 5. To the objection concerning accidental form: it must be said that an accidental form is not its own potency. For its potency is its capacity to flow into another; yet that potency does not add as much as the potency of a substantial form, since the potency of an accidental form denotes an ordering toward act, but not one sufficient through itself, but rather through the power of substance. ARTICLE II.

On the second assignment of the image.

Next there is a question about the second assignment of the image through mind, knowledge, and love. And concerning this three things are asked.

The first is about these absolutely — namely whether the image is found in them as in powers, or in habits, or in powers and habits together, or in substance and habits.

The second is about these in comparison to one another.

The third is whether this trinity of the image necessarily leads to knowledge of the Trinity with respect to the persons.

QUESTION I.

Whether the image is found in mind, knowledge, and love as in powers, or as in habits, or in both together, or according to substance and habits.

Concerning the first the discussion proceeds thus.

1. It is shown first that the image is not found in these as in powers, because the foregoing assignment was in the powers: therefore if this one were also in powers, there would be nothing but a repetition of words.

Moreover, knowledge and love do not denote a power but habits, although mind can denote a power: therefore the aforesaid three cannot be posited under the account of powers.

2. Likewise, it is shown that they do not denote habits, because Augustine says in his assignment and approbation of the image: "The mind knows itself, loves itself"; but no habit's act is to know itself or to love itself: therefore, etc.

Likewise, if mind stands for a habit, I ask for which habit. If for the habit of memory — which seems more likely, since no other can be given — but the act of this habit is to remember, and no other; yet Augustine assigns to mind these acts, namely knowing and loving: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, it is shown that the image is not in powers and habits together. For since the powers are three and the habits are three, there would not be a ternary but a senary.

4. Likewise, I ask for which power mind stands in that formula. For it stands either for all, or for two, or for one. If for all, then there is no trinity there; if for two, then there is a quaternity, since there are two habits; if for one, then it cannot have those two acts — knowing and loving: therefore the trinity is not taken according to habits and powers together.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. III replies 3–6, continued):** The chunk opens completing the replies to Question III's objections. Reply 3 establishes that being and operating must have distinct proximate principles (form and potency respectively), since otherwise a thing would always be operating whenever it exists. Reply 4 distinguishes the potency of matter (passive, with privation) from the active potency of substance — the latter "adds" more to substance than the former adds to matter. Reply 5 argues that accidental forms also depend on the substance's power to operate, so the analogy between heat's power to heat and the soul's powers fails (it would only show dependence, not identity). Reply 6 dismisses the argument from *rational/sensible/vegetable* being substantial differences.
- **"Destruction of the premise" (interemptio):** Bonaventure uses the scholastic term *interemptio* for the refutation of an argument by denying one of its premises outright (as opposed to *distinctio*, which distinguishes a true from a false reading). The powers and the soul are not in diverse genera but in the same genus by reduction — so the premise of the final contrary argument is simply false. The Scholion explains this term via Peter of Spain's *Summulae*.
- **Article II — second assignment of the image:** The structure of Distinction III Part II shifts here. Article I concerned the first Augustinian assignment (memory/understanding/will as the soul's three powers). Article II concerns the second assignment (*De Trinitate* IX): mind (*mens*), knowledge (*notitia*), and love (*amor*) — taken not as distinct powers but as the soul knowing and loving itself. Three questions follow: (1) are these powers, habits, or what? (2) their mutual comparison; (3) whether this trinity proves the personal Trinity.
- **Question I — four options rejected in the arguments:** A formally elegant dialectical structure presents four possible interpretations of mind/knowledge/love and eliminates each: (a) pure powers — ruled out because it would duplicate Article I's treatment; (b) pure habits — ruled out because Augustine assigns self-knowledge and self-love to *mens* itself, not to a habit; (c) powers plus habits — ruled out because three powers + three habits = six, not three; (d) substance plus habits — ruled out because habits are temporally acquired while the image is co-created and perpetual. Bonaventure's own solution (not yet reached in this chunk) will locate the image in the soul's substance as known and loved by itself.
- **Chunk ends mid-argument:** The chunk ends mid-sentence in the second argument against the fourth option (substance + habits). The *Conclusio* and full resolution of Article II, Question I are in the next chunk.
