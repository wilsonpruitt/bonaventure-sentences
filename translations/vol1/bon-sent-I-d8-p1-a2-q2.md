4, 5, 6. To the objection concerning incorruptibility: it must be said, as has been touched on above, that this belongs to creatures; for some creatures are so simple and so united by a good conjunction that no contrariety falls in them, and there is in them no greater ground of corruption than there is in the primary principles. Hence just as the primary principles cannot be resolved into anything, yet they would lapse into nothing if left to themselves; so is it to be understood with respect to certain substances. Hence it is not to be said as simply true that every composite is actually resolvable; but as Anselm says, "it is resolvable either in reality or in understanding." Hence the arguments adduced for this are to be conceded.

7, 8. To the objections concerning mutability (*vertibilitas*): it must be said that every creature is mutable by nature if left to itself.

If the cause of this is asked, it must be said that for this mutability — since it is a pure defect — no efficient or reducing cause leading to non-being is to be assigned, but only a deficient cause. For this reason it must be noted that "nature" is said to be natural origin. Now the origin of a creature is both from nothing and from its own principles: accordingly, something is said to be natural to a creature in two ways — either because it belongs to it from the fact that it is from nothing, or because it belongs to it from the fact that it is from its own principles. And because nothing is not any thing's efficient cause but only its deficient cause, therefore the properties that belong to a creature by reason of its being from nothing are not positive determinations but defects, and are not from a power but from the deficiency of power, and have no efficient cause but only a deficient cause. Such are vanity, instability, and mutability. If therefore it is asked from what source a creature is mutable, I say: not from any efficient cause, but through a deficiency in itself. But according to the sense in which "natural" means what belongs to a thing through its own proper and intrinsic principles, privations and defects are not said to belong to it naturally, but only capacities; and therefore in this sense of "natural," no creature is naturally mutable into non-being — yet it is not said to be naturally immutable either, because "natural" refers to what a thing's nature can bring about; but the principles of a thing are not capable of conserving the thing or of conserving themselves; and therefore immutability is not natural in this sense. Yet neither is it contrary to nature — on the contrary, it is consonant with nature; because every nature desires to be preserved, although it cannot be so of itself, and most especially that creature which desires to be beatified, namely that which is made to the image of God. And because the desire of nature is not in vain where nature falls short, the gratuitous inflow of God supplies what is lacking. And thus it is clear that mutability belongs by nature, but immutability belongs by grace.

9. To the objection concerning the comparison of grace to nature: it must be understood that grace is called an assistance coming from above, with respect to what is beyond the capacity of nature. This assistance is twofold: either with respect to being simply, or with respect to perfect being.

If with respect to being simply — for instance, with respect to the conservation of being, since no primary principles can, being as they are vain things, conserve themselves of themselves — then it does not operate through any infused or given habit. Because it regards being, and because being is common to all things, this grace is common to all. Hence this is grace having the mode of nature, and this is the grace by which the rest of things are said to be immutable.

In the other way, grace is called an assistance with respect to perfect being, and since the perfection of being belongs to those things ordered toward beatitude, it regards well-being and does not belong to all things. Therefore this is a special habit of some, not of all, and this is grace in the mode of grace properly speaking. From these considerations what is objected regarding grace is clear: for the objection proceeds according to grace understood as a special habit distinguished against nature, because in that sense it denotes something newly created; but grace in the sense just described does not.

10. And from this the final objection is also answered: because grace is called an assistance with respect to what is beyond the capacity of nature; and because the conservation of the primary principles is beyond the capacity of nature, while the going-forth of operations is not — indeed, it is below it — therefore the matter is clear, etc.

- **"mutability" (*vertibilitas*) and "immutability" (*invertibilitas*)**: The central terms. *Vertibilitas* (literally "turnability") is Bonaventure's technical term for the creature's inherent capacity to lapse into non-being or to undergo change; *invertibilitas* for resistance to such lapse. The distinction maps onto the pair *ex nihilo / ex principiis propriis* — what belongs to a creature from its creaturely origin (from nothing) vs. what belongs to it from its own intrinsic constitutive principles.

- **"from nothing as deficient cause"**: A characteristic Bonaventurian metaphysical move. *Ex nihilo* does not imply that nothing is an efficient cause of defects in creatures; rather, nothing is a *deficient cause* — it is the privative ground of instability, vanity, and mutability. These are not posited by God's creative act but reflect the creature's own ontological poverty as something that was not. This is closely related to Bonaventure's teaching on *vestigium* and the creature's intrinsic finitude.

- **Twofold grace (*duplex adiutorium*)**: The reply to objection 9 introduces an important distinction between (1) grace in the mode of nature (*gratia habens modum naturae*) — universal divine conservation, by which all creatures are preserved in being and thus in a secondary sense "immutable"; and (2) grace in the mode of grace properly speaking — the special infused habit ordered toward beatitude, belonging to rational creatures alone. The first corresponds roughly to what later theology calls *concursus* or *conservatio*; the second to *gratia gratum faciens*. This distinction resolves the objection that immutability, if from grace, must be a special and divisible gift rather than universal.

- **"desire of nature is not in vain" (*desiderium naturae non est frustra*)**: A standard axiom of scholastic teleology, here applied to the creature's desire for conservation and beatification. Where natural desire exceeds natural capacity, divine grace supplies the deficit. This grounds the immutability of spiritual creatures in God's gracious sustenance rather than their own ontological resources.

- **"the empyrean by reason of its contents"**: The opening fragment (before the reply to objections 4–6) belongs to a reply to an objection apparently concerning whether the empyrean heaven is immutable or incorruptible by reason of its simplicity or by reason of what it contains (i.e., the blessed). This is characteristic of the cosmological discussion of incorruptibility that likely featured in the missing *Respondeo*.
