DIST. V. DOUBTS. (continued)
DOUBT IV.
"Likewise it is not to be said that the divine essence begot the Son." Joachim objects against this — both against the position and against the argument. Against the position: because if essence neither generates nor is generated nor proceeds, then in divine things there is a thing that generates, a thing that is begotten, a thing that proceeds, and a thing that neither generates nor is begotten nor proceeds; and so there is a quaternity, if there are four things.
Likewise, he mocks the Master's argument: if essence generates essence and essence is one thing — therefore one thing generates itself. Similarly, Joachim says, you could have said, Peter: "God generates God, and there is one God: therefore the same thing generates itself."
I respond: It must be said that Joachim does not argue correctly, and his argument fails, because "thing" (res) is not taken uniformly — for when "thing" is said in the first way, "thing" is taken there for a thing of nature; but when it is said in the second way, it is taken for the divine nature itself. Furthermore, his argument fails from an insufficient premise — because it does not hold that if something is said of one thing and not of another, those things therefore constitute a number. Hence this does not hold: "Peter is an individual; man is not an individual: therefore Peter and man are two." For having a property and not having it is not sufficient for distinction.
To his counter-instance it must be said that he does not counter-instance correctly — because "essence" is one thing with respect to both supposit and signification, for it does not suppose for the persons; but "God" is one thing with respect to signification yet many with respect to supposit. And therefore Joachim wrongly reproaches the Master; and because, being a simple man, he was not respectful toward the Master, his booklet was by the just judgment of God condemned at the Lateran Council, and the position of the Master approved.
DOUBT V.
Likewise, a question is raised concerning what Hilary says: "The Son has nothing except what He received by being born." A question is therefore asked about this word "received" — whether it denotes substance or relation. If substance: therefore the Father similarly received; if relation: therefore since the Son received the essence, the essence is received — therefore the essence is referred to something.
I respond: It must be said that "to receive" denotes two things — namely to have and to be from another; with respect to "having" it regards the essence; but with respect to "being from another" it regards the person: hence through the verb "to receive" it is signified that the essence is had by the person who is from another.
DOUBT VI.
Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "And therefore what subsists in one from one is not referred to another" — because this seems false: for the Son subsists from the Father, and yet He is referred to the Holy Spirit.
I respond: This can be understood in two ways. In one way, that the Son has relation only to one person; and this is understood insofar as He is Son — because insofar as He is spirating, He is referred to another. Or it can mean that the person of the Son has relation only to one nature; and this is true, because the Son has in Himself only the nature of the Father.
DOUBT VII.
Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "And the immutable God follows, as it were, His own nature." For in divine things there is no before or after, and therefore neither preceding nor following. If you say this is according to the account of understanding — this amounts to nothing, because God the generating one follows nothing either according to thing or according to intellect.
I respond: It must be said that Hilary speaks improperly, and therefore adds the qualification "as it were"; and he means by "following" only this — to accompany inseparably, to be associated with, and not to depart from it; and this is clear through...
DOUBT VIII.
Likewise, a question is raised concerning the authorities of Augustine — first concerning what he says: that God begot the Son semel (at once/once). For it seems to say "semel" badly, because "semel" denotes alternation; but in the eternal generation no alternation obtains: therefore, etc.
I respond: It must be said that "semel" can denote the now of time or the now of eternity. And if it denotes the now of time — since time has diverse nows, it denotes interruption. But if the now of eternity — and that now is always invariable and one — then "semel" denotes complete invariability, perfection, and unity.
DOUBT IX.
Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: "Son of His love" — whether "love" is taken there essentially or notionally. If essentially: therefore Christ is the Son of the essence — which is not conceded. If you say the expression is improper and the sense is "Son of the essence" — that is, the one who is the essence — then no sense seems to remain, and the addition "of love" (caritatis) seems to be for nothing. And furthermore, Augustine explains "Son of His love" as "Son of the beloved"; but the Father loves the Son through the Holy Spirit: therefore, etc. If for this reason you say it is taken notionally — then the Son of God is the Son of the Holy Spirit, which is altogether absurd.
I respond: Some say that the genitive there is taken neither properly essentially nor properly notionally, but in a middle way — that is, by appropriation. For although love (caritas) is common to all and proper to the Holy Spirit, in one way it belongs to the Father by appropriation — because love is gratuitous love; and so says Richard, because gratuitous love is that which only gives and receives nothing — and this belongs to the person of the Father, and is therefore appropriated to the Father. In another way it can be said that "love" there is taken essentially, as Augustine says — that "Son of His love" means nothing other than "Son of His substance"; but the genitive is construed not in the account of a principle, but by virtue of declaration of essence; and the sense is: "Son of love" — that is, "beloved Son"; and "Son of substance or nature" — that is, "consubstantial and natural Son."
DOUBT X.
Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: that the Son is of the substance of the Father, homoousios — because since a natural son in lower things draws part of his substance from the father and part from the mother, while in God the Son draws the whole substance from the Father — it seems that the Father ought to be called not only father but also mother, and far more fittingly mother, since the mother gives more than the father. Likewise, the Son of God calls Himself wisdom and wisdom that is conceived and brought forth, in Proverbs eight. Therefore since this is proper to a mother, it seems the Father should more properly be called mother than father.
I respond: It must be said that the name "mother" is not transferred to divine things. And one reason is given by Anselm in the Monologion: because the maternal principle requires another prior principle. The account of this is that the mother is a passive principle, and every such principle is moved by another — therefore before it there is another principle. Since therefore the principle of the Son's generation is first and purely actual, the maternal principle is in no way transferred; the maternal acts are transferred, however — such as conceiving and giving birth — because what two principles do here, one principle does there.
DOUBT XI.
Likewise, a question is raised concerning what he says: that the Son and the Holy Spirit are not from nothing. For this seems false, because what is from no pre-existing matter is from nothing; but the Son and the Holy Spirit are such: therefore, etc. If you say that the Son and the Holy Spirit are from something — such as from the Father — then it is asked whether the Father is from nothing; and it seems so, because "something" and "nothing" convert; but the Father is not from something: therefore He is from nothing. Likewise, because the Father has no efficient principle, He is said to be from nothing; therefore since He has no matter, He ought to be said to be from nothing. If this is conceded of the Father — on the contrary: everything that has being from nothing is created: therefore, etc.
I respond: It must be said that when something is said to be from nothing, according to Anselm in the Monologion it can be understood in three ways. In one way, that "nothing" is taken simply in a privative or negative sense — as when it is said of one who is silent: "this man speaks of nothing." In a second way, positively — as if something were said to come into being from nothing as a knife is from iron. In a third way, partly positively and partly privatively — as when something is said to come into being from nothing because after nothing it is something, as is said: "from a pauper he becomes rich." The account of this multiplicity is the following. For first it must be distinguished that the negation of "nothing" can remain within itself or be carried over to the verb "to come into being." If carried over to the verb, then "to come into being from nothing" means "not to come into being from something" — as "to speak of nothing" means "to speak of no thing." If however it is not carried over to the verb, then "coming into being" is affirmed; and this is twofold — because "from" (de) can be understood materially, and then it is signified that nothing is the matter of something: this is the second sense. Likewise, it can be held ordinally — and then there is the third sense, as when a creature is said to come into being from nothing.
It must therefore be said that in the first sense God can be said to be from nothing — whether the Father or the divine essence; yet this mode is not in common use. With respect to the second mode, nothing at all comes into being from nothing, because nothing is the matter of nothing. With respect to the third mode — according to which Augustine speaks, that only a creature comes into being from nothing or is from nothing, because "from" there denotes order, so as to have being after non-being — "from nothing" there denies a pre-existing matter. Although therefore the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit have no pre-existing matter, nevertheless since they do not have being after non-being, they are therefore not said to be from nothing; and therefore the first argument does not hold, because it proceeds from an insufficient premise.
But the second and third arguments — made concerning the Father — proceed insofar as the negation of "nothing" is carried over outside to the verb and makes the statement negative — for otherwise "not to be from something" and "to be from nothing" would not be equivalent — and in this sense, just as it is conceded that the Father is from nothing, so too in this sense. Yet, as has been said, this sense is not in common use; for we ordinarily use the expression "from nothing" according as the negation remains within, and "from" there is taken ordinally.

DISTINCTION VI.
Whether the Father begot the Son by will or by necessity; and whether He is God willingly or unwillingly.
Furthermore, it is customarily asked whether the Father begot the Son by will or by necessity. Concerning this Orosius to Augustine says: "Did the Father beget the Son by will or by necessity? Neither by will nor by necessity — because necessity is not in God, and will cannot precede wisdom." "On this account," as Augustine says in the fifteenth book On the Trinity, "the dialectic of Eunomius must be laughed at — from whom the Eunomian heretics arose — who, since he could not understand it, nor was he willing to believe that the only-begotten Word of God is the Son of God by nature — that is, begotten from the substance of the Father — said that He is not the Son of nature or substance but the Son of the will of God, wishing to assert a will accruing to God, by which He would beget the Son, just as we sometimes will something that we did not will before; on account of which our nature is understood to be mutable — which God forbid we believe to be in God." Let us say therefore that the Word of God is the Son of God by nature and not by will, as Augustine teaches in the fifteenth book On the Trinity, where he commends a certain Catholic who responded to a most cunning heretic: "Someone responded shrewdly indeed to a most craftily questioning heretic — whether God the Father begot the Son willingly or unwillingly — so that if he said 'unwillingly,' the most absurd misery of God would follow; if however 'willingly,' the heretic would immediately conclude what he intended — namely that the Son is not of nature but of will. But that man most vigilantly asked in return whether God the Father is God willingly or unwillingly — since if he answered 'unwillingly,' a great absurdity and misery would follow, which it is great madness to believe about God; if however he said 'willingly,' it would follow — therefore He is God by His own will, not by nature." What then remained but that he fall silent, seeing himself bound by his own questioning with an insoluble bond? From the foregoing it is taught that it must not be conceded that God is God by will or by necessity, or willingly or unwillingly; likewise that He begot the Son by will or necessity, or willingly or unwillingly.
But against this it is objected thus: the will of God is the nature or essence of God, because to be and to will are not different for God; and therefore just as the essence of the three persons is one, so is the will one. If therefore God is God by nature, He is also God by will; and if the Word of God is the Son of God by nature, He is also the Son of God by will. But this is easy to refute. For even the foreknowledge or knowledge of God — by which He knows or foreknows good and evil — is the divine nature or essence; and the predestination or will of God is the same divine essence, and to know and to will are not different for God than to be. And yet although the knowledge and will of God are one and the same, nevertheless not everything that is said of the will is said of the knowledge, and conversely. Nor does God will by His will everything He knows by His knowledge — since His knowledge knows both good and evil, but His will wills only good. Indeed, the knowledge and foreknowledge of God are of good and evil, while the will and predestination are of good only; and yet in God knowledge and will, foreknowledge and predestination, are one and the same. So although the nature and will of God are one, yet the Father is said to have begotten the Son by nature and not by will, and to be God by nature and not by will.
Yet these aforesaid words — in which it was prudently said that God the Father is neither willingly nor unwillingly God, and neither willingly nor unwillingly begot the Son, whether by will or by necessity — seem to me to be taken in this sense: that we understand a preceding or accruing will, in the way Eunomius understood it. For He is not God by a preceding or efficient will, nor willing before He is God; nor did He beget the Son by a preceding or accruing will, nor did He beget the Son willing before generating, nor generating before willing. Yet He begot willingly, just as He begot powerfully and goodly and wisely and the like. For if the Father is said to have begotten the Son being wise and good, why not also willing — since it is the same for God to be willing as to be God, just as it is the same to be wise as to be God? Let us say therefore that the Father, as He is wise, so He is willing in having begotten the Son — but not by a preceding or accruing will. This sense is disclosed and confirmed by Jerome in the commentary On the Letter to the Ephesians: "Concerning the Son of God, that is our Lord Jesus Christ, it is written that He always was with the Father, and the paternal will never preceded Him in order that He might be; and He is indeed the natural Son."
Note: Hilary in the book On the Synods: "The holy Church anathematizes those who say that the Son of God is from non-existence — and likewise those who say that the Father begot the Son neither by counsel nor by will. Likewise, if anyone says the Son was born against the will of the Father, let him be anathema. For the Father did not beget the Son against His will, or compelled, or led by natural necessity against His will; but as soon as He willed — without time and impassibly — He manifested His only-begotten from Himself."

Notes

Doubt IV — Joachim of Fiore: The most historically significant doubt in Distinction V. Joachim's argument was that Peter Lombard's position (the essence neither generates nor is generated nor proceeds) implies a quaternitas — a fourth entity alongside Father, Son, and Holy Spirit. Bonaventure's rebuttal: Joachim equivocates on "thing" (res) — in the first sense (generating, generated, proceeding) "thing" means the thing of nature as individuated in a supposit; in the second sense it means the divine nature itself. Furthermore, Joachim's argument fails from an insufficient premise: having or not having a property does not suffice for real distinction. The condemnation at Lateran IV (1215) is explicitly cited.
Doubt V — "received" (Hilary): Accipere (to receive) is shown to be amphibolous: the "having" dimension regards the essence (the Son has the whole essence), while the "being from another" dimension regards the person (the Son is from the Father). No reference to the essence being received or referred to another follows.
Doubt VII — "follows His nature" (Hilary): Hilary's use of sequi (to follow) in the context of eternal generation is admitted to be an improper expression, qualified by Hilary himself with ut ita dicam ("as it were"). The sense is simply inseparable accompaniment — the immutable God generating is never separated from His nature, not that there is temporal succession.
Doubt IX — "Son of His love" (caritatis): Two solutions are offered. (1) Appropriation: caritas (love) is common to all three persons but appropriated to the Father as the gratuitous giver — a position attributed to Richard of St. Victor. (2) Essential reading: Augustine himself equates "Son of His love" with "Son of His substance" — the genitive declares essential quality, not principiation: "beloved Son" = "consubstantial Son."
Doubt X — "mother" vs. "father": The transfer of maternal language to God is rejected because the maternal principle is passive (requiring a prior active principle — Anselm, Monologion 42). The divine principle of generation is purely actual and admits no passive component. Maternal acts (conceiving, bringing forth) are transferred metaphorically, because what two principles accomplish in creaturely generation, one principle accomplishes in divine generation.
Doubt XI — "from nothing" (Anselm's threefold sense): Bonaventure deploys Anselm's (Monologion 8) analysis: (1) privative/negative — "from no thing" (the negation bears on the preposition's object); (2) positive-material — "nothing as matter" (nothing cannot be a material cause); (3) ordinal — being after non-being (proper sense of creaturely creation). The divine persons lack sense (3) — they have no being after non-being — even though they lack pre-existing matter. Hence "from nothing" in the ordinary creaturely sense does not apply to God.
Distinction VI (Lombard's text): The question of whether the Father begot the Son by will or necessity, resolved by the Orosius-Augustine tradition: neither will nor necessity in the Eunomian sense (as a preceding, accruing, or efficient will preceding the act). The Father begot willingly in the sense that He begot wisely, powerfully, and goodly — will here expressing the essential divine perfection, not a preceding volition. The Lombard's summary quotation of the Catholic respondent's dilemma against Eunomius (willingly → Son of will, not nature; unwillingly → divine misery) is a classic piece of dialectical theology. Hilary's synodal anathema closes the section by condemning both the "from nothing" and "neither by counsel nor by will" positions.
