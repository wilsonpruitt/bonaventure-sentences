DOUBT VII.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what the Master says: that by the name of partnership, something is not posited but removed — because from this it seems that, according to him, every numerical expression posits nothing but only negates; but this seems false. For since in divine things there is a true plurality of persons, they seem to be spoken of not only privatively but also positively.

I respond: This was the Master's position, which is not commonly held, because it does not have truth, as will be more clearly shown below. Nevertheless the Master is excused, because he said that numerical names posit nothing because they import number, which is not properly in divine things. For number is caused by the aggregation and distinction of units; and the distinction of units comes about in three ways: by division of the continuous, by disparity of forms, and by grade or order. Since therefore in divine things there is neither aggregation nor such distinction, there is accordingly no number properly speaking.

DOUBT VIII.

Likewise, a question arises concerning what he says: "In the beginning God created" — why this name "God" stands or supposes for the person of the Father rather than for the person of the Son, and how the Trinity is to be understood from this.

I respond: To this it must be said that the plurality of persons is indicated to us in Scripture in eight ways. In the first way by signification, as in the last chapter of Matthew: "In the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." In the second way by co-signification, as in Genesis at the beginning, where we have "God" but the Hebrews have *Elohim*, which is the nominative plural of the singular *El*. In the third way by supposition, as when it is said "God begot God"; in Proverbs eight: "Before all hills the Lord begot me." In the fourth way by appropriation, as there: "In the beginning God created," etc. — for "God" there is appropriated to the Father and "beginning" to the Son. In the fifth way by iteration, as in Isaiah six: "Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord God of hosts." In the sixth way by order of words, as in the Psalm: "May God bless us, our God, may God bless us." In the seventh way by connotation in the act of mission, as when it is said in Galatians four: "God sent," etc. In the eighth way by apparition, as the three men appeared to Abraham in Genesis eighteen.

DOUBT IX.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "That greatest of the Prophets" — because on Matthew chapter seventeen: "Moses and Elijah appeared to them," the Gloss says: "Elijah was the greatest of the Prophets" — not David, therefore.

I respond: The spirit of prophecy, given in greater abundance, makes a prophet of the Lord more excellent. It can therefore be given in greater abundance in two ways: either because it extends to more things, or because it extends to higher things. To Elijah it was given with respect to more things, namely foreknowledge of future events and the working of miracles; but to David with respect to higher things, because, as is clear from his prophecy, he saw more and more clearly, since his was intellectual prophecy.

DOUBT X.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "The Lord possessed me" — because possession is of an inferior thing, ordering is of a posterior thing, and conception similarly sounds of the fragility of the female sex, as does parturition — none of which is fitting to divine things.

I respond: Wisdom is described by comparison to her effects and by comparison to her principle. And since by comparison to her effects she has in herself an inexhaustible treasury in terms of multitude, and has order in terms of discretion, she is therefore described by the verb of possessing and ordering. By comparison to her principle she emanates by an intrinsic emanation, in that she is of the substance of the one emanating; and therefore she is described by the verb of conception and parturition.

DISTINCTION III.
PART I.

On the knowledge of the Creator through creatures, in which the vestige of the Trinity appears.

"For the Apostle says" that the invisible things of God, from the creation of the world, are seen through the things that are made, being understood by the intellect — His eternal power and divinity as well. By "creation of the world" is understood the human being "on account of the excellence by which he excels among other creatures, or on account of the affinity he has with every creature." The human being, therefore, could or indeed did behold the invisible things of God with the intellect of the mind through the things that are made — that is, through visible or invisible creatures. For he was helped by two things, namely by nature, which was rational, and by the works made by God, so that truth might be made manifest to the human being. Therefore the Apostle said: "because God revealed it to them" — namely while He made the works in which the judgment of the craftsman shines forth to some degree.

For as Ambrose says: "So that God, who is invisible by nature, might also be known from visible things, He made a work that by its own visibility manifested its maker, so that through the certain the uncertain might be known, and that He who made what is impossible for a human being to make might be believed to be God of all things." They were therefore able to know, or they did know, that beyond every creature there is one who made those things which no creature is able to make or destroy. Let any creature you will come forward and make such a heaven and such an earth, and I shall say that it is God. But since no creature can make such things, it is established that the one who made them is above every creature; and through this, the human mind was able to know that He is God.

In another way also they were able to know, or did know, the truth of God through the guidance of reason. For as Augustine says in the book On the City of God: "The greatest philosophers saw that no body is God, and therefore transcended all bodies in seeking God; they saw too that whatever is mutable is not the highest God and principle of all things, and therefore transcended every soul and mutable spirits; then they saw that everything mutable cannot exist except through that which exists immutably and simply. They understood, therefore, that He both made all these things and could not have been made by anyone."

"They also considered that whatever exists in substances is either body or spirit, and that spirit is something better than body, but far better is the one who made spirit and body."

"They also understood that the form of a body is sensible and the form of a spirit is intelligible, and they preferred the intelligible form to the sensible. By sensibles we mean what can be perceived by the sight and touch of the body; by intelligibles, what can be understood by the gaze of the mind. Since therefore in their gaze both body and spirit were more or less beautiful, and if they could lack all form they would be altogether nothing, they saw that there is something whereby those things were made beautiful, where the first and immutable form is, and thus incomparable; and they most rightly believed that to be the principle of things which was not made and from which all made things were made." Behold, in so many ways the truth of God could be known. Since therefore God is one simple essence, consisting of no diversity of parts or accidents, the Apostle nonetheless says in the plural "the invisible things of God," because the truth of God is known in multiple ways through the things that are made. For from the perpetuity of creatures the eternal Creator is understood; from their magnitude, the omnipotent one; from their order and disposition, the wise one; from their governance, the good one. But all these pertain to demonstrating the unity of the Godhead.

There remains now to show whether through the things that are made some vestige or faint indication of the Trinity could be had. Concerning this, Augustine in the sixth book On the Trinity says: "It is fitting that we who contemplate the Creator through the things that are made should understand the Trinity. For a vestige of this Trinity appears in creatures. For all these things that have been made by divine art show in themselves a certain unity and form and order. For each of these created things is both something one, as are the natures of bodies and souls, and is formed by some form, as are the figures or qualities of bodies and the disciplines or arts of souls, and seeks or holds some order, as are the weights or positions of bodies and the loves or delights of souls; and so in creatures the vestige of the Trinity shines forth. For in that Trinity there is the supreme origin of all things and most perfect beauty and most blessed delight." Now the supreme origin, as Augustine shows in the book On True Religion, is understood as God the Father, from whom are all things, from whom are the Son and the Holy Spirit. Most perfect beauty is understood as the Son — that is, the truth of the Father, in no part unlike Him, whom together with Him and in Him the Father we venerate, who is the form of all things that were made from the one and are referred back to the one, which things nonetheless would neither come to be from the Father through the Son nor be kept safe within their proper bounds unless God were supremely good — He who neither envied any nature that would be good from Him, and gave to each, to some as much as it willed, to others as much as it could, the ability to remain in that good; and this goodness is understood as the Holy Spirit, who is the gift of the Father and the Son. Therefore it is fitting for us to honor and hold fast to the gift of God itself, co-equally immutable with the Father and the Son. Through the consideration of creatures, therefore, we understand the Trinity of one substance — namely the one God the Father, from whom we are, and the Son through whom we are, and the Holy Spirit in whom we are; that is, the principle to whom we return, the form we follow, and the grace by which we are reconciled: the one, namely, at whose command as author we were founded, and His likeness through whom we are reformed unto unity, and the peace by which we adhere to Unity — namely, God who said "let there be," and the Word through whom everything was made that exists substantially and naturally, and the Gift of His kindness by which what was made from Him through the Word was pleased and reconciled to its author so that it would not perish. Behold, it has been shown how in creatures the image of the Trinity is indicated to some degree; for through the contemplation of creatures a sufficient knowledge of the Trinity could not be had or could be had without the revelation of doctrine or of interior inspiration. Hence those ancient philosophers saw the truth as if through a shadow and from afar, falling short in their contemplation of the Trinity, like the magicians of Pharaoh at the third sign. Yet we are helped in the faith of invisible things through the things that are made.

On the image and likeness of the Trinity in the human soul.

"But now let us come to that discussion in which we find the image of the Trinity in the human mind, which knows God or can know Him." For as Augustine says in the fourteenth book On the Trinity: "Although the human mind is not of the same nature as God, yet the image of that nature than which nothing is better is to be sought and found there, where our nature has nothing better — that is, in the mind. For in the mind itself, even before it participates in God, His image is found; for although, with the loss of participation in God, it is deformed, the image of God nonetheless remains. For the mind is the image of God precisely insofar as it is capable of Him and can be a participant in Him. Let us therefore inquire in it for the Trinity that is God. Behold, then, the mind remembers itself, understands itself, loves itself; if we see this, we see a trinity — not yet God, indeed, but the image of God. For here there appears a certain trinity of memory, understanding, and love." Let us therefore treat especially of these three: memory, understanding, and will.

"These three, then, as Augustine says in the tenth book On the Trinity, are not three lives but one life; not three minds but one mind; one essence. Memory, however, is said in relation to something, and understanding and will or love are similarly said in relation to something; but life is said with reference to itself, and so are mind and essence. These three are therefore one in that they are one life, one mind, one essence; and whatever else they are each said to be with reference to themselves, they are also said together — not in the plural, but in the singular. But they are three in that they are referred to one another."

"They are also equal — not only individually to individually, but also individually to all together; otherwise they would not mutually contain each other; but they do mutually contain each other. For both individually and all together they are contained by each and all. For I remember that I have memory and understanding and will; and I understand that I understand and will and remember; and I will that I will and remember and understand."

"And I remember my entire memory and understanding and will together. For what I do not remember of my memory is not in my memory; but nothing is so present in memory as memory itself: therefore I remember it entirely. Likewise, whatever I understand, I know that I understand, and I know that I will whatever I will; but whatever I know, I remember. Therefore I remember my entire understanding and my entire will."

"Similarly, when I understand these three, I understand them all together. For there is nothing among the intelligibles that I do not understand, except what I am ignorant of. But what I am ignorant of I neither remember nor will. Whatever therefore of the intelligibles I do not understand, consequently I neither remember nor will. Whatever, therefore, of the intelligibles I do remember and will, consequently I understand."

"My will also grasps my entire understanding and my entire memory when I use all that I understand and remember. Since therefore mutually, individually and all together, all things and their wholes are grasped by each, the entire individual things are equal to the entire individual things, and the entire individual things are equal together to all the entire things; and these three are one — one life, one mind, one essence." "Behold, the human mind is the image — though unequal — of that supreme Unity and Trinity in which there is one essence and three persons." Now mind is here taken for the soul itself, in which that image of the Trinity is found; "but mind is properly said, as Augustine says, not of the soul itself, but of what is most excellent in it" — in which sense it is often used. It should also be known that memory is not only of absent and past things, but also of present things, as Augustine says in the fourteenth book On the Trinity — otherwise it could not contain itself.

Here it must be carefully noted in what sense it is to be understood that those three things — namely memory, understanding, and will — are said to be one, one mind, one essence. This does not seem to be true according to the proper meaning of the expression. For mind — that is, the rational spirit — is a spiritual and incorporeal essence. But those three are natural properties or powers of the mind itself and differ from one another, because memory is not understanding or will, nor is understanding will or love.

"And these three are also referred to themselves," as Augustine says in the ninth book On the Trinity: "For the mind cannot love itself or remember itself unless it also knows itself — for how does it love or remember what it does not know?" In a wondrous manner, therefore, these three are inseparable from themselves; and yet each individually and all together are one essence, even though they are said in relation to one another.

But now it must be seen in what sense these three are said to be one substance — namely because they exist substantially in the soul or mind itself, not like accidents in subjects, which can be present and absent. Hence Augustine in the ninth book On the Trinity says: "We are admonished, if we can see even this much, that these things exist in the soul substantially, not as in a subject, as color in a body, because even though they are said in relation to one another, each of them nonetheless exists substantially in its own substance." Behold, in what sense those three things are said to be one or one substance. "These three, as Augustine says in the fifteenth book On the Trinity, are divinely established in the mind by nature; and whoever vividly perceives how great is that in it from which even the eternal and immutable nature can be recalled, perceived, and desired — for it is recalled through memory, perceived through understanding, embraced through love — surely finds in it the image of that supreme Trinity."

CHAPTER III.

On the likeness between the creating and the created trinity.

"Yet let him beware lest he compare this image, made by the same Trinity, to it in such a way that he thinks it altogether like, but rather in whatever likeness there is, let him perceive a great unlikeness as well."

"This can be shown briefly. One human being through those three remembers, understands, loves — he who is neither memory nor understanding nor love, but has these. There is therefore one human being who has these three, not who himself is these three. But in that supreme simplicity of nature which is God, although God is one, there are nonetheless three persons, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit," and these three are one God. "The thing of the Trinity, therefore, is one thing; the image of the Trinity in another thing is another. On account of this image, even that in which these three are is called an image — namely the human being. Just as both the panel and the picture painted on it are called an image; but the panel is called by the name of image on account of the picture that is in it."

"Again, this image — which is a human being having those three — is one person. But that Trinity is not one person but three — the Father of the Son and the Son of the Father and the Spirit of the Father and the Son. So in this image of the Trinity, those three are not one human being but belong to one human being. But in that supreme Trinity whose image this is, those three do not belong to one God but are one God; and those three are three persons, not one." "For those three are not the human being, but belong to or are in the human being. But can we say that the Trinity is thus in God such that those three are something of God, and not God Himself?" Far be it from us to believe this. Let us say, then, that in our mind there is the image of the Trinity — but a slight and imperfect one — which bears a likeness to the supreme Trinity in such a way that it is for the most part unlike it. It must also be known that "this Trinity of the mind, as Augustine says in the fourteenth book On the Trinity, is not the image of God merely because the mind remembers and understands and loves itself, but because it can also remember and understand and love Him by whom it was made."

There is also another way in which the trinity in the soul — which is an image of that supreme and ineffable Trinity — can be distinguished under other names. For as Augustine says in the ninth book On the Trinity: "Mind and its knowledge and love are certain three things. For the mind knows itself and loves itself; nor can it love itself unless it also knows itself — for how does it love or remember what it does not know?" "When therefore the mind knows and loves itself, a trinity remains — namely mind, love, and knowledge." "But mind here is taken not for the soul but for what is more excellent in the soul." Now these three, although they are distinct from one another, are nonetheless said to be one, because they exist substantially in the soul.

And the mind itself is, as it were, the parent; and its knowledge is, as it were, its offspring. "For when the mind knows itself, it begets knowledge of itself, and is the sole parent of its own knowledge. The third is love, which proceeds from the mind itself and from knowledge, when the mind knowing itself loves itself; for it could not love itself unless it knew itself. It also loves its pleasing offspring — that is, its knowledge; and so love is a certain embrace of parent and offspring."

"Nor is the offspring less than the parent, when the mind knows itself as great as it is; nor is love less than the parent and the offspring — that is, than the mind and the knowledge — when the mind loves itself as much as it knows itself and as great as it is."

"These are also each in themselves: for the loving mind is in love, and love is in the knowledge of the one who loves, and knowledge is in the knowing mind." Behold, in these three some vestige of the Trinity appears.

The rational mind, therefore, considering these three and that one essence in which they exist, extends itself to the contemplation of the Creator and sees unity in trinity and trinity in unity. For it understands that God is one, one essence, one principle. It understands that if there were two, either each would be insufficient, or one would be superfluous — for if something were lacking to one that the other had, supreme perfection would not be there; but if nothing were lacking to one that the other had, since everything would be in one, the other would be superfluous. It understood, therefore, that there is one God, the one author of all things; and it saw that without wisdom He would be, as it were, a foolish thing; and therefore it understood that He has wisdom, which was begotten from Him; and because He loves His wisdom, it also understood that there is love there.

On the unity of the Trinity.

"Therefore, in accordance with this consideration, as Augustine says in the ninth book On the Trinity, let us believe the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit to be one God, creator and ruler of the whole creation; and the Father not to be the Son, and the Holy Spirit not to be either the Father or the Son, but a Trinity of persons related to one another." For as he himself says in the book On Faith to Peter: "There is one nature or essence of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit — not one person. For if there were one person just as there is one substance of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, a Trinity would truly not be said. Again, there would indeed be a true Trinity, but the Trinity itself would not be one God, if just as the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are distinguished from one another by the property of their persons, so they had been also separated by diversity of natures." "But the faith of the Patriarchs, Prophets, and Apostles proclaims the one God to be the Trinity." "In that holy Trinity, therefore, there is one God the Father, who alone begot essentially from Himself one Son; and there is one Son, who alone was essentially born from the one Father; and there is one Holy Spirit, who alone essentially proceeds from Father and Son. But all of this one person cannot do — that is, beget itself and be born from itself and proceed from itself." For as Augustine says in the first book On the Trinity: "There is nothing that begets itself so as to exist."

COMMENTARY ON DISTINCTION III.

PART I.

On the knowledge of God through remote likenesses.

"For the Apostle says that the invisible things of God," etc.

DIVISION OF THE TEXT.

Above, the Master treated of the holy Trinity and Unity insofar as it is believed. In this second part he treats of the same insofar as it is understood. And this part is divided into three parts: in the first he brings forward fitting likenesses and arguments for understanding the Trinity. In the second he resolves the emerging doubts, below at the beginning of distinction four: "Here there arises a quite necessary question. It is established," etc. In the third, those doubts having been resolved, he determines the properties and conditions of the Trinity and Unity, below at distinction eight: "Now concerning the truth or the property," etc.

Likewise, the first part has two. In the first he brings forward remote likenesses; in the second, near or express likenesses, which are found in the image, at: "But now let us come to that discussion."

Likewise, the first part has two parts. In the first he shows Unity; in the second, Trinity, at: "There remains now to show whether through the things that are made," etc.

The first part has four sections. In the first he proves by the authority of the Apostle in Romans one that God is knowable through creatures. In the second he adduces various modes of knowing and arguments, at: "For as Ambrose says." In the third he states that those modes are implied in the authority of the Apostle, at: "Behold, in so many ways." In the fourth he indicates what can be proved through the aforesaid arguments — namely the unity of essence, not the Trinity — at: "But all of these pertain to demonstrating the unity," etc.

"There remains now to show whether," etc. Above, the Master showed the unity or entity of the divine essence through arguments. In this second part he shows the Trinity through a remote likeness, which is found in the vestige; and this chapter has four sections. In the first the Master shows the character of the vestige in creatures. In the second he shows what in the Creator corresponds to the vestige — namely the trinity of appropriated attributes: origin, beauty, and delight, so that individual items are matched to individual ones — at: "For in that Trinity there is the supreme origin." In the third he teaches the contemplation of the Trinity in God through the consideration of the vestige in creatures, at: "Through the consideration of creatures, therefore," etc., where he shows the trinity of appropriated attributes in God both with respect to the act of creation and with respect to the act of reformation. In the fourth section he shows that the consideration of the Trinity through the vestige is not sufficient but is remote, at: "Behold, it has been shown how in creatures."

"But now let us come to that discussion." This is the second part of the present distinction, in which the Master proves the Trinity and Unity through a near and express likeness, of the kind that is an image. And this part is divided in two: in the first he shows the trinity and unity in the Creator through the trinity and unity considered in the powers of the soul; in the second, through the habitual dispositions, at: "It can also be distinguished in another way and under other names."

The first part again has four sections. In the first the Master shows where the image is to be sought in the soul — namely in the superior part and in its three powers: memory, understanding, and will. In the second he touches on the conditions of the image, which are trinity, unity, and equality, at: "These three, then, as Augustine says." In the third he raises and resolves the doubt that arises from the foregoing — namely whether the soul is its own powers — at: "Here it must be carefully noted." In the fourth he teaches how to guard against the error that could be caused or arise from the presentation of the created trinity — that it would be altogether like the uncreated — where he shows that the unlikeness of the image to God is very great, at: "Yet let him beware."

"It can also be distinguished in another way and under other names," etc. This is the second part of the second part of this distinction, in which the Master intends to investigate the image in the habitual dispositions of the soul as far as is possible; and this part is divided into four sections. First, he touches on those things in which the image of the Trinity is found — namely mind, knowledge, and love. Second, those having been indicated, he shows that the image is found in these on account of their consubstantiality, order, and equality, at: "Now these three, although they are distinct from one another." Third, he shows how the rational mind, in the contemplation of the created trinity, advances toward seeing and knowing the uncreated Trinity in Unity, at: "The rational mind, therefore, considering these three." Fourth, he briefly recollects what has been demonstrated above, at: "Therefore, in accordance with this consideration."

TREATMENT OF THE QUESTIONS.

For the understanding of what is touched upon in this first part of the distinction, four things are asked.

The first is whether God is knowable by a creature.

The second is whether He is knowable through a creature.

The third is whether the mode of knowing through creatures belongs to the human being with respect to every state — namely the state of innocence, the state of fallen nature, and the state of glorified nature.

The fourth is what is knowable about God through creatures.

THE SINGLE ARTICLE.

On the knowability of God.

QUESTION I.

Whether God is knowable by a creature.

Concerning the first — that God is not knowable by a creature — it is shown:

1. By the authority of Dionysius in the Divine Names: "It is impossible either to speak or to understand God."

2. Likewise, it is shown by reason through a fourfold supposition that must be present in cognition — namely proportion, union or reception, judgment, and information. For the intellect understands only what is proportionable to it, and what is in some way united to it, and about which it judges, and by which the gaze of understanding is informed.

From the first supposition the argument runs thus: it is necessary that there be a proportion between the knower and the knowable; but there is no proportion between God and the intellect, because God is infinite and the intellect is finite: therefore, etc. Furthermore, if there is some proportion, it does not seem to be sufficient, because the uncreated truth is more distant from the human intellect than any created intelligible is from the sense. But the sense, which is perceptive of the sensible, is never elevated to knowledge of the created intelligible: therefore neither will the intellect ever be elevated to knowledge of the uncreated intelligible.

3. Likewise, from the second supposition thus: it is necessary that there be a union of the knowable with the knower, so that one is in the other; but the knower is not in the knowable, but conversely; but it is impossible for the infinite to be contained by the finite itself: therefore it is impossible for it to be in it: therefore for God to be in the intellect is impossible, since He is infinite.

4. Likewise, from the third supposition thus: for cognition it is necessary that there be in the knower a judgment concerning the known; but every one who judges has power over what is judged; but the finite does not have power over the infinite: therefore it does not judge concerning it; but judgment was required for cognition: therefore the finite intellect does not know the infinite God, over whom it has no power.

5. Likewise, from the fourth thus: it is necessary that the intellect that knows be informed by the known; but everything that informs another does so either through its essence or through a likeness; but God does not inform through His essence, because He is united to nothing as a form; nor through an abstracted likeness, because an abstracted likeness is more spiritual than that from which it is abstracted; but nothing is or can be more spiritual than God: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. The rational soul is made in the image of God. But as Augustine says in the book On the Trinity — and it is in the text of the present distinction — "The mind is the image of God precisely insofar as it is capable of God and can be a participant in Him." But to be capable of Him is not according to substance or essence, since this applies to all creatures: therefore it is through knowledge and love: therefore God can be known by a creature.

2. Likewise, it is shown by reason thus: all spiritual knowledge comes about by reason of light, and by reason of uncreated light, as Augustine says in the Soliloquies; but light is supremely knowable, and God is supreme light: therefore He is supremely knowable by the soul itself: therefore, etc.

3. ### Notes

- **Opening (Doubt VI continued):** The chunk opens with the tail of Bonaventure's response to Doubt VI from the prior chunk, distinguishing likeness according to accident or part (which admits diversity) from likeness according to the whole (which does not). Translated seamlessly.
- **Doubt VII — the Master's position on numerical expressions:** Bonaventure explicitly distances himself from Peter Lombard's position that numerical expressions in Trinitarian discourse are purely privative. He excuses the Master on narrow grounds while signaling that the fuller treatment follows at dist. 24. The phrase "This was the Master's position, which is not commonly held" is a rare moment of direct critique of the Lombard.
- **Doubt VIII — eight modes of Trinitarian signification in Scripture:** A compact taxonomy unique in the Sentences commentary tradition. Alexander of Hales is noted by the Quaracchi editors as listing seven of these modes; Bonaventure adds the eighth (apparition). All eight rendered with their technical scholastic names.
- **"Intellectual prophecy" (prophetia intellectualis):** Bonaventure's distinction between David (prophecy through intellectual vision — the highest Augustinian grade) and Elijah (prophecy through imaginative vision — more numerous but lower miracles and foreknowledge). Follows Augustine's taxonomy from *De Genesi ad litteram* XII.
- **Distinction III begins mid-chunk:** From "For the Apostle says" onward, the text transitions entirely into Bonaventure's commentary on Distinction III — first the Lombard's base text on the vestige of the Trinity in creation and the image in the soul (lengthy Augustine *catenae*), then Bonaventure's own *Divisio Textus* and the opening of Question I. All translated continuously.
- **Augustine *catenae*:** The extended passages from *De Trinitate* (IX, X, XIV, XV) and *De Vera Religione* are translated from Bonaventure's version of the text, which diverges slightly from the critical edition of Augustine in places. Per the brief, these divergences are left as-is.
- **"Remote likenesses / near likenesses" (similitudines longinquae / propinquae):** Bonaventure's technical distinction between the *vestigium* (trace of the Trinity found in all creatures) and the *imago* (image of the Trinity found in the rational soul). The vestige is a remote, one-way likeness; the image is a near, reflexive likeness. Both terms retained throughout.
- **Question I ends mid-text:** The chunk ends in the midst of the fifth *contra* argument of Question I (*illa maxime*). Translated as given.
