### English

DIST. VI. SOLE ARTICLE, QUESTION I.

---

*[The preceding division of the text has indicated that the Master first offers a solution, then argues against it, then opposes the prior solution and responds, and finally returns to an explanation of the first solution.]*

TREATMENT OF THE QUESTIONS.

SOLE ARTICLE.

On the generation of the Son according to its conditions.

For the understanding of this part, three things are principally inquired.

First, it is inquired whether the generation of the Son is according to the condition of necessity.

Second, whether it is according to the account of will.

Third, whether it is according to the condition of exemplarity.

---

QUESTION I.

Whether the generation of the Son is according to the account of necessity.

Concerning the first point, that the generation is according to the condition of necessity, it is shown as follows.

1. Nature in the Father is more fecund and more actual with respect to producing the Son than light is with respect to producing a ray; but light must necessarily produce a ray, such that its production proceeds according to the condition of necessity: therefore, much more so in the Father with respect to the Son.

2. Likewise, everything that emanates from another emanates either according to the condition of necessity or of contingency: therefore the Son also emanates by one of these two modes; but not according to the condition of contingency, because then it would be contingent that the Son be generated: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, it is impossible to take away from the omnipotent the greatest power; but God the Father is omnipotent, whose greatest power is to generate the Son: therefore it is impossible to take away from him the power to generate the Son. But in eternal things, potency is conjoined to act: therefore by the same account it is impossible to remove the act of generation: therefore it is impossible that he not generate; but "impossible not to be" and "necessary to be" are convertible: therefore it is necessary that he generate.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. Augustine, in his work *To Orosius*, and the Master say in the text: "The Father generated the Son neither by will nor by necessity, because necessity does not exist in God": therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, Hilary in the book *On the Synods*: "The Father, led by no natural necessity, generated the Son": therefore there was no natural necessity there, nor any other necessity, as it seems; therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, a more noble producing agent is one that is master of its action than one that is subject to it; but an agent acting according to the account of necessity is subject to its action, because whether it wills or not it must act: therefore if the Father, who is the most noble agent, produces the Son in the most noble manner, he does not do so according to the condition of necessity.

4. Likewise, the same point can be shown as follows: what is given gratuitously is not given of necessity; but Richard says that in the Father there is gratuitous love, because he gives being to the Son and to the Holy Spirit: if therefore he gives gratuitously, he gives willingly and freely.

---

*The generation in divine things is necessary with the necessity of immutability, but not with any other species of necessity.*

I respond: For the understanding of what has been set forth, it must be noted that necessity is manifold. For there is a necessity arising from a discordant principle, a necessity arising from a deficient principle, and a necessity arising from a concordant and sufficient principle.

That necessity which arises from a discordant principle acts against the will, and this necessity is that of coercion. And it is concerning these two modes [i.e., the first two] that the heretic inquires, since they are the most commonly employed.

That necessity similarly which arises from a deficient principle is twofold: for it is either with respect to that by which a thing is naturally completed, and this is the necessity of indigence, as of food and drink — of this it is said in 1 John 3: *Whoever sees his brother in necessity*, etc. — or it is with respect to that which is incurred from the defect itself, and this is the necessity of inevitability, of the kind found in death and in the first movements; this necessity man incurs from the lack of original justice, as in the Psalm: *Deliver me from my necessities*.

That necessity similarly which arises from a sufficient and concordant principle is likewise twofold: for it is either from a principle sufficient for disposing, and this is the necessity of disposed matter, which may be called the necessity of exigence; or it is from a principle sufficient for completing, and this is the necessity of immutability.

And this last necessity obtains in God, and principally in God, because he alone is wholly sufficient unto himself and wholly concordant with himself. Moreover, this necessity is not repugnant to the freedom of the will, but only to mutability, which does not exist in God.

1. To the objection drawn from the text, it must be said that this is to be understood of the necessity of coercion, which in no manner obtains in God, and it was concerning this that the heretic was inquiring.

2. Similarly, Hilary too understands it in this sense, whence he adds in the text: "not by natural necessity, as though he were unwilling" — for then there would be coercion, inasmuch as the will would be resisting.

3. To the objection that an agent by necessity is subject to its action: it must be said that this is false, unless the necessity be one repugnant to the will. For when there is a repugnant necessity, the will must be subjected because it cannot prevail. But when the necessity is supremely consonant, incapable of being discordant from the will, then it introduces no subjection, as is evident. For God necessarily is blessed and necessarily wills to be blessed; and just as it is necessary that he be blessed, so too that he will it; and thus is it to be understood with regard to the generation of the Son.

4. To the final objection concerning gratuitous love, it must be said that in gratuitous love there are two elements: one is that it gives from pure liberality, such that there is no exigence or debt of merit or of nature; the other is that it gives without expectation of return, and it is with respect to this second element that love in the Father is called gratuitous, not with respect to the first. For the natural fecundity of the Father is necessarily the ground of communicating nature to another.

### Notes

- **"sole article" (*articulus unicus*)**: Distinction 6 has only one article, covering three questions (necessity, will, exemplarity). The heading *Art. Unicus* is rendered "Sole Article" to preserve the technical sense that only one article organizes the entire distinction.

- **"condition of necessity / condition of exemplarity" (*conditio necessitatis / exemplaritatis*)**: *Conditio* here is a technical term for the formal character or mode under which a thing obtains; rendered "condition" throughout rather than "property" or "attribute" to preserve the scholastic register.

- **Opening text reference**: The chunk begins *in medias res* within the *Divisio Textus*, summarizing the Master's argumentative movement (first solution → counter-argument → response → return to first solution). The bracketed paraphrase at the opening renders what is clearly a division-of-text passage whose precise antecedent was in the prior chunk; rendered conservatively to reflect the text's transitional function.

- **"emanates from another" (*emanat ab alio*)**: *Emanatio* is Bonaventure's preferred term for the procession of divine persons; "emanate" is retained as the established technical rendering.

- **"the necessity of immutability" (*necessitas immutabilitatis*)**: This is the *Conclusio* of the question. The five species of necessity (coercion, indigence, inevitability, exigence, immutability) form a structured taxonomy; the final species alone applies to God. The Quaracchi scholion cross-references Bonaventure's distinction between this absolute sense and a weaker sense used by some other doctors (citing Brulifer) — this nuance is preserved in the translation by rendering the *Conclusio* banner accurately.

- **"concordant and sufficient principle" (*principio conveniente et sufficiente*)**: *Conveniens* carries the sense of "fitting," "consonant," "agreeing with" the nature of the agent; "concordant" captures this better than the more common "fitting" in this technical context of necessary vs. coerced action.

- **Richard (of St. Victor)**: The reference to *amor gratuitus* in the fourth *sed contra* argument is to Richard's Trinitarian theology of love, consistent with prior chunks. The reply to this objection introduces the key distinction: gratuitousness refers to giving without expectation of return (*sine retributione*), not to giving without natural exigence — the Father's natural fecundity necessarily grounds the communication of nature.

- **"first movements" (*primi motus*)**: A standard term in scholastic moral theology for involuntary initial impulses or passions prior to the consent of the will; retained as "first movements" per convention.

- **OCR artifacts**: Several words show OCR degradation (e.g., *iripiej* for what is likely *triplex* in the *Respondeo* opening; *subdivisios* appearing twice as a marginal tag). These have been corrected silently. *Discoryveniente* corrected to *disconveniente*. The fragment at the end of the fourth *sed contra* ("quam libentcr et gratis impendit...") appears to be a marginal gloss or cross-reference bleeding into the main text; it has been omitted as non-authorial.
