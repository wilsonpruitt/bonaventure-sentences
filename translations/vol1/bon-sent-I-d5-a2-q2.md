Since therefore through generation persons are pluralized, and substance is not numbered in them, it follows that most truly substance or essence is communicated through generation — because through generation it comes to be one in many.

CONCLUSION.

The divine essence is communicated through generation, because through generation it comes to be one in many.

1. To the objection that generation is the principle of distinguishing: it must be said that generation through itself is the principle of distinguishing supposit from supposit, because generation terminates at that through itself; it is also the principle of communicating the common nature — because it produces like from like. But since in lower things nature is numbered in supposits, it is therefore also the principle of distinguishing even the common — but accidentally. In God, however, it is neither through itself nor accidentally.

2. To the objection that to cause the common to be in this individual is the account of appropriating: it must be said that this holds true when the common is appropriable or appropriated through being in this individual. But the divine essence is not appropriable or appropriated through being in this individual in this way, because it is not appropriable either with respect to its signification or with respect to its supposition — since it signifies and supposes the same thing, as was shown above; and its signification is not distinguished by any addition. For this is true: "the essence of the Father and the essence that is in the Father is the Son."

Or it can be said differently: to cause to be in this individual is twofold — either absolutely, and so it is the account of appropriating; or in comparison — so that what was previously in one comes to be afterward in another — and so it is the account of communicating the nature. But there is a nature that is distinguished in supposits — namely one to which addition is made — and one that is not, like the divine nature; and in the first there is both the account of communicating and of distinguishing, in the divine nature only of communicating.

3. To the objection that if essence is communicated through generation, generation makes essence common: it must be said that this is true insofar as "common" is said according to act — in that it is in many; but not according to potency. Hence with generation removed, the substance is common, because communicable; but it is not common as having been communicated.

4. To the objection that what is given through generation is generated: it must be said that this is false — because to be generated implies production and distinction, but giving (*datio*) implies authority and communication; and since through generation the substance is not distinguished even though it is communicated: therefore, etc.

DOUBTS CONCERNING THE MASTER'S TEXT.

In this part doubts arise concerning the text, and the first doubt concerns the Master's arguments. For his first argument leads to this incongruity: that if the essence were generated by the Father, the essence would be placed for a relative term; and this argument is given at: "Therefore it is not to be said that the Father begot the divine essence." But this seems to be no incongruity at all. For if the essence agrees more with the supposit than the universal agrees with the singular; and if it is not incongruous for the universal to be placed for the singular — then it is also not incongruous for the essence to be placed for a relative.

I respond: It must be said that if the essence were placed for a relative term, it would be incongruous — not from the side of the thing, but from the side of the expression of the faith. For then the unity of the essence would seem to be and would be marked as involving a certain distinction. It would also be incongruous because what is placed for a relative — insofar as it is a relative — does not indicate essence. If therefore the essence were placed for a relative, the essence would sometimes not indicate essence — which is contrary to every account of reason.

DOUBT II.

Likewise, a second doubt arises concerning the second argument, in which the Master leads to the incongruity that the same thing would beget itself — given at: "Likewise, since God the Father is the divine essence, if He were its begetter, He would indeed be the begetter of the very thing that He Himself is." For "essence" denotes something common, just as the name "God" does; but if one says "the man Peter generates a man, and he himself is a man: therefore he generates himself" — this argument is worth nothing. Similarly it seems here: "the Father generates God" — and by equal reasoning not in the matter at hand either.

I respond: It must be said that "common" denotes what is in many; but this can be in three ways. Either it is pluralized in many both with respect to form and with respect to supposit — as the name "man"; and such a term has a unity of account that admits distinction both with respect to thing and with respect to mode. Hence it is rightly said "man is from man" and "there are two men." In another way, something is common that is pluralized with respect to supposit but not with respect to form — as the name "God"; and such a term has a unity of thing, according to which the thing names the nature with a multiplication of supposits: and therefore it admits a distinguishing term that imports distinction as a mode, not as a thing. Hence "God from God" is conceded, but not "God is another than God." In a third way, something is common in name that is in many yet is not pluralized with respect to form — because it is one in many — nor with respect to supposits — because it does not suppose for them — as the name "essence." Of such a common it is true to say that it admits no distinction — neither with respect to mode nor with respect to thing; for then it would be signified that the same thing is distinguished from itself. And thus the Master's argument is sound: if the Father begot the essence, the Father begot Himself; and the Master speaks of the common from the side of the signifying word, not from the side of the thing — because from the side of the thing the same commonness is in "God" as in "essence."

DOUBT III.

Likewise, a third objection is raised against the third argument: if the Father begot the divine essence, then the begotten would be the cause of the begetting one — that He is and that He is God; and this is given at: "And if so, not the begotten gives the begetter," etc. But this argument seems to be worth nothing, because the account of cause does not obtain in divine things — "for a cause is that of whose being another follows" — but in divine things there is no other: therefore, etc.

Likewise, it does not follow: "the Father begot wisdom: therefore He is wise through the wisdom He begot" — and therefore by equal reasoning neither does the aforesaid expression hold.

I respond: It must be said that taking the name "cause" properly, it does not obtain in divine things; yet the account of principiating and the account of informing do obtain and are admitted there, and in their place the name of cause is used. Since therefore the account of being is signified by the name "essence" — essence therefore signifies in some way in the account of cause with respect to being. If therefore essence were begotten by being, essence would be signified in the account of an effect or of something principiated; and then the same thing would be both principle and principiated with respect to the same — which is impossible; and this is the foundation of the Master's argument.

To the objection concerning wisdom: it must be said that this has been addressed in the questions outside the text, because a name as abstract as "essence" does not suppose for a relative, whereas "wisdom" does well suppose for a relative; and so there is a fallacy of accident there: "the Father is wise through wisdom and begot wisdom: therefore He is wise through the wisdom He begot, or through begotten wisdom." But in the name of "essence" there is no fallacy of accident, because it signifies and supposes the same thing.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. II, Conclusio and replies 1–4):** The chunk opens completing the *Conclusio* begun in the prior chunk, then gives the four replies. The key move: the divine essence is communicable *of itself* (as the universal form is communicable of itself), but it is actually communicated *only through* the multiplication of supposits — which is what generation and procession accomplish. Communication is thus real but does not make the essence a *term* of generation.
- **Reply 1 — generation distinguishes supposits, not essence:** In lower things generation both distinguishes and communicates the common nature, because the form is numerically multiplied in distinct supposits (it is *numeratur*). In God the essence is not numbered in supposits — it is numerically one in all three. So generation distinguishes supposits (its primary and per se effect) and communicates the essence (its secondary effect) — but does *not* distinguish the essence.
- **Reply 2 — communication vs. appropriation:** To cause the common to be in *this* individual is appropriation when the common is modified by the individuation (as "humanity" is appropriated to Peter). But "essence" signifies and supposes the same thing — it has no suppositional variation. Hence being in a person neither appropriates nor modifies the essence; it only communicates it.
- **Reply 4 — giving (*datio*) vs. generating (*generari*):** Generation implies both production and distinction; giving (*datio*) implies authority and communication without distinction. The essence is *given* by the Father to the Son without being *generated* — a giving without distinction of the thing given.
- **Three types of "common" (Doubt II):** Bonaventure's most refined taxonomy in this section: (1) "man" — common both in form and in supposit; admits real distinction of form and mode; "man is from man" holds; (2) "God" — common in supposit but not in form (the divine essence is numerically one); admits distinction as mode but not as thing; "God from God" holds, "God is another than God" does not; (3) "essence" — common in name and in being (in many), but not pluralized in form or in supposit (does not suppose for individual persons); admits no distinction of any kind. The Master's argument is therefore sound *for "essence"* but would not work for "God" or "man."
- **Doubt III — "cause" in divine things:** Bonaventure accepts that proper causality (*causa* in the strict Aristotelian sense — that of whose being another follows) does not obtain in God, where there is no real *aliud*. But the accounts of *principiating* and *informing* do obtain. "Essence" signifies the account of being itself — it is in the position of a cause with respect to being. If begotten, it would be *principiated* by the very being whose account it names — which would make the same thing both principle and principiated of itself: the fundamental incongruity the Master identifies.
- **"Wisdom" vs. "essence" (Doubt III response):** "Wisdom" (*sapientia*) is concrete enough to suppose for a relative (the Son, the Word), so there is a fallacy of accident in inferring from "the Father begot wisdom" that "the Father is wise through the wisdom He begot." But "essence" (*essentia*) is so abstract that it always signifies and supposes the same thing — no switch of supposition is possible, so no fallacy of accident occurs: the Master's argument is direct and sound.
