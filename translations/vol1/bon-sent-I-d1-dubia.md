DOUBT XV.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "That it should be loved for its own sake alone" — because as "through itself" is to "through accident," so "for its own sake" is to "for the sake of another." But nothing that belongs to something through accident belongs to it through itself: therefore nothing that belongs to something for the sake of another belongs to it for its own sake. If therefore virtues are to be desired for the sake of another, then not for their own sake.

I respond: As the Philosopher says, "the end and the 'for the sake of which' are the same." But the end is twofold: in one way the ultimate end, which is the end in the most proper sense, in which there is rest; in another way an end under an end, which is called properly a terminus. Thus "for the sake of which" is also diversified; hence in one way it excludes the end, in another it does not, as the Master says.

As for the objection concerning "through itself": I say that, just as "through itself" in one sense is opposed to what is "through another" — and in this sense "through itself" is the same as "according to what it itself is," or "the first universal" — and in another sense is opposed to what is "through accident": so too "for its own sake" is taken in two ways. In one way it is opposed to what is "for the sake of another," and in another way it is opposed to what is "through accident," because it is ordered toward that thing only accidentally. According to this, the Master distinguishes "for its own sake," and consequently the honorable or the enjoyable, and enjoyment itself.

DOUBT XVI.

Likewise, an objection is raised concerning what he says: "And there are certain things through which we enjoy, such as the virtues." It seems that the soul could enjoy with its powers alone. For without any virtue it can enjoy a created good; therefore, since uncreated good is more enjoyable, and the soul is more naturally suited to this, it will be able to enjoy it with its powers alone.

I respond: It must be said that the highest good is that which is above us; therefore, in order for us to be united to it, a twofold medium necessarily intervenes: one medium through which the soul is naturally suited to be united to something diverse from itself — and this is the power; another special one beyond this, which elevates it — and this is virtue. The objection does not apply, because a power can of itself fall short and incline, but not of itself be elevated.

DISTINCTION II.

CHAPTER I.

On the Trinity and Unity.

This therefore is to be held with true and pious faith: "that the Trinity is the one and only true God, as Augustine says in the first book On the Trinity — namely the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit; and that this Trinity is said to be, is believed to be, and is understood to be of one and the same substance or essence, which is the highest good, which is seen by the most pure minds. For the weak gaze of the human mind is not fixed upon so excellent a light unless it has been cleansed through the righteousness of faith." The same in the first book of the Retractions: "I do not approve of what I said in the prayer: 'O God, who hast willed that only the pure should know the truth.' For it can be replied that many who are not pure know many true things. Concerning this highest and most excellent matter, therefore, one must proceed with modesty and fear, and with most attentive and devoted ears must it be heard, where the unity of the Trinity — namely of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit — is sought, because nowhere is error more dangerous, nowhere is anything sought with more labor, and nowhere is anything found more fruitfully." Therefore let everyone who hears and reads the things said of the ineffable and inaccessible light of the Godhead strive to imitate and preserve what the venerable Doctor Augustine says of himself in the first book On the Trinity: "It will not trouble me, he says, wherever I am uncertain, to seek; nor will I be ashamed, wherever I err, to learn. Whoever therefore hears or reads these things: where he is equally certain, let him go on with me; where he is equally uncertain, let him seek with me; where he recognizes his own error, let him return to me; where mine, let him call me back. So let us enter together on the path of charity, tending toward Him of whom it is said: Seek His face always."

CHAPTER II.

What the intention was of those who wrote on the Trinity.

"All the Catholic writers, as Augustine says in the same work, who have written on the Trinity which is God, have intended to teach according to the Scriptures that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are of one substance and are one God in inseparable equality, so that there is unity in essence and plurality in persons; and therefore they are not three gods, but one God, even though the Father has begotten the Son, and therefore the Son is not who the Father is; and the Son has been begotten by the Father, and therefore the Father is not who the Son is; and the Holy Spirit is neither the Father nor the Son, but only the Spirit of the Father and the Son, co-equal to both and belonging to the unity of the Trinity." "Let us hold, therefore, that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit are by nature one God, as Augustine says in the book On Faith to Peter; and yet that the Father Himself is not the Son; nor is the Son Himself who the Father is; nor is the Holy Spirit Himself who the Father is or who the Son is. For there is one essence of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, which the Greeks call *ousia*, in which the Father is not one thing, the Son another, the Holy Spirit yet another, even though the Father is personally other, the Son other, the Holy Spirit other."

CHAPTER III.

What order is to be observed when treating of the Trinity.

Furthermore, as Augustine teaches in the first book On the Trinity: "First, according to the authorities of the holy Scriptures, it must be demonstrated whether the faith is as it presents itself. Then, against garrulous reasoners, more elated than capable, Catholic reasoning and fitting comparisons must be used for the defense and assertion of the faith, so that, satisfying their inquiries, we may more fully instruct the gentle, and so that those who could not find what they seek may complain of their own minds rather than of the truth itself or of our exposition."

CHAPTER IV.

On the testimonies of the Old Testament by which the mystery of the Trinity is declared.

Let us therefore set forth in the midst the authorities of the Old and New Testaments by which the truth of the divine Unity and Trinity is demonstrated. And let the very opening of the Law come first, where Moses says: "Hear, O Israel, the Lord your God is one God." Likewise: "I am the Lord your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt; you shall have no other gods before me." Behold, here he signified the unity of the divine nature. "For 'God' and 'Lord,' as Ambrose says in the first book On the Trinity, is a name of nature, is a name of power." Likewise, elsewhere God speaking to Moses says: "I am who I am; and if they ask my name, go and say to them: He who is has sent me to you." For in saying "I am," not "We are," and "He who is," not "He who are," he most openly declared that there is one God alone. In the Canticle of Exodus also it is read: "The Lord, Almighty is His name" — he does not say "of the Lord," wishing to signify unity. The Lord also showed at once the plurality of persons and the unity of nature in Genesis, saying: "Let us make man according to our image and likeness." For in saying "let us make" and "our," He showed the plurality of persons; in saying "image," the unity of essence. For as Augustine says in the book On Faith to Peter: "If in that nature of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit there were only one person, it would not be said: 'Let us make man according to our image and likeness.' For when He says 'according to the image,' He shows that there is one nature according to whose image man was to be made; but when He says 'our,' He shows that the same God is not one but several persons." Hilary too in the third book On the Trinity says these words signify that in the Trinity there is neither diversity nor singularity or solitude, but likeness and plurality or distinction. For he says thus: "He who said 'Let us make man according to our image and likeness' showed that they are mutually like each other in that He says 'our image and likeness.'" "For an image alone does not exist, and a likeness does not exist for itself alone: nor does the likeness of one to another permit diversity to be mixed in the two." Likewise the same author in the fourth book: "He wished it to be understood more absolutely that this signification was not to be referred to Himself alone, in saying: 'Let us make man according to our image and likeness'; for the profession of partnership removed the understanding of the singular, because no partnership can exist for a solitary person alone, nor again does the solitude of a solitary person use 'let us make,' nor does anyone say 'our' of one alien from himself. Both expressions — namely 'let us make' and 'our' — just as they do not allow for one who is solitary and the same, so they do not signify one who is different and alien from himself. To a solitary being it is fitting to say 'I will make' and 'my'; but to one who is not solitary it is fitting to say 'let us make' and 'our.' Both expressions, as they do not signify one who is solitary, so neither do they signify one who is different or diverse. For us, therefore, He must be confessed as neither solitary nor diverse. And so God is found to work toward the making of man according to the image and likeness common to Himself with God, so that neither does the signification of the agent admit the understanding of solitude, nor does the operation constituted toward the same image or likeness allow for diversity of divinity."

In these words Hilary wished the plurality of persons to be understood by the name of partnership, and signified that by the name of partnership or plurality something is not posited, but removed. For when the plurality or partnership of persons is spoken of, solitude and singularity are denied; when we say that there are several persons, we signify that there is not only one. Therefore Hilary, wishing these things to be understood subtly and rightly, says: "The profession of partnership removed the understanding of the singular" — he does not say "posited something." So too when we say three persons, we remove singularity and solitude, and signify that the Father is not alone, nor is the Son alone, nor is the Holy Spirit alone, and that it is not only Father and Son, nor only Father and Holy Spirit, nor only Son and Holy Spirit. This matter will be treated more fully below, where it will also be shown in what sense the three persons are said to be alike, and whether there is in any way diversity or difference among them.

But now let us return to the matter at hand and, to demonstrate the plurality of persons and the unity of the divine essence, let us bring forward other authorities of the Saints. Moses says: "In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth" — by *God* signifying the Father, by *beginning* the Son. And for what among us is called *God*, the Hebrew truth has *Elohim*, which is the plural of the singular *El*, which is *God*. Therefore because it does not say *El*, which is God, but *Elohim*, which can be interpreted as "gods" or "judges," this is referred to the plurality of persons. To this also seems to belong what the devil said through the serpent: "You will be like gods," for which the Hebrew has *Elohim*, as if to say: you will be like the divine persons. That greatest of prophets and kings also, David, who claims understanding beyond others, saying: "I have understood beyond the elders" — showing the unity of the divine nature, says: "Lord is His name" — he does not say "of the Lord." Elsewhere also, showing at once its unity and eternity, he says from the person of God: "Israel, if you will hear me, there shall be in you no new God, nor shall you worship a foreign God." "One of these, as Ambrose says in the first book On the Trinity, signifies eternity, the other the unity of undifferentiated substance, so that we do not believe the Son or the Holy Spirit to be later than the Father or of another divinity. For if the Son or the Holy Spirit is later than the Father, He is new; and if He is not of one divinity, He is foreign; but He is not later, because He is not new, nor is He foreign, because the Son was born from the Father, the Holy Spirit proceeded from the Father." Elsewhere also, hinting at the distinction of persons, he says: "By the word of the Lord the heavens were made firm, and by the Spirit of His mouth all their power." Elsewhere also he says: "May God bless us, our God, may God bless us, and may all the ends of the earth fear Him." For the threefold confession of God expresses the Trinity of persons; but the unity of essence it discloses when, in the singular, he subjoins *Him*. Isaiah also says that he heard the Seraphim crying: "Holy, Holy, Holy, Lord God." By saying *Holy* three times he signifies the Trinity; by subjoining *Lord God*, the unity of essence. David also openly hints at the eternal generation of the Son, speaking from the person of the Son: "The Lord said to me: You are my Son; today I have begotten you." Concerning this ineffable generation Isaiah says: "Who shall declare His generation?" In the book of Wisdom also the eternity of the Son with the Father is shown, where Wisdom speaks thus: "The Lord possessed me at the beginning of His ways, before He made anything from the beginning: I was set in order from eternity, before the earth was made; the depths did not yet exist, and I was already conceived: not yet were the fountains, not yet the mountains or the hills, and I was already brought forth: He had not yet made the earth and the cornerstones of the world: when He prepared the heavens, I was there; when He was laying the foundations of the earth, I was with Him composing all things, and I delighted through all the days, playing before Him." Behold an open testimony concerning the eternal birth, whereby Wisdom herself testifies that before the world she was conceived and brought forth — that is, begotten — and that she exists eternally with the Father. Wisdom also says elsewhere: "I came forth from the mouth of the Most High, the firstborn before all creation." The Prophet Micah also indicated at once the eternal generation of the Word and the temporal birth from Mary, saying: "And you, Bethlehem Ephrata, are small among the thousands of Judah: from you shall come forth one who is to be ruler in Israel, and his going forth is from the beginning, from the days of eternity."

We also have express testimonies concerning the Holy Spirit in the Old Testament. For in Genesis it is read: "The Spirit of the Lord moved over the waters." And David says: "Where shall I go from your Spirit?" And in the book of Wisdom it is said: "The Holy Spirit of discipline will flee from deceit, for the spirit of wisdom is kind." Isaiah also says: "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me," etc.

### Notes

- **Doubt XIV continuation / Doubt XV opening:** The text opens mid-argument (Doubt XIV's response), transitioning into Doubt XV. The fragment at the start — "is most proximate to act... just as God as one imprints unity, so God as good imprints goodness" — completes the threefold account of why the inference "because He is good, we exist" holds. Translated to preserve the logical structure.
- **"First universal" (primum universale):** Bonaventure is invoking an Aristotelian-Thomistic technical term from the *Posterior Analytics* concerning per se predication. Rendered literally as "first universal" to preserve the scholastic reference. The editor's note indicates this refers to the strict adequation of predicate and subject.
- **"Power" vs. "virtue" (potentia / virtus):** Doubt XVI turns on this Bonaventurian distinction: *potentia* is the soul's natural capacity for union; *virtus* (infused virtue) is the additional supernatural elevation required. Both translated consistently throughout. Bonaventure's point is that natural capacity is insufficient for union with the supernatural highest good.
- **Distinction II, Chapter IV — Peter Lombard's text:** From this point forward the translation is of the *Sentences* of Peter Lombard himself (the *Magistri* text), not Bonaventure's commentary. This is the base text on which Bonaventure comments. It consists largely of patristic *catenae* (Augustine, Ambrose, Hilary) stitched together.
- **"Elohim" discussion:** The Lombard's Hebrew etymology (*hel* / *heloym*) is pre-critical and inaccurate by modern standards but is translated faithfully as written (*El* for the singular, *Elohim* for the plural).
- **"*Ousia*" (usian in the Latin):** The Lombard preserves the Greek transliteration. Translated as "*ousia*" with the Greek term retained as the Lombard himself calls attention to it.
- **Hilary quotations:** These lengthy passages from Hilary's *De Trinitate* are translated from Bonaventure's edition of the Lombard, not from Hilary directly; minor divergences from the critical text of Hilary are expected and left as-is per the brief's instruction to translate what is given.
