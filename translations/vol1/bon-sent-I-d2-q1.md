2. Likewise, the second supposition is that God is omnipotent. From this it is argued: therefore He could bring it about that every power other than His could do nothing: therefore if there are two gods diverse in nature, one can do this to the other — that the other could do nothing — and conversely. But that from which power can be taken away is not God: therefore if there are two gods, neither is God.

3. Likewise, the third supposition is that God is simply supreme. From this it is argued: therefore all things are below Him; therefore all things are from Him and toward Him. If therefore there are two gods, one is below the other and conversely; one is from the other according to a diverse nature and conversely; one is toward the other and conversely; but what is below another in nature and from another and toward another is not God: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, this can be proved by reduction to the impossible. If there are two gods, either one is where the other is, or not. If one is where the other is: then one is in the other, since they have the same mode of being: therefore one is material to the other: therefore the other is not God. If one is not where the other is: then each is limited, and therefore neither is infinite.

5. Likewise, if there are several good gods, either one understands the other or not. If not: then each is ignorant. If it does understand: either through presence, or through a species, or through itself as through an exemplar of that other. If through presence: then one is in the other, and God glides into God and perfects God; if through a species: then it is composite; if through an exemplar: then one is the exemplar of the other, and therefore also its principle.

6. Likewise, if there are two different gods, each of whom is the highest good: either one loves the other as it ought to be loved, or not. If so — since each is the highest good, each is to be loved with the love of enjoyment: therefore each enjoys the other; but one who enjoys another good beside itself is in need of it: therefore one is in need of the other: therefore each is needy, and therefore neither is God.

On the contrary: 1. God can do more than our intellect can think. But the human intellect — as that of the pagans — understands several omnipotent gods: therefore God can bring this about. But whatever can be in the divine nature is there, because it is eternal: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, more goods are better than fewer; but several gods are more goods: therefore two would be something better than one. But according to Anselm, everything that is better must be attributed to God: therefore several gods are to be posited.

3. Likewise, those whose operation is diverse have diverse power and diverse nature; but the operation of the three persons is diverse, since the appearance in the dove was of the Holy Spirit alone, and the Son alone assumed human nature: therefore they are diverse in substance: therefore there are several gods.

4. CONCLUSION.

That there are several gods is impossible — indeed, if one correctly understands what God is, it is not even conceivable.

I respond: It must be said that it is impossible for there to be several gods, and if the signification of this name "God" is correctly understood, it is not only impossible but not even conceivable. For "God" signifies the simply supreme both in reality and in the estimation of the one thinking. Because He is supreme in reality, all things are from Him and in Him and toward Him, and in Him rest is altogether; therefore it is impossible to understand, while preserving this concept, that anything other than Him be equal to Him. Likewise, nothing greater than God can be conceived, nor even anything equal, because He is the supreme in estimation. Therefore it is impossible and inconceivable to posit several gods.

1. To the objection, therefore, concerning the pagans: it must be said that this was not an act of understanding but of fiction; moreover, they were not understanding God according to the divine dignity — and therefore the objection does not hold.

And to the argument that God can do more, etc.: it must be said that there are two kinds of understanding — rational and phantasmatic. The first is true, but not the second; because we can imagine many things through the phantasy that God cannot bring about, since it does not belong to Him, "in whom what is unfitting is impossible."

2. To the objection that more goods are better than fewer: it must be said that this holds in the case of created and finite good, which by reason of its finitude admits an increase of goodness through the addition of another good; but it does not hold in the case of the infinite good, because however much you add infinite good to the infinite, I always understand there to be as much goodness in one as you do in many.

3. To the other objection concerning the diversity of operations: it must be said that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit agree in every operation, but differ in relation. Hence in the Incarnation there is the operation of producing that nature and there is the union; in the first the three persons agree, in the second they do not. — Similarly in the dove there is the formation of the dove and its signification; in the first they agree, in the second they do not. Augustine's example from On the Trinity is that for the formation of the word "memory," memory, understanding, and will all concur; yet the word "memory" signifies one of the powers. The case is similar in the matter at hand.

4. To the objection that great power, etc.: it must be said that to produce a person is threefold — either from oneself, or from some created thing, or from nothing. From Himself God can produce the simply supreme; but that one will not be another in nature, on account of the simplicity of the nature. From another thing or from nothing He cannot produce the simply supreme, but only the supreme in a genus — not on account of a defect of the acting power, but on account of a defect of the creature, which must necessarily be limited; and therefore He cannot produce another God.

### Notes

- **Opening (Q. I continued):** The chunk opens mid-argument, in the third sentence of the first *fundamentum* argument for the unity of the divine essence. Translated seamlessly from the prior chunk's end.
- **"Shares / communicates" (communicare):** The Quaracchi editors' footnote notes that *communicare aliquid cum aliquo* here means "to have something in common with another / to agree with another in something" — not the theological sense of divine self-communication used elsewhere. Rendered "share" or "have in common" throughout this argument to reflect this usage.
- **"In eternal things being and being able are the same" (in aeternis idem est esse et posse):** An Aristotelian axiom (*Physics* III) cited in both argument 4 *contra* and the *Conclusio*. Retained as a quotation. The point is that God's eternal omnipotence entails the actuality of whatever He can do — hence if He can produce another God, there already is another God.
- **"What is unfitting is impossible" (inconveniens est impossibile):** From Anselm's *Cur Deus Homo* I.20 — "however small an unfittingness in God is impossible." Bonaventure's compressed version distinguishes rational understanding (which cannot conceive multiple omnipotent gods without contradiction) from phantasmatic imagination (which can picture anything). Rendered following the Anselmian source.
- **"Rational and phantasmatic understanding" (intellectus rationalis / phantasticus):** A characteristic Bonaventurian distinction between genuine conceptual understanding (governed by logical constraints) and mere imaginative picturing (which has no such constraints). The pagans' polytheism was the latter, not the former.
- **Reply to objection 3 (diverse operations):** The argument that distinct Trinitarian operations (Incarnation of the Son, apparition of the Holy Spirit as dove) imply distinct natures was "the strongest objection of the Arians, Nestorians, Eutychians, and Paulianists," per Albert the Great (noted by the Quaracchi editors). Bonaventure's reply distinguishes the productive act (common to all three persons) from the relational terminus (proper to one person). The Augustine example of the word "memory" — where all three faculties contribute to forming the word, yet only memory is its referent — illustrates the distinction.
- **Reply to objection 4 (supreme production):** Bonaventure distinguishes three modes of production: from oneself (generation of the Son, which yields one who is supreme but not numerically other in nature), from a created thing, and from nothing. Only the first yields the simply supreme; the other two yield only the supreme in a genus. The conclusion — God cannot produce "another God" — rests on the impossibility of numerical otherness in nature without limitation.
