From this it is also clear why enjoyment is not defined through faith and hope as it is through charity, because charity directly moves the concupiscible power, whose act it is to enjoy. Yet this question is similarly founded upon a falsehood, because the love by which enjoyment is defined is common to chaste love and to disordered love, by which the miser enjoys gold, and is not proper to charity itself.

1. To what is therefore objected — that every power is delighted and at rest when united with its object — it must be said that the will itself, as Augustine holds, loves for itself and for others. And as Anselm also says: "The will inclines the other powers and merits on their behalf"; therefore its rest and delight overflow into the other powers. Whence just as the will does not know for itself, but reason knows for itself and for the will, so the will is delighted for itself and for reason and causes it to be at rest.

2. To what is objected — that the lover does not enjoy unless he sees or has — it must be said that seeing and having are required for enjoyment, and similarly loving. For if someone sees something and has it, he is never delighted unless he loves it; yet vision is required in a different way than love. For vision disposes, and so does possession, but love supplies the delight. Hence it is as it were a penetrating point, and therefore it is most properly suited to unite and consequently to delight and to bring to rest: therefore it is enjoyment essentially, not dispositively. For this reason it is to be understood that the act of the will can be considered in two ways, namely by way of appetite and by way of complacency. In the first way it can precede vision itself; in the second it follows, and in this consists the perfect account of enjoyment itself, namely in complacency with respect to the thing seen and possessed.

3. To what is objected — that vision is the whole reward — it must be said that this is not said properly, but by concomitance, because vision and complacency, in which the perfect account of enjoyment consists, are inseparably related.

4. To what is objected — that we enjoy by faith and hope — it must be said that this is not true through itself, because each of these receives the account of tending from charity; therefore enjoyment is not from these except through charity. For charity alone regards the end under the account of end and object. Hence one is not said to hope God as one is said to love him.

ARTICLE III.
On what is to be enjoyed.

Having shown what enjoyment is in its essence, the question is raised concerning what is to be enjoyed, and first, whether it is possible to enjoy God; second, whether he alone is to be enjoyed.

QUESTION I.
Whether God is to be enjoyed.

Concerning the first, that God is to be enjoyed is shown as follows.

1. "That is to be enjoyed which makes us blessed," because in blessedness there is right enjoyment; but by God we are made blessed, because he is our blessedness: therefore God is to be enjoyed.

2. Likewise, the good is lovable; therefore the more good is more lovable and the supremely good is supremely lovable; but we enjoy what we love supremely: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the beautiful delights, and what is more beautiful delights more, therefore what is supremely beautiful delights supremely; but we enjoy that in which we are supremely delighted: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. Where there is enjoyment there is delight; "but delight is the conjunction of the fitting with the fitting"; but between God and the creature there is no fittingness, but rather the greatest distance: therefore neither delight, therefore neither enjoyment.

2. Likewise, an excellent sensible object corrupts the sense or at least causes distress, because delight is in the middle terms: therefore by the same reasoning an excellent intelligible object corrupts or causes distress to the intellect. But God is the most excellent light: therefore he causes distress, not delight, therefore, etc. If you say that the sensory power is corruptible but the intellect is not — this does not resolve the difficulty, because the intellect, as long as it is in the flesh, is wearied and impeded in its act when the body is corrupted.

3. Likewise, where there is enjoyment there is rest; but in the infinite there is no rest, "because there is always something more to take outside": therefore since God is infinite, in God there is no rest, therefore neither enjoyment.

CONCLUSIO.

God properly is to be enjoyed.

I respond: It must be said that God is to be enjoyed, because he alone perfectly brings the soul to its end and delights it for its own sake and above all things.

### Notes

- **Chunk scope.** The text spans the conclusion of Art. II Q. Unica (responses to objections 1–4), then the full body of Art. III Q. I up to the point where the *Respondeo* breaks off mid-sentence within the treatment of the threefold analogy of fittingness. The translation follows the Latin exactly as supplied.

- **"Penetrating point"** (*acumen penetrans*). Bonaventure's image for love as that which most properly effects union, drawn from Hugh of St. Victor's commentary on Dionysius. Translated literally to preserve the metaphor.

- **"By way of appetite and by way of complacency"** (*per modum appetitus et complacentiae*). A key Bonaventurian distinction: appetite is the will's prior movement toward an object; complacency is its resting delight in the object once seen and possessed. This is the locus of *fruitio* properly speaking.

- **"Vision is the whole reward"** (*visio est tota merces*). Bonaventure's response — that vision is said to be the reward not properly but by concomitance with complacency — anticipates the later scholastic debate (noted in the Scholion) about whether beatitude consists formally in the act of intellect or of will.

- **"Delight is the conjunction of the fitting with the fitting"** (*delectatio est coniunctio convenientis cum convenienti*). A standard scholastic definition traceable to Avicenna, harmonized with Aristotle's account of pleasure. Rendered as a set phrase throughout.

- **"Delight is in the middle terms"** (*delectatio est in mediis*). An Aristotelian principle from *De Anima* and *Ethics*: pleasure arises from proportion, not from overwhelming excess. Translated literally.
