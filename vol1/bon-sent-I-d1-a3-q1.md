# I Sent., d. 1, a. 3, q. 1
## *Utrum Deo sit fruendum*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 38 -->

**QUAESTIO I.** *Utrum Deo sit fruendum.*

Circa primum, quod sit Deo fruendum, ostenditur sic.

1. «Eo fruendum est, quod beatos nos facit»[^1], quia in beatitudine est recta fruitio; sed Deo beati efficimur, quia ipse est nostra beatitudo: ergo Deo est fruendum.

2. Item, bonum est amabile; ergo magis bonum magis amabile et summe bonum summe amabile[^2]; sed eo fruimur, quod summe amamus: ergo etc.

3. Item, pulcrum delectat, et[^3] magis pulcrum magis delectat; ergo summe pulcrum summe delectat; sed eo fruimur, in quo summe delectamur: ergo etc.

**Sed contra.**

1. Ubi est fruitio, ibi est delectatio; «sed delectatio est coniunctio convenientis cum convenienti»[^4]; Dei autem ad creaturam nulla est convenientia, immo summa distantia: ergo nec delectatio, ergo nec fruitio.

2. Item, excellens sensibile corrumpit sensum vel saltem contristat, quia delectatio est in mediis[^5]: ergo pari ratione excellens intelligibile corrumpit vel contristat intellectum. Sed Deus est excellentissima lux: ergo contristat, non delectat, ergo etc. Si dicas, quod sensibilis potentia est corruptibilis, non intellectus; hoc non solvit, quia intellectus, dum est in carne, fatigatur et impeditur in actu, corpore corrupto.

3. Item, ubi fruitio, ibi quietatio; sed in infinito non est quietatio, «quia semper est aliquid extra accipere»[^6]: ergo cum Deus sit infinitus, in Deo non est quietatio, ergo nec fruitio.

**Conclusio.**

*Deo proprie est fruendum.*

**Respondeo.** Dicendum, quod Deo fruendum est, eo quod ipse solus perfecte finit[^7] et delectat ipsam animam propter se et super omnia.

**Ad 1.** Ad illud ergo quod obiicitur, quod delectatio est coniunctio convenientis etc.; dicendum, quod[^8] est convenientia per unius naturae participationem vel per comparationem communem. Prima convenientia facit communitatem univocationis, secunda communitatem analogiae sive proportionis. Et haec est secundum triplicem differentiam: aut secundum similem comparationem duorum ad duo, ut sicut homo[^9] ad animal, ita albedo se habet ad colorem; aut secundum dissimilem comparationem duorum ad unum, ut animalis et cibi ad sanitatem; vel

<!-- page 39 -->

secundum comparationem duorum ad invicem, ut puta cum unum est imitatio vel similitudo alterius. Similitudo enim non convenit cum consimili[^10] in tertio, sed se ipsa. Sic est in proposito, quia anima est expressa similitudo Dei. Haec convenientia in uno extremo ponit inclinationem et indigentiam, in alio quietationem et sufficientiam, quia unum[^11] factum est propter alterum, unde ordinatur ad alterum. Et quoniam ad inclinationis quietationem et indigentiae suppletionem, ubi est sensus[^12], est delectatio sive sequitur, ideo anima cum tali modo quietatur a Deo, fruitur eo.

**Ad 2.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod excellentia sensibilis corrumpit sensum: ergo etc.; dicendum, quod non est simile de intelligere et sentire. Et ad hoc est triplex ratio: una est ex parte virtutis apprehensivae, alia ex parte apprehensi sive obiecti, tertia est ex parte modi apprehendendi.

Ex parte virtutis apprehensivae, quia sensus potest corrumpi, intellectus autem non. Cuius ratio est, quia sensus dependet ab organo, in quo est quaedam medietas et harmonia, quae non tantum corrumpitur per contrarium, sed per excellens; sed intellectus non dependet ab organo, quia est vis immaterialis, ideo non tristatur in excellenti[^13][^14].

Ex parte obiecti non est simile, quia obiectum intelligibile[^15] excellens iuvat et confortat, quia influentia talis cognoscibilis procedit ab intimis et intrat ipsam potentiam, et ideo ipsam confortat et corroborat. Sicut si magnus mons daret virtutem portandi se, facilius ferretur quam parvus; sic est in intelligibili, quod Deus est. Sensibile autem obiectum tantum extra excitat, et ideo hoc corrumpit, illud autem non.

Ex parte modi apprehendendi similiter est dissimilitudo, quia sensus in apprehensione sui obiecti tendit ad exterius, unde percipiendo[^16] illud dispergitur exterius nec fortificatur interius, ideo debilitatur. Sed obiectum intellectus cum sit intimum ipsi intellectui, in eius perceptione virtus non dispergitur, sed colligitur, et quanto virtus est magis unita, tanto fortior.

**Ad 3.** Ad illud quod obiicitur de infinito; dicendum, quod infinitum dicitur dupliciter, scilicet per privationem perfectionis; et sic materia dicitur infinita, et talis infiniti non est finire, sed potius finiri indiget, secundum quod[^17] est possibile. Alio modo infinitum dicitur per privationem limitationis; et quod sic infinitum est, proprie habet finire, quoniam ultra ipsum, cum non sit maius cogitare, non contingit aliquid appetere. Unde talis infinitas convenit ultimo fini, quae[^18] maxime habet finiendi rationem.

## English

<!-- page 38 -->

**Question 1.** *Whether God is to be enjoyed.*

Concerning the first [question], that God is to be enjoyed, it is shown thus.

1. "That is to be enjoyed by which we are made blessed"[^1], since right enjoyment lies in beatitude; but we are made blessed by God, because he himself is our beatitude: therefore God is to be enjoyed.

2. Again, the good is lovable; therefore what is more good is more lovable, and what is highest good is most highly lovable[^2]; but we enjoy that which we love most highly: therefore etc.

3. Again, the beautiful delights, and[^3] what is more beautiful delights more; therefore what is most beautiful delights most; but we enjoy that in which we delight most highly: therefore etc.

**On the contrary.**

1. Where enjoyment is, there is delight; "but delight is the joining of what is befitting with what is befitting"[^4]; but there is no befittingness of God to a creature — rather, there is the greatest distance: therefore neither delight, therefore neither enjoyment.

2. Again, an excessive sensible [object] corrupts the sense, or at least pains it, because delight lies in things of a mean[^5]: therefore, by parity of reasoning, an excessive intelligible corrupts or pains the intellect. But God is the most excellent light: therefore he pains and does not delight, therefore etc. If you say that a sensible faculty is corruptible but not the intellect, this does not resolve [the difficulty], because the intellect, while it is in the flesh, is wearied and impeded in its act when the body is corrupted.

3. Again, where enjoyment is, there is rest; but in the infinite there is no rest, "because there is always something more to take on from outside"[^6]: therefore, since God is infinite, in God there is no rest, therefore neither enjoyment.

**Conclusion.**

*God, properly speaking, is to be enjoyed.*

**Reply.** I answer that God is to be enjoyed, because he alone perfectly brings to an end[^7] and delights the soul itself, for its own sake and above all things.

**Reply to 1.** To the objection that delight is the joining of what is befitting, etc., it must be said that[^8] there is a befittingness either by the sharing of one nature or by a common comparison. The first befittingness makes a univocal community; the second, a community of analogy or proportion. And this [latter] is according to a threefold difference: either according to a similar comparison of two things to two, as *just as* man[^9] stands to animal, so whiteness stands to color; or according to a dissimilar comparison of two to one, as of an animal and its food to health; or

<!-- page 39 -->

according to a comparison of two to each other — as, for instance, when one is an imitation or a likeness of the other. For a likeness does not agree with what is like[^10] in some third thing, but by itself. So it is in the case at hand, because the soul is an express likeness of God. This befittingness places in one of the two extremes inclination and indigence, and in the other rest and sufficiency, because the one[^11] is made for the sake of the other — hence it is ordered to the other. And since, where there is sense[^12], delight either is present or follows upon the stilling of inclination and the supplying of indigence, therefore the soul, when in this way it is brought to rest by God, enjoys him.

**Reply to 2.** To the objection that excellence in the sensible corrupts the sense, therefore etc., it must be said that understanding and sensing are not parallel cases. And for this there is a threefold reason: one on the side of the apprehensive power, another on the side of the apprehended thing or object, and a third on the side of the mode of apprehending.

*On the side of the apprehensive power*: because the sense can be corrupted, but the intellect cannot. The reason is that sense depends on an organ, in which there is a certain mean and harmony that is corrupted not only by a contrary but also by what is excessive; but the intellect does not depend on an organ, since it is an immaterial power, and therefore it is not pained by an excellent[^13] [object][^14].

*On the side of the object* it is not parallel, because an excessive intelligible[^15] object helps and strengthens [the intellect], since the influence of such a knowable thing proceeds from the innermost [parts] and enters into the faculty itself, and so it strengthens and reinforces it. Just as, if a great mountain were to give the power to carry itself, it would be carried more easily than a small one, so it is in the intelligible, which is God. But a sensible object only excites from outside, and therefore that [sensible] corrupts, whereas the [intelligible] does not.

*On the side of the mode of apprehending* there is likewise a dissimilarity, because the sense, in apprehending its object, tends outward — so that, in perceiving[^16] it, [the power] is dispersed outward and not strengthened inward, and so it is weakened. But since the object of the intellect is innermost to the intellect itself, in its perception the power is not dispersed but gathered; and the more the power is unified, the stronger it is.

**Reply to 3.** To the objection concerning the infinite, it must be said that *infinite* is said in two ways: namely, by the privation of perfection — and thus matter is called infinite, and such an infinite is not able to bring to an end, but rather needs to be brought to an end, insofar as[^17] that is possible. In another way the infinite is spoken of by the privation of limitation; and what is infinite in this way properly does have [the power] of bringing to an end, because, since nothing greater than it can be thought, beyond it nothing can be desired. Whence such infinity befits the ultimate end, which[^18] most of all has the character of bringing to an end.

- **Key terms** (per project glossary): *frui* → enjoy; *fruibile* → enjoyable / what is to be enjoyed; *delectatio* → delight; *convenientia* → befittingness / agreement (the technical term for the fit between two natures); *conveniens cum convenienti* → "what is befitting with what is befitting" (Bonaventure's definition of delight, via Avicenna and Aristotle's *Rhetoric*); *quietatio* → rest / stilling; *finire* → to bring to an end (with the paired sense of *quietare*, glossed in fn 7); *medietas / in mediis* → mean / in things of a mean; *per privationem perfectionis / limitationis* → by the privation of perfection / of limitation; *analogia proportionis / proportionalitatis* → analogy of proportion / of proportionality.

- **The Avicennan definition of delight.** Objection 1 *contra* and the reply ad 1 both turn on the definition *delectatio est coniunctio convenientis cum convenienti*. Fn 4 traces the pedigree from Aristotle's *Rhetoric* I through Avicenna's *Metaphysics* VIII to the scholastic commonplace. Bonaventure's reply distinguishes two kinds of *convenientia*: univocal (by shared nature) and analogical (by common comparison) — the latter itself subdivided into three kinds. The doctrine of analogy of proportionality here is a key early statement of the distinction later developed in d. 14, d. 29, d. 46, and d. 48.

- **Sense vs. intellect (Reply to 2).** The threefold contrast — on the side of power, of object, and of mode of apprehension — is the heart of this question and is densely cross-referenced by the Quaracchi editors to *De Anima* II–III. Fn 14 assembles the relevant Aristotelian loci: II text 123, text 143, III text 4, and text 7 (the passage on the intellect's unmixed impassibility). The English preserves the scholastic distinction between *virtus apprehensiva*, *apprehensum / obiectum*, and *modus apprehendendi*.

- **Two senses of "infinite" (Reply to 3).** Fn 17 documents competing readings at *secundum quod est possibile* near the end of the reply. Bonaventure's distinction — between the infinite by privation of perfection (matter) and the infinite by privation of limitation (God) — is the germ of the solution and is preserved carefully in the English.
