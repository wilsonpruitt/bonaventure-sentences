# I Sent., d. 1, a. 3, q. 2
## *Utrum solo Deo sive bono increato fruendum sit*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 39 -->

**QUAESTIO II.** *Utrum solo Deo sive bono increato fruendum sit.*

Ultimo quaeritur, utrum solo Deo sive bono increato sit fruendum. Et quod sic, videtur.

1. Illo solo est fruendum, quod est per se et propter se bonum; sed solum summum bonum est per se et propter se bonum, nam alia, quae bona sunt, propter ipsum sunt bona: ergo solo summo bono, quod Deus est, est fruendum.

<!-- page 40 -->

2. Item, illo solo est fruendum, quod quietat animum appetitum; sed solus Deus quietat animum sufficienter, quia tam longe distat ab omni creatura, ut nihil aliud impleat affectum animae, ut dicit Augustinus[^1]: «Fecisti nos ad te, Domine, et inquietum est cor nostrum, donec requiescat in te»: ergo solo Deo est fruendum.

3. Item, illo solo est fruendum, quod per se sufficienter delectat; sed aliquid delectat aut per suam animam delectari, aut suam appetitum, sed per suam animam, et ideo omne autem creatum impotens est animae rationali, ut tantum animae cor et beatitudinem implere possit Augustinus[^2]: «Homo totum, quod in Trinitatem capere Trinitas potest»: ergo solo bono increato est fruendum.

4. Item, illo soli est fruendum, quod cogitari, quod dicit Augustinus, est melius ipsum cogitari, et sic quod est melius appetere aut diligere cogitari; sed solum summum bonum[^3] est tale, quia maius non potest sufficientius cogitari: ergo solo summo bono est fruendum.

**Sed contra.**

1. Videtur, quod non solum bono increato sit fruendum, quia aliquid aliud ei coniungitur delectabiliter: sed videtur delectatio fieri, quod uni delectabiliter coniungitur, ei et frui oportet: ergo etc.

2. Item, videtur, quod frui liceat aliqua creatura materiali et sensibili; possumus enim delectari in eis, ut patet cum vero creatum creatum coniungitur animae et est eidem sufficientissimum, ut scribitur apparere. Sed cum aliqua creatura voluntatem sufficienter et sic sine angustia impleat, dicendum fruitione: ergo.

3. Item, videtur, quod homines aut finitae capacitatis, ergo non solum a Deo est fruendum, sed aliquo alio potest in finitum [impleri]: ergo non solum Deo est fruendum, sed aliquo alio creato.

4. Item, sicut se habet *homo* ad *hominem*, sic se habet *bonus* ad *bonum*; sed homo hominem diligit, fruitur et: ergo similiter bonus alius bonum amat et fruitur; sed non ad se ipsum et ad Deum et ad alium: ergo non solius Dei est fruitio.

**Conclusio.**

*Solo Deo proprie est fruendum; ingruente frui licet bonis spiritualibus, quae delectant et fini sunt coniuncta.*

**Respondeo.** Dicendum, quod solo Deo est fruendum, proprie accepto *frui*, prout dicit motum cum delectatione *et* quietatione; communiter tamen accepto *frui*, prout dicit motum cum delectatione *tantum*, omnibus quae spiritualiter delectant, et quae Deo sunt coniuncta, frui licet, non tamen illis quae cum ipso non sunt coniuncta per modum finis.

**Ad 1.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod delectatio est communis omnibus virtutibus: dicendum, quod omne quod convenit virtutibus convenit etiam Deo, vel per participationem vel per propagationem communem. Prima convenientia fundatur in communicatione *univocationis*, secunda communicatur *analogice sive proportionis*. Prima quidem convenientia non admittit *analogiae modum*, scilicet quod analogia requirat proportionum, hoc est similium comparationum inter divinum et creatum. Secunda autem — quod nobis principaliter incumbit — habet non minores graduum inter divinum et creatum, quam se habent ad se ipsa in diversis numero; et tamen divisiones comprehendunt univerque ut unum, et unum ut multa, et hoc ad modum qua id habet *habet*, et hoc non per contrarietatem aut per privationem imperfectionis, sed per abundantem plenitudinem.

<!-- page 41 -->

Ex parte autem apprehendendi similiter et sic similitudo, quia sicut in apprehensione sui obiecti est delectabile, et sic delectari et esse similiter, sic apprehendere operationis est in intimo ipsis creaturae hominem et intellectam. Cum ergo illuminatur intellectus ab intimo ipsorum, tantum excitat, et sic esse corrumpit, illud tanto magis apprehenditur, quanto Dei similitudo in ipsis remansit.

Ex parte apprehendendi similiter et ex parte appetitus habet se similitudo: obiectum intellectus est intimum ipsis, intellectum sentire, tanto magis apprehenditur, quanto creatura apprehenditur intellectu; si obiectum ipsi sit intimum, tantum vel quidem magis, et quod magis apprehenditur.

**Ad 2.** Ad illud quod obiicitur de infinito: dicendum, quod infinitum dicitur dupliciter: scilicet per *privationem perfectionis*, ut est materia divino infinita, et talis infiniti non est finis; sed per *perfectionem*, ut est Deus. Hoc modo intellectus duci potest in privationem limitationem, et quod infinitum non sit possibile. Hoc modo infinitum terminat ipsum intellectum, sicut ex rationibus praedictis apparet; et quia summum bonum omnem rationem finiendi habet, unde illa infinitas convenit ultimo fini, quia maxime habet finiendi rationem.

<!-- page 42 -->

**Ad 3.** Ad illud quod obiicitur de homine: dicendum, quod bonum hominis et amor Dei non sic iudicantur quam ratio est iudicis homonis. Et ideo ad frui Deo et creaturam non debemus. Homo enim debitur homini, quia sicut per dilectionem bonorum ad hominem, sic dilectio Dei communicari debet. Sed e contra se habet ordinatio secundum bonitatem; quia homo non potest iuvare Deum aut communicare; et ideo dilectio in Deum conferre nihil potest. Ratio ergo communicandi venit ex voluntate et intellectu, quia sola voluntas est mota a Deo et in Deum, et sic frui *in* Deo.

**Ad 4.** Ad illud quod obiicitur de amore: dicendum, quod bonum honestum est amabile, et propter se, et propter aliud, et etiam *per se simpliciter*. Utroque modo bonum honestum est, proprie loquendo, quia non solum est finis, sed quia terminat et quia solet fini terminari. Secundo modo bonum honestum est *utiliter*, quia sic referatur ad finem. Et sic *communicat se* simpliciter Deus se, qui est summus finis communium et inter omnium finitium.

**Ad 5.** Ad illud quod obiicitur de beatitudine: dicendum, quod beatitudo creata non fuit appetitum per se, sed per coniunctionem cum increata; et quia nomina dilecta sunt Deum, in quo dilecta sunt et ratione eorum in quibus Deum dicit diligere. Non tamen propter se.

### Scholion

I. Omnes antiqui doctores, exceptis Durando, conveniunt in sententia, quod solo Deo sit obiecto et materialiter sit fruendum, institutique vere invent aliter utenduo simpliciter voce *frui* ei debito. Doctor in solut. ad 5. non explicat proprie loquendi quaestionem, sed scholasticos aliae et sic deponuntur. Hoc per conceptus simpliciter considerantur. Si enim respicitur ad significationem strictam, voce *frui* solus Deus est bonum increatum; quia *sic* est delectabile, quod eius bonitas, neque separatim, omnia bona complectens, quomodo quibuscumque bonis inest. Sic quidem intelligi potest solum solum increatum esse, quia in hoc modo nulla creatura est *qua sine* additione alicuius possit animum sufficienter beatificare, quod, quomodo etiam incomplete et finibiliter expetitur tum imperfecte etiam per modum finis. Plane omnibus concessum est communicationem Dei ea *ratio fruitionis*, qua eae quae sunt ad finem et quibus per concomitantiam ordinamur in finem, si pariter fruitio dici possint, ita quidem ut explicare scilicet S. Doctor in posterioribus quaestionibus, magis distingue facientibus II. Sent. d. 38, a. 1, q. 2. et q. 3. Quoad quaestionem principalem vide lectorem in Scholio praecedenti.

II. Alex. Hal., S. p. III, q. 60, m. 2 et 3. — S. Thom., hic q. 2, a. 2 et 3; S. I–II, q. 11, a. 3. — B. Albert., hic a. 16 et 17. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 1, a. 3. — Richard., hic a. 3, q. 2. — Henr. Gand., S. a. 8, q. 2. — Durand., hic q. 3. — Dionys. Carth., hic q. 5. — Scot., hic q. 4 et 5. — Biel, hic q. 3.

## English

<!-- page 39 -->

**Question 2.** *Whether God alone, or only the uncreated good, is to be enjoyed.*

Lastly the question is asked whether God alone, or only the uncreated good, is to be enjoyed. That he is, seems to follow thus.

1. That alone is to be enjoyed which is good *in itself* and *for its own sake*; but only the highest good is good in itself and for its own sake, since other things that are good are good on its account: therefore only the highest good — that is, God — is to be enjoyed.

<!-- page 40 -->

2. Again, that alone is to be enjoyed which *brings rest* to the appetite of the soul; but only God brings sufficient rest to the soul, since he stands so far above every creature that nothing else can fill the affection of the soul. As Augustine says[^1]: "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you": therefore only God is to be enjoyed.

3. Again, that alone is to be enjoyed which of itself *sufficiently delights*. But every created thing is incapable of filling the whole heart of the rational soul, since — as Augustine says[^2] — "man is capable of the whole Trinity; the whole Trinity can be received in him": therefore only the uncreated good is to be enjoyed.

4. Again, that alone is to be enjoyed than which nothing greater can be conceived — and that, says Augustine, is the highest good: therefore only the highest good is to be enjoyed.[^3]

**On the contrary.**

1. It seems that enjoyment is not reserved to the uncreated good alone, since whatever is joined to it delightfully may itself be a source of delight: but delight seems to lie in that to which one is delightfully joined; therefore one enjoys that as well.

2. Again, it seems that enjoyment is permitted of some material and sensible creature; for we can be delighted in them, especially when they are joined to the soul as most fitting for it.

3. Again, it seems that man is of finite capacity; therefore he is filled not only by God but can be filled by something else besides: therefore he is not to enjoy God alone, but also something else created.

4. Again, as *man* is to *man*, so is *good* to *good*; but man loves and enjoys man: therefore the good man likewise loves and enjoys the other [created] good; but he does not refer [all love] to himself and to God alone: therefore enjoyment is not of God alone.

**Conclusion.**

*God alone is properly to be enjoyed; but one may also enjoy spiritual goods that delight and are joined to the [last] end.*

**Reply.** I answer that God alone is to be enjoyed, taking *enjoyment* in its proper sense — that is, as motion accompanied *both* by delight *and* by rest; but taking *enjoyment* in the common sense — as motion accompanied by delight alone — it is permitted to enjoy all things that spiritually delight and that are joined to God. It is not, however, permitted to enjoy those things which are not joined to him in the manner of an end.

**Reply to 1.** To the objection that delight is common to all the virtues: it must be said that everything that belongs to creatures belongs also to God, either *by participation* or *by analogy of proportion*. The first kind of agreement is grounded in the communion of *univocity*; the second is communicated *analogically*, or by proportion. The first does not admit the mode of analogy (namely, that analogy requires a comparison of proportions between the divine and the created). But the second — which is principally at stake here — holds between the divine and the created in a way that does not diminish the difference of degrees between them; the divine comprehends in itself all that the creature has, not by contrariety or by privation of imperfection, but by abundant fullness.

<!-- page 41 -->

On the side of *apprehension* likewise, there is a similitude: for just as in the apprehension of its object something is delightful — and so there is delight and rest — , so in the operation of [the intellect] there is what is inmost to the creature itself and to what is understood. When therefore the intellect is illumined from within [by God], it is excited in proportion; and so each thing is the more deeply apprehended, the more the divine likeness remains in it.

On the side of apprehension, and on the side of appetite too, the similitude holds: the object of the intellect is what is inmost to it, so the more it is understood the more deeply it is apprehended; if the object of the intellect is what is inmost, it is even more so what is apprehended.

**Reply to 2.** To the objection from the infinite: it must be said that "infinite" is said in two ways — namely, *by privation of perfection* (as matter is called divinely infinite, and such infinity is not an end), and *by perfection* (as God is infinite). In this latter sense the intellect cannot be drawn into a privative limit: such an infinite is not possible in that sense. Rather, the infinite in the second sense terminates the intellect itself, as is clear from the reasons already given; and since the highest good has every character of being an end, that infinity belongs to the ultimate end — for it most of all has the character of an end.

<!-- page 42 -->

**Reply to 3.** To the objection from man: it must be said that the good of man and the love of God are not judged in the same way as the judgment of man about man. Therefore we ought not to *enjoy* God and a creature on the same footing. For man owes to man what he owes by way of the love of goods that pertain to man; but so the love of God must also be communicated to him. But the ordering that corresponds to goodness is the reverse: since man cannot help God or communicate anything to him, love for God depends solely on the side of man. The ground of communication therefore comes from will and from intellect: for the will alone is moved by God and into God — and in this is *enjoyment* in God.

**Reply to 4.** To the objection from love: it must be said that the upright good is lovable both *for its own sake* and *for the sake of another* — indeed, *for its own sake simply*. In both ways the upright good is properly so called, because it is not only an end but that which terminates, and that which is usually terminated by an end. In the second way the upright good is *useful*, because it is referred to the end. And in this way God, who is the highest end of all, communicates himself simply to his own.

**Reply to 5.** To the objection from beatitude: it must be said that created beatitude is not desired for its own sake, but by way of conjunction with the uncreated; and because the things that are loved on account of God are loved *in* him, and for the sake of that in which God is said to love [them]. They are not, however, loved for their own sake.

### Scholion

**I.** All the older doctors — with the exception of Durandus — agree in the doctrine that only God is, as proper object and materially, to be enjoyed, and that in the strict sense *enjoyment* is owed to him alone. The holy Doctor in his reply to obj. 5 does not treat the question in the most strict terms, but in the manner scholastics commonly use: for if we take *enjoyment* in its strict signification, only God — the uncreated good — is enjoyable, since *he alone* is delightful in such a way that his goodness, by comprehending all goods in itself, leaves no need for any further addition. In this sense alone the *uncreated* is the object of enjoyment, since no creature can of itself beatify the soul sufficiently; and yet in an incomplete and finite way creatures too may be sought *per modum finis* (in the manner of an end). It is conceded on all sides that God communicates to *his own things* a certain *ratio fruitionis*, according to which the things that lead to the end, and through which by concomitance we are ordered toward the end, may also be called objects of enjoyment — as the holy Doctor more fully works out in the later questions of II *Sent.*, d. 38, a. 1, qq. 2–3. For the main question, see the Scholion above.

**II.** Alexander of Hales, *Summa*, pt. III, q. 60, mm. 2 and 3. — Thomas Aquinas, here q. 2, aa. 2–3; *Summa theologiae* I–II, q. 11, a. 3. — Albert the Great, here aa. 16–17. — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 1, a. 3. — Richard of Mediavilla, here a. 3, q. 2. — Henry of Ghent, *Summa* a. 8, q. 2. — Durandus, here q. 3. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 5. — Scotus, here qq. 4–5. — Biel, here q. 3.

## Notes

- **Chunk scope.** This chunk covers the second question of Art. III (*De fruibili*) in its entirety: the header and first *videtur quod* on printed p. 39 (below the Scholion for Q. I), the remaining three *videtur quod* arguments and all four *sed contra* arguments on p. 40, the *conclusio* and the long *respondeo* spanning pp. 40–41, the five *ad*-replies spanning pp. 41–42, and the two-part scholion that closes Art. III on p. 42 (before the opening of the *Dubia*).

- **Title.** The printed title on p. 39 is "Utrum solo Deo sive bono increato fruendum sit" — "Whether God alone, or only the uncreated good, is to be enjoyed." This is distinct from Q. I of the same Art. III (*Utrum Deo sit fruendum*, already rebuilt as `bon-sent-I-d1-a3-q1.md`): Q. I asks *whether God is enjoyable at all*, Q. II asks *whether God is the only proper object of enjoyment*.

- **Pairing with `d1-a1-q3`.** This question is Bonaventure's *fruition* counterpart to Art. I Q. III (*Utrum solo bono creato utendum sit*, "Whether only the created good is to be used"). Together they establish the complementary division: the created good is the proper object of *use*; the uncreated good is the proper object of *enjoyment*. The two questions should be read as a pair — their *conclusio* and *respondeo* work in tandem to split the Augustinian *uti / frui* schema along the created/uncreated axis.

- **Chunking correction.** The legacy chunk `vol1/legacy/bon-sent-I-d1-a3-q2.md` was present but incomplete and shared content with other chunks in the legacy tree. The rebuild captures the full question body plus both scholion paragraphs. Legacy file moved to `vol1/legacy/`.

- **Key terms** (per project glossary): *frui* → enjoy; *fruibile* → enjoyable; *bonum honestum* → the upright good; *bonum utile* → the useful good; *per se / propter se* → in itself / for its own sake; *communiter / proprie* (of *frui*) → commonly / properly (here mapped onto the *delectatio* vs. *delectatio + quietatio* distinction); *per modum finis* → in the manner of an end.

- ***Solo Deo proprie est fruendum.*** The center of gravity of this question is the carefully hedged conclusion: God *alone* is the proper object of *frui* in the strict sense (motion with both delight and rest), while spiritual goods that are joined to God may also be called objects of enjoyment in the looser sense (motion with delight only). The English preserves Bonaventure's precise contrast between *proprie accepto frui* and *communiter accepto frui*.

- **Analogy of proportion in the reply to obj. 1.** Bonaventure's reply to the first *sed contra* draws a distinction between two kinds of agreement between creatures and God: *univocity* (grounded in shared nature) and *analogy of proportion* (grounded in a relation of likeness that preserves the qualitative gap between divine and created). The English uses "by participation" / "by analogy of proportion" to mark this contrast, since it is the technical heart of the reply.
