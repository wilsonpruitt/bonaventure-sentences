# I Sent., d. 1, a. 3, q. 2
## *Utrum solo Deo sive bono increato fruendum sit*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 39 -->

### Quaestio II. *Utrum solo Deo sive bono increato fruendum sit.*

Ultimo quaeritur, utrum solo Deo sive bono increato sit fruendum; et quod sic, videtur.

**Fundamenta.**

1. Illo solo est fruendum, quod est propter se diligendum; sed illud solum[^1] est propter se diligendum, quod est propter se bonum; illud autem propter se est bonum, quod bonitatem, quam habet, nec habet ab alio, nec bonitas illa est *ad aliud*; hoc autem est solus Deus: ergo etc.

<!-- page 40 -->

2. Item, illo solo est fruendum, quod quietat animae appetitum; sed animae appetitus non quietatur sufficienter nisi in eo quod est illa perfectius et maius bonum; hoc autem est solus Deus: ergo etc.

3. Item, appetitus non sufficienter quietatur nisi per aliquid, quod animam implet, quia ex ea parte anima deficit in quiete, qua deficit in plenitudine; sed[^2] est capax Dei secundum appetitum: ergo si omne creatum est in infinitum minus illo, nullum supplet animae appetitum. Et hoc est quod dicit Augustinus[^3]: «Animam totius Trinitatis capacem nihil minus quam Trinitas potest implere».

4. Item, bonum, quod potest cogitari, potest appeti: ergo si aliquo maius bonum potest cogitari, maius illo potest appeti; sed omni finito maius potest cogitari: ergo maius potest appeti: ergo nullum finitum terminat animae appetitum sufficienter: ergo solo bono infinito, quod Deus est, est fruendum, quo habito, non potest amplius cogitari nec desiderari.

**Contra:**

1. Videtur, quod bono creato contingat frui, quia, quamvis appetitus materiae sit infinitus, tamen per formam creatam incorruptibilem[^4] sufficienter finitur: ergo similiter videtur, quod sit aliquod bonum creatum, quod sufficienter finiet animae appetitum; sed omni tali est fruendum: ergo aliquo bono creato est fruendum.

2. Item, appetitus humanus est finitae capacitatis, ergo non capit nisi finitum, vel si capiat infinitum, capit finite; ergo si finiatur per illud quod capit, finitur per bonum finitum: ergo videtur, quod aliquo bono finito sit fruendum.

3. Item, specialiter videtur, quod homine sit fruendum, quia omni eo est fruendum, quo Deus fruitur; sed Deus homine fruitur, quia hominem amat; sed amore illo, quo amat se, amat hominem, quia non est in eo[^5] duplex amor; sed primus est fruitionis: ergo et secundus.

4. Item, videtur specialiter de virtute; quia omni eo est fruendum, quod est propter se appetendum; haec est manifesta per se; sed omne bonum honestum est propter se appetendum, «quia honestum est quod sua vi nos trahit et dignitate sua nos allicit», sicut dicit Tullius[^6], et Augustinus dividit honestum contra utile; sed virtus cadit in genere honesti: ergo virtute est fruendum.

5. Item, videtur specialiter de beatitudine. Omni eo est fruendum, quo habito animus quiescit et delectatur et nihil ultra potest quaerere; sed beatitudo creata est huiusmodi: ergo etc.

### Conclusio.

> Solo Deo proprie est fruendum; improprie frui licet bonis spiritualibus, quae delectant et fini sunt coniuncta.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod solo Deo est fruendum, proprie accepto frui, prout dicit motum cum delectatione et quietatione. Sed communiter accepto frui, prout dicit motum cum delectatione tantum[^7], omnibus, quae spiritualiter delectant et coniuncta sunt fini, cuiusmodi sunt fructus, dona et beatitudines, potest frui homo non indebite; sed primo modo solo Deo.

Et ratio huius est, quia nihil potest animam sufficienter finire nisi bonum, ad quod est[^8]. Hoc autem est bonum summum, quod superius est anima, et bonum infinitum, quod excedit animae vires. Cognitio enim animae naturalis[^9] est cognitio non arctata; unde nata est quodam modo omnia cognoscere, unde non impletur cognitio eius aliquo cognoscibili, nisi quod habet in se omnia cognoscibilia et quo cognito omnia cognoscuntur. Similiter affectio eius nata est diligere omne bonum; ergo nullo bono sufficienter finitur affectus, nisi quod[^10] est bonum omnis boni et quod est omnia in omnibus.

<!-- page 41 -->

De quo bono Exodi trigesimo tertio[^11]: *Ostendam tibi omne bonum*. Hoc autem est summum bonum: ergo etc.

Item[^12], nihil sufficienter finit animam nisi bonum infinitum, quia ad hoc, ut finiatur, necesse est, ipsam finiri secundum aestimationem; alioquin non esset beata, nisi se existimaret beatam. Aestimatio autem supergreditur omne finitum, quia omni finito potest maius cogitari aliquid: ergo cum affectio possit[^13] se extendere ubi et aestimatio, necessario affectio animae supergreditur omne finitum; et si hoc, nullo bono finito sufficienter finitur. Fruendum est ergo solo Deo, quia summum bonum et infinitum.

Et non solum Deo fruendum propter perfectam finitionem, sed etiam propter perfectam delectationem. Ad delectationem enim concurrit[^14] delectabile et coniunctio eius cum eo quod delectatur. Omne autem, quod delectat animam, delectat in ratione boni et pulcri; et quoniam solus Deus est ipsa[^15] bonitas et pulcritudo, ideo in Deo solo est perfecta delectatio.

Item, quia ibi est coniunctio; solus autem Deus est qui perfectissime coniungitur. Nam coniungitur secundum[^16] veritatem et intimitatem. Solus enim Deus propter summam simplicitatem et spiritualitatem illabitur animae, ita quod secundum veritatem est in anima et intimior animae quam ipsa sibi.

Omnes enim[^17] hae quatuor rationes ad unam reducuntur, scilicet ad hanc: quia nata est anima ad percipiendum bonum infinitum, quod Deus est, ideo in eo solo debet quiescere et eo frui.

**Ad argumenta pro parte opposita:**

1. *Ad illud* vero quod obiicitur de appetitu materiae; dicendum, quod appetitus materiae ordinatur ad formam tanquam ad perfectionem substantialem, ex qua[^18] et materia fit unum; et ideo necesse est, formam esse eiusdem generis cum materia, nec est omnino supra materiam: ideo finita est omnis talis forma. Talis autem, si sit incorruptibilis, propter unionem[^19] arctat appetitum materiae et arctatum finit; unde non finitur in sua amplitudine. Appetitus autem animae non sic arctatur propter unionem cum appetibili, immo dilatatur; et ideo oportet, quod perficiatur aliquo omnino summo.

2. *Ad illud* quod obiicitur, quod[^20] non capit nisi finite; dicendum, quod infinitum bonum finite capit, quoniam ipsa est finita. Sed quoniam bonum illud est infinitum, ideo ab ipso totaliter absorbetur, ut iam eius capacitas undique terminetur. Unde non tantum gaudebit, sed sicut dicit Anselmus[^21], *in gaudium Domini introibit*. Quod si tantum caperet et non vinceretur nec absorberetur, adhuc posset insurgere appetitus ad amplius aliquid capiendum. Patet igitur, quod ad hoc quod anima compleatur, quamvis capacitatem habeat finitam[^22], tamen necesse est adesse bonum infinitum.

3. *Ad illud* vero quod obiicitur de homine; dicendum, quod Deus diligit hominem, et amor Dei est amor fruitionis; tamen homine non fruitur, quia diligendo se diligit hominem, id est, fruendo se utitur homine. Quod patet sic: quia dilectio Dei ad creaturam non est affectio, sed effectus[^23] communicatio. Communicatio autem secundum rationem habitualem attenditur secundum bonitatem; quia bonum est quod natum est se communicare. Secundum rationem vero actualem attenditur sive respicit voluntatem; quia enim vult, ideo facit. Ratio ergo communicandi venit ex voluntate et bonitate, sicut ratio cognoscendi ab exemplari et intelligentia. Quia igitur ex conversione voluntatis supra suam bonitatem nos diligit[^24], ideo se fruendo nos diligit.

4. *Ad illud* quod obiicitur de virtute, quod virtus est bonum honestum; dicendum, quod bonum honestum dicitur dupliciter: aut quod est pure bonum, aut in quo relucet eius similitudo. Primo modo honesto est fruendum, proprie accepto frui, quia hoc solum terminat et quietat. Secundo modo non, nisi communiter accipiatur, prout dicit delectationem.

<!-- page 42 -->

Huiusmodi enim[^25] propter assimilationem ad Deum habent pulcritudinem, qua nos delectant et alliciunt; tamen in ipsis non est quiescendum, quia tunc amittunt pulcritudinem, quia «virtutes, cum ad se referuntur, superbae sunt et inflatae[^26]».

5. *Ad illud* quod obiicitur de beatitudine; dicendum, quod beatitudo creata non finit appetitum per se, sed per coniunctionem cum increata: unde anima ipsam[^27] diligit propter Deum, non propter se.

### Scholion

**I.** Omnes antiqui doctores, excepto Durando, conveniunt in ea sententia, quod solo Deo obiective et materialiter sit fruendum, beatitudine vero creata sive beatitudine subiective sumta (ut moderni loquuntur), formaliter tantum. Seraphicus Doctor in solut. ad 5. iam reprobaverat Durandi (hic. q. 2.) singularem et falsam opinionem, quod actus mentis, quo Deus immediate attingitur, sit fruitionis obiectum immediatum, Deus vero eiusdem obiectum remotum.

**II.** Quomodo quis improprie frui possit virtutibus, magis explicatur II. Sent. d. 38. a. 1. q. 2. ad 3, ubi etiam exponitur, quomodo dona, fructus et beatitudines coniuncta sint fini, qui secundum Scripturam est caritas. — Valde notanda sunt profunda et copiosa argumenta, quibus Seraphicus probat, quod in Deo solo sit perfecta delectatio et quietatio, de quo etiam egregie loquitur II. Sent. d. 16. a. 1. q. 1. Cfr. etiam S. Thom. S. c. Gent. I. c. 43; III. c. 48. 50. — Quoad illapsum Dei in animam cfr. II. Sent. d. 8 p. II. a. 1. q. 2.

Quoad quaestionem principalem vide auctores in Scholio praecedenti.

## English

### Question II. *Whether God alone, or only the uncreated good, is to be enjoyed.*

Lastly it is asked whether God alone, or only the uncreated good, is to be enjoyed; and that it is so, seems [evident].

**Foundations.**

1. That alone is to be enjoyed which is to be loved for its own sake; but that alone[^1] is to be loved for its own sake which is good for its own sake; and that is good for its own sake which neither has from another the goodness it has, nor is that goodness *in relation to anything else*; but this is God alone: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, that alone is to be enjoyed which quiets the appetite of the soul; but the soul's appetite is not sufficiently quieted save in that which is a more perfect and greater good than itself; but this is God alone: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, the appetite is not sufficiently quieted save through something which fills the soul, since the soul fails in rest on that side on which it fails in fullness; but [the soul][^2] is capable of God according to appetite: therefore, if every created thing is infinitely less than that [God], no [created thing] supplies the soul's appetite. And this is what Augustine[^3] says: «Nothing less than the Trinity can fill a soul that is capable of the whole Trinity».

4. Likewise, the good which can be thought can be desired: therefore, if a greater good than some [given good] can be thought, a greater than that can be desired; but a greater than every finite [good] can be thought: therefore a greater can be desired: therefore no finite [thing] sufficiently terminates the soul's appetite: therefore the only infinite good, which is God, is to be enjoyed, which once possessed, nothing further can be thought or desired.

**On the contrary:**

1. It seems that the created good may be enjoyed, since although the appetite of matter is infinite, yet through a created incorruptible form[^4] it is sufficiently terminated: therefore similarly it seems that there is some created good which sufficiently terminates the soul's appetite; but every such [good] is to be enjoyed: therefore some created good is to be enjoyed.

2. Likewise, the human appetite is of finite capacity; therefore it does not grasp save the finite, or if it grasps the infinite, it grasps finitely; therefore if it is terminated by that which it grasps, it is terminated by a finite good: therefore it seems that some finite good is to be enjoyed.

3. Likewise, specially it seems that the human being is to be enjoyed, since that is to be enjoyed by which God enjoys; but God enjoys the human being, since he loves the human being; but with that love with which he loves himself, he loves the human being, since there is not in him[^5] a twofold love; but the first is of enjoyment: therefore also the second.

4. Likewise, specially it seems concerning virtue; since that is to be enjoyed which is to be desired for its own sake; this is manifest per se; but every honorable good is to be desired for its own sake, «since the honorable is what by its own force draws us and by its own dignity attracts us», as Tully[^6] says, and Augustine divides the honorable against the useful; but virtue falls in the genus of the honorable: therefore virtue is to be enjoyed.

5. Likewise, specially it seems concerning beatitude. That is to be enjoyed by the possession of which the mind rests and is delighted, and can seek nothing further; but created beatitude is of this kind: therefore, etc.

### Conclusio.

> God alone is properly to be enjoyed; it is improperly permitted to enjoy spiritual goods, which delight and are conjoined to the end.

**I respond:** It must be said that God alone is to be enjoyed, taking "to enjoy" properly, insofar as it expresses a motion with delight and quieting. But, taking "to enjoy" commonly, insofar as it expresses a motion with delight only[^7], a human being can not unfittingly enjoy all things which spiritually delight and are conjoined to the end—of which kind are the fruits, gifts, and beatitudes; but in the first mode [he can enjoy] God alone.

And the reason for this is that nothing can sufficiently terminate the soul except the good for which it exists[^8]. But this is the highest good, which is above the soul, and the infinite good, which exceeds the powers of the soul. For the soul's natural cognition[^9] is a cognition not constrained; whence it is born in a certain way to cognize all things, whence its cognition is not filled by any cognoscible save what has in itself all cognoscibles and by which once cognized all things are cognized. Likewise its affection is born to love every good; therefore the affection is not sufficiently terminated by any good save by that which[^10] is the good of every good and which is "all in all".

Of which good [it is said] in Exodus the thirty-third[^11]: *I will show thee every good*. But this is the highest good: therefore, etc.

Likewise[^12], nothing sufficiently terminates the soul save the infinite good, because for it to be terminated, it is necessary that it be terminated according to estimation; otherwise it would not be blessed, unless it esteemed itself blessed. But estimation transcends every finite [thing], since something greater than every finite [thing] can be thought: therefore, since affection can[^13] extend itself where also estimation [extends], necessarily the soul's affection transcends every finite [thing]; and if this, [it is] not sufficiently terminated by any finite good. Therefore God alone is to be enjoyed, since [he is] the highest and infinite good.

And not only is God to be enjoyed on account of perfect termination, but also on account of perfect delight. For to delight there concurs[^14] the delightable and the conjunction of it with that which is delighted. But everything which delights the soul delights under the account of good and beautiful; and since God alone is goodness and beauty itself[^15], therefore in God alone is there perfect delight.

Likewise, because there is conjunction; but God alone is the one who is most perfectly conjoined. For he is conjoined according to[^16] truth and intimacy. For God alone, on account of supreme simplicity and spirituality, glides into the soul, so that according to truth he is in the soul and more intimate to the soul than [the soul] itself is to itself.

For all[^17] these four reasons reduce to one, namely to this: that since the soul is born to perceive the infinite good, which is God, therefore in him alone it ought to rest and enjoy him.

**To the arguments for the opposite side:**

1. *To that* which is objected concerning the appetite of matter; it must be said that the appetite of matter is ordered to form as to substantial perfection, out of which[^18] also matter becomes one [thing]; and therefore it is necessary that the form be of the same genus as the matter, nor is it altogether above matter: therefore every such form is finite. But such [a form], if it be incorruptible, on account of union[^19] constrains the appetite of matter and once constrained terminates it; whence it is not terminated in its [own] amplitude. But the appetite of the soul is not so constrained on account of union with the desirable, rather it is dilated; and therefore it is required that it be perfected by something altogether highest.

2. *To that* which is objected, that[^20] [the soul] does not grasp save finitely; it must be said that it grasps the infinite good finitely, since [the soul] itself is finite. But since that good is infinite, therefore by it [the soul] is wholly absorbed, so that its capacity is on every side terminated. Whence it will not so much rejoice, but, as Anselm[^21] says, *it will enter into the joy of the Lord*. For if it merely grasped and were not conquered and absorbed, the appetite could still rise up to grasp something further. It is therefore evident that, for the soul to be completed, although it has a finite capacity[^22], yet it is necessary that the infinite good be present.

3. *To that* which is objected concerning the human being; it must be said that God loves the human being, and the love of God is the love of enjoyment; yet he does not enjoy the human being, since by loving himself he loves the human being, that is, by enjoying himself he uses the human being. Which is evident thus: that God's love toward the creature is not affection, but [a] communication of effect[^23]. But communication, according to habitual account, is attended to according to goodness; since the good is what is born to communicate itself. According to actual account, however, it is attended to or regards the will; since because [God] wills, therefore he acts. Therefore the account of communicating comes from will and goodness, just as the account of cognizing [comes] from the exemplar and intelligence. Since therefore from the conversion of his will upon his own goodness he loves us[^24], therefore by enjoying himself he loves us.

4. *To that* which is objected concerning virtue, that virtue is an honorable good; it must be said that the honorable good is said in two ways: either as what is purely good, or as that in which its likeness shines forth. In the first mode the honorable is to be enjoyed, taking "to enjoy" properly, since this alone terminates and quiets. In the second mode not, unless it be taken commonly, insofar as it expresses delight.

For such [virtues][^25] on account of assimilation to God have beauty, by which they delight and attract us; nevertheless in them one is not to rest, since then they lose their beauty, since «the virtues, when they are referred to themselves, are proud and inflated[^26]».

5. *To that* which is objected concerning beatitude; it must be said that created beatitude does not terminate the appetite per se, but through conjunction with the uncreated: whence the soul loves it[^27] on account of God, not on account of itself.

### Scholion

**I.** All the ancient doctors, except Durandus, agree in this opinion: that God alone is to be enjoyed objectively and materially, but [that] in created beatitude—or in beatitude taken subjectively (as the moderns speak)—only formally. The Seraphic Doctor in the reply to the fifth [objection] had already rejected the singular and false opinion of Durandus (here, q. 2.), [namely] that the act of the mind by which God is immediately attained is the immediate object of enjoyment, while God is the remote object of the same.

**II.** How one can improperly enjoy the virtues is more fully explained in II Sent. d. 38. a. 1. q. 2. ad 3, where it is also expounded how the gifts, fruits, and beatitudes are conjoined to the end, which according to Scripture is charity. — The profound and copious arguments by which the Seraphic [Doctor] proves that in God alone is perfect delight and quieting are greatly to be noted, on which subject he also speaks excellently in II Sent. d. 16. a. 1. q. 1. Cfr. also S. Thom., S. c. Gent. I. c. 43; III. c. 48. 50. — As to the gliding-in of God into the soul, cfr. II Sent. d. 8 p. II. a. 1. q. 2.

As to the principal question, see the authors in the preceding Scholion.

[^9]: **La.** *Naturalis* refertur ad *cognitio*, ita ut sensus sit: *Si inspicias naturam cognitionis, quae animae competit, ipsa non est arctata.* — Vat., contradicentibus mss. et ed. 1, *rationalis* loco *naturalis*, sed non ita bene. — De propositione, quae deinde sequitur, vide Aristot., III. *de Anima*, text. 4. in q. praeced. cit.

    **En.** *Naturalis* refers to *cognitio*, so that the sense is: *If you inspect the nature of the cognition which belongs to the soul, it is not constrained.* — The Vatican [ed.], against the manuscripts and ed. 1, has *rationalis* in place of *naturalis*, but not so well. — On the proposition which then follows, see Aristotle, *On the Soul* III, text 4, cited in the preceding question.

[^10]: **La.** Cod. K adiungit *per illud*, quae additio videtur adiuncta, quia supplendum est *eo* sive *illo*.

    **En.** Cod. K adds *per illud* (through that), which addition seems adjoined, since *eo* or *illo* must be supplied.

[^13]: **La.** Vat. contra codd. A T aa bb etc. *posset*, sed minus apte, quia agitur hic de naturali consecutione, quae subiecto semper inest. K more plainly: *et si hoc, ergo*.

[^19]: **La.** Multi codd. antiquiores et meliores ut ACFGLORSTUXYZ etc. ponunt *sibi* pro *sui*. Mox cod. T post *unde* falso omittit *non*. — Sententiam hic positam S.
