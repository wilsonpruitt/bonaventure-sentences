# I Sent., d. 1, a. 3, q. 2
## *Utrum solo Deo sive bono increato fruendum sit*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 39 -->

**QUAESTIO II.** *Utrum solo Deo sive bono increato fruendum sit.*

Ultimo quaeritur, utrum solo Deo sive bono increato sit fruendum; et quod sic, videtur.

**Fundamenta.**

1. Illo solo est fruendum, quod est propter se diligendum; sed illud solum[^1] est propter se diligendum, quod est propter se bonum; illud autem propter se est bonum, quod bonitatem, quam ha-

<!-- page 40 -->

bet, nec habet ab alio, nec bonitas illa est ad aliud; hoc autem est solus Deus: ergo etc.

2. Item, illo solo est fruendum, quod quietat animae appetitum; sed animae appetitus non quietatur sufficienter nisi in eo quod est illa[?] perfectius et maius bonum; hoc autem est solus Deus: ergo etc.

3. Item, appetitus non sufficienter quietatur nisi per aliquid, quod animam implet, quia ex ea parte anima deficit in quiete, qua deficit in plenitudine; sed[^2] est capax Dei secundum appetitum: ergo si omne creatum est in infinitum minus illo, nullum supplet animae appetitum. Et hoc est quod dicit Augustinus[^3]: «Animam totius Trinitatis capacem nihil minus quam Trinitas potest implere».

4. Item, bonum, quod potest cogitari, potest appeti: ergo si aliquo maius bonum potest cogitari, maius illo potest appeti; sed omni finito maius potest cogitari: ergo maius potest appeti: ergo nullum finitum terminat animae appetitum sufficienter: ergo solo bono infinito, quod Deus est, est fruendum, quo habito, non potest amplius cogitari nec desiderari.

**Contra:**

1. Videtur, quod bono creato contingat frui, quia, quamvis appetitus materiae sit infinitus, tamen per formam creatam incorruptibilem[^4] sufficienter finitur: ergo similiter videtur, quod sit aliquod bonum creatum, quod sufficienter finiet animae appetitum; sed omni tali est fruendum: ergo aliquo bono creato est fruendum.

2. Item, appetitus humanus est finitae capacitatis, ergo non capit nisi finitum, vel si capiat infinitum, capit finite; ergo si finiatur per illud quod capit, finitur per bonum finitum: ergo videtur, quod aliquo bono finito sit fruendum.

3. Item, specialiter videtur, quod homine sit fruendum, quia omni eo est fruendum, quo Deus fruitur; sed Deus homine fruitur, quia hominem amat; sed amore illo, quo amat se, amat hominem, quia non est in eo[^5] duplex amor; sed primus est fruitionis: ergo et secundus.

4. Item, videtur specialiter de virtute; quia omni eo est fruendum, quod est propter se appetendum; haec est manifesta per se; sed omne bonum honestum est propter se appetendum, «quia honestum est quod sua vi nos trahit et dignitate sua nos allicit», sicut dicit Tullius[^6], et Augustinus dividit honestum contra utile; sed virtus cadit in genere honesti: ergo virtute est fruendum.

5. Item, videtur specialiter de beatitudine. Omni eo est fruendum, quo habito animus quiescit et delectatur et nihil ultra potest quaerere; sed beatitudo creata est huiusmodi: ergo etc.

**Conclusio.**

> *Solo Deo proprie est fruendum; improprie frui licet bonis spiritualibus, quae delectant et fini sunt coniuncta.*

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod solo Deo est fruendum, proprie accepto *frui*, prout dicit motum cum delectatione et quietatione. Sed communiter accepto *frui*, prout dicit motum cum delectatione tantum, omnibus, quae spiritualiter delectant et coniuncta sunt fini, cuiusmodi sunt fructus, dona et beatitudines, potest frui homo non indebite; sed primo modo solo Deo.

Et ratio huius est, quia nihil potest animam sufficienter finire nisi bonum, ad quod est[^7]. Hoc autem est bonum summum, quod superius est anima, et bonum infinitum, quod excedit animae vires. Cognitio enim animae naturalis[^8] est cognitio non arctata; unde nata est quodam modo omnia cognoscere, unde non impletur cognitio eius aliquo cognoscibili, nisi quod habet in se omnia cognoscibilia et quo cognito omnia cognoscuntur. Similiter affectio eius nata est diligere omne bonum; ergo nullo bono sufficienter finitur affectus, nisi quod[^9] est bonum omnis boni et quod est omnia in omnibus. De quo

<!-- page 41 -->

bono Exodi trigesimo tertio[^10]: *Ostendam tibi omne bonum*. Hoc autem est summum bonum: ergo etc.

Item[^11], nihil sufficienter finit animam nisi bonum infinitum, quia ad hoc, ut finiatur, necesse est, ipsam finiri secundum aestimationem; alioquin non esset beata, nisi se existimaret beatam. Aestimatio autem supergreditur omne finitum, quia omni finito potest maius cogitari aliquid: ergo cum affectio possit[^12] se extendere ubi et aestimatio, necessario affectio animae supergreditur omne finitum; et si hoc[^12], nullo bono finito sufficienter finitur. Fruendum est ergo solo Deo, quia summum bonum et infinitum.

Et non solum Deo fruendum propter perfectam finitionem, sed etiam propter perfectam delectationem. Ad delectationem enim concurrit[^13] delectabile et coniunctio eius cum eo quod delectatur. Omne autem, quod delectat animam, delectat in ratione boni et pulcri; et quoniam solus Deus est ipsa[^14] bonitas et pulcritudo, ideo in Deo solo est perfecta delectatio.

Item, quia ibi est coniunctio; solus autem Deus est qui perfectissime coniungitur. Nam coniungitur secundum[?] veritatem et intimitatem. Solus enim Deus propter summam simplicitatem et spiritualitatem illabitur animae, ita quod secundum veritatem est in anima et intimior animae quam ipsa sibi[^15].

Omnes enim[^16] hae quatuor rationes ad unam reducuntur, scilicet ad hanc: quia nata est anima ad percipiendum bonum infinitum, quod Deus est, ideo in eo solo debet quiescere et eo frui.

**Ad 1.** Ad illud vero quod obiicitur de appetitu materiae; dicendum, quod appetitus materiae ordinatur ad formam tanquam ad perfectionem substantialem, ex qua[^17] et materia fit unum; et ideo necesse est, formam esse eiusdem generis cum materia, nec est omnino supra materiam: ideo finita est omnis talis forma. Talis autem, si sit incorruptibilis, propter[?] unionem arctat appetitum materiae

<!-- page 42 -->

et arctatum finit; unde non finitur in sua amplitudine. Appetitus autem animae non sic arctatur propter unionem cum appetibili, immo dilatatur; et ideo oportet, quod perficiatur aliquo omnino summo.

**Ad 2.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod[^18] non capit nisi finite; dicendum, quod infinitum bonum finite capit, quoniam ipsa est finita. Sed quoniam bonum illud est infinitum, ideo ab ipso totaliter absorbetur, ut iam eius capacitas undique terminetur. Unde non tantum gaudebit, sed sicut dicit Anselmus[^19], in gaudium Domini introibit. Quod si tantum caperet et non vinceretur nec absorberetur, adhuc posset insurgere appetitus ad amplius aliquid capiendum. Patet igitur, quod ad hoc quod anima compleatur, quamvis capacitatem habeat finitam[^20], tamen necesse est adesse bonum infinitum.

**Ad 3.** Ad illud vero quod obiicitur de homine; dicendum, quod Deus diligit hominem, et amor Dei est amor fruitionis; tamen homine non fruitur, quia diligendo se diligit hominem, id est, fruendo se utitur homine. Quod patet sic: quia dilectio Dei ad creaturam non est affectio, sed effectus[^21] communicatio. Communicatio autem secundum rationem habitualem attenditur secundum bonitatem; quia bonum est quod natum est se communicare. Secundum rationem vero actualem attenditur sive respicit voluntatem; quia enim vult, ideo facit. Ratio ergo communicandi venit ex voluntate et bonitate, sicut ratio cognoscendi ab exemplari et intelligentia. Quia igitur ex conversione voluntatis supra suam bonitatem nos diligit[^22], ideo se fruendo nos diligit.

**Ad 4.** Ad illud quod obiicitur de virtute, quod virtus est bonum honestum; dicendum, quod bonum honestum dicitur dupliciter: aut quod est pure bonum, aut in quo relucet eius similitudo. Primo modo honesto est fruendum, proprie accepto *frui*, quia hoc solum terminat et quietat. Secundo modo non, nisi communiter accipiatur, prout dicit delectationem. Huiusmodi enim[^23] propter assimilationem ad Deum habent pulcritudinem, qua nos delectant et alliciunt; tamen in ipsis non est quiescendum, quia tunc amittunt pulcritudinem, quia «virtutes, cum ad se referuntur, superbae sunt et inflatae»[^24].

**Ad 5.** Ad illud quod obiicitur de beatitudine; dicendum, quod beatitudo creata non finit appetitum per se, sed per coniunctionem cum increata: unde anima ipsam[?] diligit propter Deum, non propter se.

### Scholion

**I.** Omnes antiqui doctores, excepto Durando, conveniunt in ea sententia, quod solo Deo *obiective* et *materialiter* sit fruendum, beatitudine vero creata sive beatitudine *subiective* sumta (ut moderni loquuntur), *formaliter* tantum. Seraphicus Doctor in solut. ad 5. iam reprobaverat Durandi (hic q. 2.) singularem et falsam opinionem, quod actus mentis, quo Deus immediate attingitur, sit fruitionis obiectum immediatum, Deus vero eiusdem obiectum remotum.

**II.** Quomodo quis improprie frui possit virtutibus, magis explicatur II *Sent.* d. 38, a. 1, q. 2 ad 3, ubi etiam exponitur, quomodo dona, fructus et beatitudines coniuncta sint fini, qui secundum Scripturam est caritas. — Valde notanda sunt profunda et copiosa argumenta, quibus Seraphicus probat, quod in Deo solo sit perfecta delectatio et quietatio, de quo etiam egregie loquitur II *Sent.* d. 16, a. 1, q. 1. Cfr. etiam S. Thom., S. c. Gent. I, c. 43; III, c. 48. 50.[?] — Quoad illapsum Dei in animam cfr. II *Sent.* d. 8, p. II, a. 1, q. 2.

Quoad quaestionem principalem vide auctores in Scholio praecedenti.

## English

<!-- page 39 -->

**Question II.** *Whether God alone, or only the uncreated good, is to be enjoyed.*

Lastly the question is asked whether God alone, or only the uncreated good, is to be enjoyed; and that he is, seems [to be the case].

**Foundations.**

1. That alone is to be enjoyed which is to be loved for its own sake; but that alone[^1] is to be loved for its own sake which is good for its own sake; and that is good for its own sake which has the goodness it has from no other, and whose goodness is not [ordered] to another;

<!-- page 40 -->

but this is God alone: therefore etc.

2. Again, that alone is to be enjoyed which brings rest to the appetite of the soul; but the appetite of the soul is not sufficiently brought to rest except in that which is more perfect and a greater good than itself[?]; but this is God alone: therefore etc.

3. Again, the appetite is not sufficiently brought to rest except by something which fills the soul, since the soul falls short of rest in just that respect in which it falls short of fulness; but[^2] [the soul] is capable of God according to its appetite: therefore if every created thing is infinitely less than him, no created thing meets the appetite of the soul. And this is what Augustine says[^3]: "Nothing less than the Trinity can fill the soul, which is capable of the whole Trinity".

4. Again, the good which can be thought can be desired: therefore if a good greater than something can be thought, a greater than it can be desired; but a greater can be thought than every finite thing: therefore a greater can be desired: therefore no finite thing terminates the appetite of the soul sufficiently: therefore only by the infinite good, which is God, is enjoyment to be had — for, this being possessed, nothing further can be thought or desired.

**On the contrary:**

1. It seems that one may enjoy the created good, since, although the appetite of matter is infinite, yet by an incorruptible created form[^4] it is sufficiently terminated: therefore, similarly, it seems that there is some created good which will sufficiently terminate the appetite of the soul; but everything of that kind is to be enjoyed: therefore some created good is to be enjoyed.

2. Again, the human appetite is of finite capacity; therefore it grasps only the finite, or, if it grasps the infinite, it grasps it finitely; therefore if it is terminated by what it grasps, it is terminated by a finite good: therefore it seems that some finite good is to be enjoyed.

3. Again, in particular it seems that man is to be enjoyed, since everything is to be enjoyed which God enjoys; but God enjoys man, since he loves man; but with that love by which he loves himself, he loves man, since there is not in him[^5] a twofold love; but the first [love] is of enjoyment: therefore the second is also.

4. Again, in particular concerning virtue: since everything is to be enjoyed which is desirable for its own sake — this is self-evident; but every upright good (*bonum honestum*) is desirable for its own sake, "since the upright is that which by its own force draws us, and by its own dignity allures us", as Tullius [Cicero] says[^6], and Augustine divides the upright over against the useful; but virtue falls under the genus of the upright: therefore virtue is to be enjoyed.

5. Again, in particular concerning beatitude. Everything is to be enjoyed by the having of which the soul rests and is delighted, and can seek nothing further; but created beatitude is of this kind: therefore etc.

**Conclusion.**

> *God alone is properly to be enjoyed; improperly, one may enjoy spiritual goods which delight and are joined to the [last] end.*

**I respond:** It must be said that God alone is to be enjoyed, taking *to enjoy* properly — namely, as it signifies a motion with delight and rest. But taking *to enjoy* commonly — as it signifies a motion with delight only — a man may, not improperly, enjoy all things that spiritually delight and are joined to the [last] end (such as the fruits, the gifts, and the beatitudes); but in the first way [he may enjoy] God alone.

And the reason for this is that nothing can sufficiently terminate the soul except the good for which it is made[^7]. But this is the highest good, which is above the soul, and the infinite good, which exceeds the powers of the soul. For the natural[^8] cognition of the soul is a cognition not narrowed down; whence it is born to know all things in a certain way, and so its cognition is not filled by any knowable thing except that which has within itself all knowables, and by knowing which all things are known. Likewise its affection is born to love every good; therefore its affection is not sufficiently terminated by any good except that which[^9] is the good of every good and that which is all in all. Of which

<!-- page 41 -->

good [we read] in Exodus thirty-three[^10]: *I will show thee every good*. But this is the highest good: therefore etc.

Likewise[^11], nothing sufficiently terminates the soul except the infinite good, since, in order that it be terminated, it is necessary that it be terminated according to estimation; otherwise it would not be blessed unless it esteemed itself blessed. But estimation transcends every finite [thing], since something greater can be thought than every finite thing: therefore, since affection can[^12] extend itself to where estimation also [reaches], necessarily the affection of the soul transcends every finite thing; and if so[^12], it is not sufficiently terminated by any finite good. Therefore one is to enjoy God alone, since [he is] the highest good and the infinite.

And not only is God to be enjoyed because of the perfect termination, but also because of the perfect delight. For to delight there concur[^13] the delightful thing and its conjunction with that which is delighted. But everything that delights the soul delights under the formal character of the good and the beautiful; and since God alone is goodness and beauty themselves[^14], therefore in God alone is there perfect delight.

Likewise, [it is in God alone] because there [in him] there is conjunction; and God alone is the one who is most perfectly conjoined. For he is conjoined according to[?] truth and intimacy. For God alone, on account of the highest simplicity and spirituality, glides into the soul, in such a way that according to truth he is in the soul and more intimate to the soul than the soul itself is to it[^15].

For all[^16] these four reasons reduce to one, namely this: that the soul is born to receive the infinite good, which is God, and therefore in him alone ought it to rest and him to enjoy.

**Reply to 1.** To the objection from the appetite of matter: it must be said that the appetite of matter is ordered to form as to a substantial perfection, from which[^17] also matter becomes one; and so it is necessary that the form be of the same genus as the matter, and that it not be altogether above matter: therefore every such form is finite. But such [a form], if it be incorruptible, by reason of[?] the union narrows the appetite of matter

<!-- page 42 -->

and once narrowed terminates it; whence it is not terminated in its [full] amplitude. But the appetite of the soul is not narrowed in this way by union with the desirable; rather it is widened; and so it is necessary that it be perfected by something altogether highest.

**Reply to 2.** To the objection that[^18] [the soul] grasps only finitely: it must be said that it grasps the infinite good finitely, since [the soul] itself is finite. But since that good is infinite, therefore it is wholly absorbed by it, so that its capacity is on every side terminated. Whence it will not so much rejoice [over the joy] as, as Anselm says[^19], it will enter into the joy of its Lord. For if it only grasped [the good] but were not overcome and absorbed [by it], the appetite could still rise up to grasping something further. It is clear therefore that, in order that the soul be completed — although it has finite capacity[^20] — yet it is necessary that the infinite good be present.

**Reply to 3.** To the objection from man: it must be said that God loves man, and the love of God is the love of enjoyment; yet he does not enjoy man, since by loving himself he loves man — that is, by enjoying himself he uses man. Which is plain thus: that the love of God toward the creature is not affection, but a communication of an effect[^21]. But communication, according to its habitual character, is regarded according to goodness; for the good is what is born to communicate itself. According to its actual character, [communication] is regarded as, or has reference to, will; for it is because he wills, that he acts. Therefore the ground of communicating comes from will and from goodness, just as the ground of cognizing [comes] from the exemplar and from intelligence. Since therefore from the conversion of his will above his own goodness he loves us[^22], therefore by enjoying himself he loves us.

**Reply to 4.** To the objection from virtue, that virtue is the upright good: it must be said that the upright good is said in two ways — either [as] that which is purely good, or [as] that in which its [the highest good's] likeness shines forth. In the first mode the upright is to be enjoyed, *to enjoy* being taken properly, since this alone terminates and brings rest. In the second mode it is not [to be enjoyed], unless [enjoyment] be taken commonly, insofar as it signifies delight. For things of this kind[^23], by reason of their being made like to God, have a beauty by which they delight and allure us; yet one is not to come to rest in them, since then they lose their beauty, since "the virtues, when they are referred to themselves, are proud and puffed up"[^24].

**Reply to 5.** To the objection from beatitude: it must be said that created beatitude does not terminate the appetite of itself, but through conjunction with the uncreated; whence the soul loves it[?] for the sake of God, not for its own sake.

### Scholion

**I.** All the ancient doctors, with the exception of Durandus, agree in this opinion: that God alone is *objectively* and *materially* to be enjoyed, while created beatitude — that is, beatitude taken *subjectively* (as the moderns speak) — is [to be enjoyed] only *formally*. The Seraphic Doctor in his solution to [obj.] 5 had already rejected the singular and false opinion of Durandus (here, q. 2), which holds that the act of mind by which God is immediately attained is the immediate object of enjoyment, while God is the remote object of the same.

**II.** How one may improperly enjoy the virtues is more fully explained at II *Sent.* d. 38, a. 1, q. 2 ad 3, where it is also expounded how the gifts, fruits, and beatitudes are joined to the end, which according to Scripture is charity. — Strongly to be noted are the deep and copious arguments by which the Seraphic [Doctor] proves that in God alone is there perfect delight and rest, on which subject he also speaks excellently at II *Sent.* d. 16, a. 1, q. 1. Cf. also St. Thomas, *Summa contra Gentiles* I, c. 43; III, cc. 48, 50.[?] — As to the inflowing of God into the soul, cf. II *Sent.* d. 8, p. II, a. 1, q. 2.

As to the principal question, see the authors in the preceding Scholion.

- **Pairing with `d1-a3-q1`.** This question is Bonaventure's *fruition* counterpart to Art. I Q. III (*Utrum solo bono creato utendum sit*, "Whether only the created good is to be used"). Together they establish the complementary division: the created good is the proper object of *use*; the uncreated good is the proper object of *enjoyment*. The two questions should be read as a pair — their *Conclusio* and *Respondeo* work in tandem to split the Augustinian *uti / frui* schema along the created/uncreated axis.

- **Key terms** (per project glossary): *frui* → enjoy; *fruibile* → enjoyable; *bonum honestum* → the upright good; *bonum utile* → the useful good; *per se / propter se* → in itself / for its own sake; *communiter / proprie* (of *frui*) → commonly / properly (here mapped onto the *delectatio* vs. *delectatio + quietatio* distinction); *per modum finis* → in the manner of an end; *aestimatio* → estimation; *finire* → to terminate / bring to an end (paired with *quietare*); *illabi* → to glide into / inflow upon (of God's presence to the soul).

- ***Solo Deo proprie est fruendum.*** The center of gravity of this question is the carefully hedged conclusion: God *alone* is the proper object of *frui* in the strict sense (motion with both delight and rest), while spiritual goods that are joined to God may also be called objects of enjoyment in the looser sense (motion with delight only). The English preserves Bonaventure's precise contrast between *proprie accepto frui* and *communiter accepto frui*.
