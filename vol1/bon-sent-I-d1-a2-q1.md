# I Sent., d. 1, a. 2, q. unica
## *Utrum frui sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 35 -->

**ARTICULUS II.** *Quid sit* frui.

Habito de *uti* et *utibili*, consequenter quaeritur de *frui* et *fruibili*, et primo, quid sit *frui* per essentiam, utrum scilicet sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium.

**QUAESTIO UNICA.** *Utrum* frui *sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium.*

Quod sit actus voluntatis, videtur per primam definitionem, quae est: «*Frui* est amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se ipsam»[^1]; sed amor est voluntatis: ergo et *frui* similiter. Si dicas, quod amor sumitur ibi communiter, secundum quod est in qualibet vi respectu sui actus: contra, in omnibus aliis definitionibus idem dicitur. Unde dicitur in sequenti[^2]: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus voluntas delectata conquiescit». Quietatio enim voluntatis est. Et in alia[^3] similiter: «Frui est cum gaudio uti». Gaudium autem ad voluntatem proprie pertinet.

<!-- page 36 -->

2. Item, ostenditur ratione, quia fruitione delectamur[^4], secundum quod delectabile est differentia boni, cum sit bonum honestum, conferens et delectabile; bonum autem obiectum est voluntatis: ergo *frui* est solius voluntatis.

3. Item, eo fruimur, quo quietamur; sed quietatio respicit rationem finis, «et finis rationem boni»[^5], et «bonum est obiectum voluntatis»: ergo fruitio, quae ordinat ad illud, similiter est voluntatis.

**Sed contra.**

1. Omnis virtus appetit uniri suo obiecto, quo habito delectatur, si cognoscit, et quiescit: ergo motus cum quietatione et delectatione est omnium virium: ergo cum talis sit fruitio, fruitio erit in omnibus viribus, non tantum in voluntate.

2. Item, absentia rei amatae potius contristat amantem quam delectat[^6]; quod enim delectat ipsum, hoc est, quia videt ipsam et habet. Si ergo motus fruitionis est cum delectatione vel est ipsa delectatio: ergo hoc est, quia videt illud in quo est ipsa delectatio; sed visio respicit cognitivam: ergo etc.

3. Item, Augustinus in libro de *Doctrina christiana*[^7]: «Haec est summa merces, ut ipso perfruamur». Sed super illud Psalmi: *Ostendam illi salutare etc.*, dicit Glossa[^8], quod «visio est tota merces»: ergo si fruitio est merces, fruitio est visio per essentiam; sed visio est in cognitiva sive in ratione: ergo etc.

4. Item, fide et spe tendimus in Deum: aut ergo fruendo, aut utendo; sed non utendo, cum Deo non sit utendum: ergo fruendo: ergo fide et spe fruimur; sed fides est habitus rationis cognitivae; sed cuius est habitus, eius est actus: ergo actus fruitionis est actus rationis; similiter videtur de irascibili: ergo etc.

**Conclusio.**

*Frui sumtum essentialiter est actus voluntatis; sumtum dispositive est actus etiam aliarum potentiarum.*

**Respondeo.** Secundum aliquos[^9] *frui* pertinet ad omnes vires; et hoc dicunt, quia omnes vires remunerabuntur. Qui dicunt etiam quod *frui* in gloria erit magis proprie rationis, quia immediatius se habet ad delectationem.

Sed aliter videtur dicendum secundum Augustinum, scilicet quod sit actus ipsius voluntatis. Cum enim tres dentur definitiones de *frui*, omnes dantur penes actum voluntatis, quem tripliciter est considerare. Primo modo *communiter*, prout dicit motum cum delectatione; et sic definit Augustinus[^10]: «Frui est uti cum gaudio». Secundo modo, prout dicit motum cum quietatione; et hoc modo definitur ab Augustino de *Doctrina christiana*: «Frui est amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se ipsam»; et hoc modo accipitur *proprie*. Tertio modo accipitur, prout complectitur utrumque, scilicet quietationem et delectationem; et hoc modo definitur ab Augustino decimo *de Trinitate*: «Frui est quiescere in cognitis, voluntate propter se delectata»; et sic accipitur *propriissime*.

Quia ergo *frui* secundum omnem acceptionem dicit delectationem vel quietem vel utrumque, et omne tale habet rationem boni, et hoc est obiectum voluntatis: ideo loquendo essentialiter, *frui* est actus voluntatis. Sed quia voluntas nec delectatur nec quietatur nisi in eo quod cognoscit (vel per fidem vel per speciem), et in eo quod habet vel per spem vel in re, ideo actus aliarum virium ad hunc disponunt, non tamen sunt ipsum *frui* essentialiter loquendo.

<!-- page 37 -->

Ex hoc patet solutio ad illud quod communiter solet quaeri, quare *frui* non definitur per actum cognitionis, sicut per actum delectationis. Tamen haec quaestio fundatur super falsum: Augustinus enim ponit in notificationem eius quod dicit: «*Frui* est cognitis», non sicut dispositionem, sed sicut dispositum; et sic cognitio est dispositio ad *frui*, non autem *frui* per essentiam.

**Ad 1.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, cum virtus habet delectari et quiescere in suo obiecto: dicendum, quod per Anselmum[^11] voluntas inclinat alias vires et illas movet et regit, et ideo requiritur ad delectationem inclinatio aliarum virium. Unde sicut voluntas non sibi cognoscit, sed regit cognitionem et facit sibi cognoscendo, sic etiam a cognitione et aliis viribus non accipitur fruitio tanquam eis per se conveniens, sed quia voluntas per eas sibi *fruitur*.

**Ad 2.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod viator non fruitur, quia videt quod non habet: dicendum, quod *videre* et *habere* requiruntur ad *frui*, similiter et *amare*: ambo enim videt quis vel habet; sed aliter requiritur visio, aliter requiritur amor. Nam visio et habere requiruntur dispositive, amor autem requiritur essentialiter. Et ideo nullum inconveniens est, fruitionem esse actum voluntatis tanquam radicem, licet exigat dispositiones in aliis viribus.

**Ad 3.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod fide et spe fruimur: dicendum, quod ista quae inducuntur non sunt proprie de fide et spe, sed per concomitantiam. Voluntas enim per illas habitus tendit in obiectum suum et in eo quiescit; sed ipsum tendere et quiescere est voluntatis, licet per actus cognitivae disponatur.

**Ad 4.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod actus fruitionis est actus rationis: dicendum, quod fide et spe tendimus in Deum quantum ad dispositionem, sed quantum ad consummationem actus, hoc est voluntatis. Similiter et de irascibili dicendum est, quod eius actus disponit, non autem consummat fruitionem.

### Scholion

**I.** In solutione principali huius quaestionis, scil. *quod frui sit actus voluntatis*, consentiunt fere omnes scholastici, praesertim inter doctores antiquos. Conveniunt Alex. Hal., Richard., B. Albert. et S. Thom., licet pars illorum — et praecipue S. Thom. — quatenus eiusmodi actum formaliter considerat quasi actus intellectivae nonnihil recedat a S. Doctore. Re vera neuter ipsorum a fundamentali doctrina Augustiniana discedit.

**II.** Alex. Hal., *Summa* p. II, q. 62, m. 1. — S. Thom., hic q. 1, a. 1; *Summa theol.* I–II, q. 11. — B. Albert., hic a. 1 et 2. — Richard., hic a. 2, q. 1. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 1, a. 1. — Henr. Gand., S. a. 8, q. 1. — Scot., hic q. 2 et *Quodl.*, q. 16. — Durand., hic q. 1. — Dionys. Carth., hic q. 3. — Biel, hic q. 2.

## English

<!-- page 35 -->

**Article 2.** *What* enjoyment *is.*

Having treated of *use* and the *usable*, we next ask about *enjoyment* and the *enjoyable*, and first, what *enjoyment* is in essence — namely, whether it is an act of the will, or of the other powers.

**Sole Question.** *Whether* enjoyment *is an act of the will, or of the other powers.*

That it is an act of the will seems to follow from the first definition, which is: "*To enjoy* is to cling with love to some thing for its own sake"[^1]; but love is of the will; therefore *enjoyment* likewise [is of the will]. If one replies that *love* is here taken in a general sense — as found in every power with respect to its own act —, one may answer: in all the other definitions the same is said. For in the next one it is said[^2]: "We enjoy the things we know, in which the will takes its delighted rest." But rest is of the will. And in another[^3] it is similarly said: "To enjoy is to use with joy"; and joy properly pertains to the will.

<!-- page 36 -->

2. Again, it is shown from reasoning: because we are delighted in enjoyment[^4], and *delightful* is a differentia of the good (since it is the good that is upright, useful, and delightful); but the good is the object of the will: therefore *enjoyment* belongs to the will alone.

3. Again, we enjoy that in which we come to rest; but rest bears upon the character of an end, "and an end upon the character of the good"[^5], and "the good is the object of the will": therefore enjoyment, which orders toward that [end], likewise [belongs to the will].

**On the contrary.**

1. Every power desires to be united with its object, delights in it once possessed (if it knows it), and comes to rest in it: therefore motion-with-rest-and-delight belongs to every power: therefore, since enjoyment is such a motion, enjoyment will be in all [powers], not only in the will.

2. Again, the absence of the beloved thing saddens the lover more than it delights him[^6]; for what delights him is the fact that he sees it and has it. If, then, the motion of enjoyment is accompanied by delight or is the delight itself: this is so because he *sees* that in which the delight consists; but seeing belongs to the cognitive [power]: therefore etc.

3. Again, Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*[^7]: "This is the highest reward — that we enjoy Him." But on the verse of the Psalm *I will show him my salvation, etc.*, the Gloss says[^8] that "the vision [of God] is the whole reward": therefore if enjoyment is the reward, enjoyment is vision in essence; but vision is in the cognitive [power] or in reason: therefore etc.

4. Again, by faith and by hope we tend toward God: either, then, by enjoying or by using; but not by using, since God is not to be used: therefore by enjoying: therefore we enjoy by faith and hope; but faith is a habit of the cognitive reason; and whose is the habit, his is the act: therefore the act of enjoyment is an act of reason. The same seems to hold for the irascible [appetite]: therefore etc.

**Conclusion.**

*"To enjoy" taken essentially is an act of the will; taken dispositively, it is an act of the other powers as well.*

**Reply.** According to some[^9], *enjoyment* pertains to all the powers; and they say this because all the powers will be rewarded. They also say that *enjoyment* in glory will belong more properly to reason, because reason bears more immediately upon delight.

But it seems rather that we should say with Augustine that it is the act of the will itself. For since three definitions of *enjoyment* are given, all of them are framed in terms of an act of the will, which can be considered in three ways. First, *commonly*, insofar as it expresses motion with delight; and thus Augustine defines it[^10]: "To enjoy is to use with joy." Second, insofar as it expresses motion with rest; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: "To enjoy is to cling with love to some thing for its own sake"; and in this way it is taken *properly*. Third, it is taken insofar as it embraces both — rest and delight — ; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in Book X *On the Trinity*: "To enjoy is to rest in what one knows, the will being delighted for its own sake"; and in this way it is taken *most properly*.

Since, therefore, *enjoyment* in every acceptation expresses delight or rest or both, and every such thing has the character of the good, and this is the object of the will: it follows that, speaking essentially, *enjoyment* is an act of the will. But because the will neither delights nor rests except in what it knows (either by faith or by species), and in what it has (either by hope or in reality), the acts of the other powers *dispose* toward this [act]; they are not, however, enjoyment itself, essentially speaking.

<!-- page 37 -->

From this is clear the reply to what is commonly asked: why *enjoyment* is not defined by the act of cognition as it is by the act of delight. But this question rests on a false premise: Augustine in fact puts cognition into the description of what he says as that *in which* enjoyment takes place — not as its disposition, but as that-which-is-disposed. And so cognition is a disposition to *enjoyment*, not enjoyment itself in essence.

**Reply to 1.** To the objection that every power has delight and rest in its own object: it must be said that, according to Anselm[^11], the will inclines the other powers and moves and governs them, and therefore the inclination of the other powers is required for [the will's] delight. Hence just as the will does not itself know, but governs cognition and causes cognition for itself, so from cognition and the other powers enjoyment is not received as befitting them in themselves — rather, the will *enjoys for itself through* them.

**Reply to 2.** To the objection that the wayfarer does not enjoy, since he sees what he does not yet have: it must be said that *seeing* and *having* are both required for *enjoyment*, as is *loving*. For one who enjoys both sees and has [his object]; but seeing and having are required dispositively, whereas love is required essentially. And so there is nothing unfitting in saying that enjoyment is an act of the will at its root, even though it requires dispositions in the other powers.

**Reply to 3.** To the objection that we enjoy by faith and hope: it must be said that the texts adduced are not properly about faith and hope, but about them *by concomitance*. For the will, through those habits, tends toward its object and rests in it; but the tending and resting themselves belong to the will, even though they are disposed by the acts of the cognitive [power].

**Reply to 4.** To the objection that the act of enjoyment is an act of reason: it must be said that we tend toward God by faith and hope as regards the *disposition* [to the act], but as regards the *consummation* of the act, this belongs to the will. And the same must be said of the irascible [appetite]: its act disposes toward enjoyment but does not consummate it.

### Scholion

**I.** In the main resolution of this question — namely *that enjoyment is an act of the will* — nearly all the scholastics agree, especially among the older doctors. Alexander of Hales, Richard of Mediavilla, Albert the Great, and Thomas Aquinas all concur — although some of them, and especially Thomas, in taking the act formally as an act of the intellective part, depart somewhat from the holy Doctor. Still, neither of them really departs from the fundamental Augustinian doctrine.

**II.** Alexander of Hales, *Summa*, pt. II, q. 62, m. 1. — Thomas Aquinas, here q. 1, a. 1; *Summa theologiae* I–II, q. 11. — Albert the Great, here aa. 1–2. — Richard of Mediavilla, here a. 2, q. 1. — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 1, a. 1. — Henry of Ghent, *Summa* a. 8, q. 1. — Scotus, here q. 2, and *Quodlibet*, q. 16. — Durandus, here q. 1. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 3. — Biel, here q. 2.

- **Key terms** (per project glossary): *frui* → enjoy / enjoyment; *fruibile* → enjoyable; *quietatio / quiescere* → rest; *motus cum delectatione* → motion with delight; *dispositive* → dispositively (i.e. as a preparing condition, not as the thing itself); *per concomitantiam* → by concomitance; *essentialiter* → essentially (i.e. in its very essence, as distinct from *dispositive*).

- **Three acceptations of *frui*.** The heart of the *respondeo* is Bonaventure's harmonization of Augustine's three definitions of *frui* — *communiter* (motion with delight; *Qq. 83*, q. 30), *proprie* (motion with rest; *De doctr. christ.*), and *propriissime* (embracing both; *De Trin.* X). The English follows the project glossary in rendering these as *commonly / properly / most properly*, matching q. 1's parallel schema for *uti*.

- **Essentially vs. dispositively.** Bonaventure's central move is to distinguish what enjoyment is *in its root* (an act of the will) from what enjoyment *requires as a disposition* (acts of cognition and of the other powers). The translation tries to keep this distinction visible in every *ad*-reply by preserving the adverbial contrast between *essentialiter* and *dispositive*.
