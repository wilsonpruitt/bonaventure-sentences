# I Sent., d. 1, a. 2, q. unica

## *Utrum frui sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 35 -->

### Articulus II. *Quid sit* frui.

Habito de *uti* et *utibili*, consequenter quaeritur de *frui* et *fruibili*, et primo, quid sit *frui* per essentiam, utrum scilicet sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium.

### Quaestio Unica. *Utrum frui sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium.*

1. Quod sit actus voluntatis, videtur per primam definitionem, quae est: *Frui est amore inhaerere* etc.[^1]; sed amor est voluntatis: ergo et *frui* similiter. Si dicas, quod amor sumitur ibi communiter, secundum quod est in qualibet vi respectu sui actus; contra: in omnibus aliis definitionibus idem dicitur. Unde dicitur in sequenti[^2]: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus voluntas delectata conquiescit». Quietatio autem voluntatis est.

<!-- page 36 -->

Et in alia[^9] similiter: «Frui est uti cum gaudio». Gaudium autem ad voluntatem proprie pertinet.

2. Item, ostenditur ratione, quia fruitione delectamur[^10], secundum quod delectabile est differentia boni, cum sit bonum honestum, conferens et delectabile; bonum autem obiectum est voluntatis: ergo *frui* est solius voluntatis.

3. Item, eo fruimur, quo quietamur; sed quietatio respicit rationem finis, «et finis rationem boni[^11]», et «bonum est obiectum voluntatis»: ergo fruitio, quae ordinat ad illud, similiter.

**Sed contra:**

1. Omnis virtus appetit uniri suo obiecto, quo habito delectatur, si cognoscit, et quiescit: ergo motus cum quietatione et delectatione est omnium virium: ergo cum talis sit fruitio, fruitio erit in omnibus, non tantum in voluntate;

2. Item, absentia rei amatae potius contristat amantem, quam delectat[^12]; quod enim delectat ipsum, hoc est, quia videt ipsam et habet. Si ergo motus fruitionis est cum delectatione vel est ipsa delectatio: ergo hoc[^13] est, quia videt illud in quo est ipsa delectatio; sed visio respicit cognitivam: ergo etc.

3. Item, Augustinus in libro *de Doctrina christiana*[^14]: «Haec est summa merces, ut ipso perfruamur». Sed super illud Psalmi: *Ostendam illi salutare* etc., dicit Glossa[^15], quod «visio est tota merces»: ergo si fruitio est merces, fruitio est visio per essentiam; sed visio est in cognitiva sive in ratione: ergo etc.

4. Item, fide et spe tendimus in Deum: aut ergo fruendo, aut utendo; sed non utendo, cum Deo non sit utendum: ergo fruendo: ergo fide et spe fruimur; sed fides est habitus rationis cognitivae; sed cuius est habitus, eius est actus: ergo actus fruitionis est actus[^16] rationis; similiter videtur de irascibili: ergo etc.

### Conclusio.

> *Frui* sumtum essentialiter est actus voluntatis, sumtum dispositive est actus etiam aliarum potentiarum.

**Respondeo:** Secundum aliquos[^17] *frui* pertinet ad omnes vires; et hoc dicunt, quia omnes vires remunerabuntur; qui dicunt etiam, quod[^18] in gloria erit magis proprie rationis, quia immediatius se habet ad delectationem.

Sed aliter videtur dicendum secundum Augustinum, scilicet quod sit actus ipsius voluntatis. Cum enim tres dentur definitiones de *frui*, omnes dantur penes actum voluntatis, quem tripliciter est considerare. Primo modo communiter, prout dicit motum cum delectatione; et sic definit Augustinus[^19]: «Frui est uti cum gaudio». Secundo modo, prout dicit motum cum quietatione; et hoc modo definitur ab Augustino *de Doctrina christiana*: «Frui est amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se ipsam»; et hoc modo accipitur *proprie*. Tertio modo accipitur, prout complectitur utrumque, scilicet quietationem et delectationem; et hoc modo definitur ab Augustino decimo *de Trinitate*: «Frui est quiescere in cognitis, voluntate propter se delectata», et sic accipitur *propriissime*.

Quia ergo *frui* secundum omnem acceptionem dicit delectationem vel quietem vel utrumque, et omne tale habet rationem boni, et hoc est obiectum voluntatis: ideo loquendo essentialiter, *frui* est actus voluntatis. Sed quia voluntas nec delectatur nec quietatur nisi in eo, quod cognoscit vel per fidem vel per speciem[^20], et in eo, quod habet per spem vel in re, ideo actus aliarum virium ad hunc disponunt, non tamen sunt ipsum *frui*, essentialiter loquendo.

Ex hoc patet solutio[^21] ad illud quod communiter solet quaeri, quare *frui* non definitur per actum cognitionis, sicut delectationis. Tamen[^22] haec quae-

<!-- page 37 -->

stio fundata est super falsum. Augustinus enim ponit in notificatione una, quod est cognitionis, cum dicit: «Frui est quiescere in cognitis»; cadit tamen ibi sicut dispositio.

Ex hoc etiam patet, quare non definitur per fidem et[^23] spem, sicut per caritatem, quia caritas inducitur in concupiscibilem, cuius est *frui*. Tamen ista quaestio similiter fundata est super falsum, quia amor, quo definitur *frui*, est communis ad amorem castum et libidinosum[^24], quo avarus fruitur auro, non proprius ipsius caritatis.

**Ad opposita:**

1. Ad illud ergo quod obiicitur, quod omnis virtus habet delectari et quietari[^25], cum unitur suo obiecto; dicendum, quod ipsa voluntas, ut vult Augustinus[^26], amat sibi et aliis. Et sicut etiam dicit Anselmus[^27]: «Voluntas inclinat alias vires et aliis meretur», ideo eius quietatio et delectatio redundat in alias vires. Unde sicut voluntas non sibi cognoscit, sed ratio cognoscit sibi et voluntati, ita voluntas sibi et rationi delectatur et ipsam quiescere facit.

2. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod amans non fruitur, nisi videat vel habeat; dicendum, quod videre et habere requiruntur ad *frui*, similiter et amare. Nam si quis videt aliquid et habet, nunquam delectatur, nisi amet; aliter tamen requiritur visio quam amor. Nam visio disponit, similiter et tentio, sed amor delicias suggerit. Unde est quasi acumen penetrans[^28], et ideo ei maxime convenit unire et per consequens delectare et quietare: ideo essentialiter, non dispositive, est fruitio. Propter quod est intelligendum, quod actus voluntatis potest dupliciter considerari, scilicet per modum appetitus et[^29] complacentiae. Primo modo antecedere potest ipsam visionem; secundo vero consequitur, et in hoc est perfecta ratio ipsius fruitionis, scilicet in complacentia rei visae et habitae.

3. Ad illud: *Visio est tota merces*; dicendum, quod illud non dicitur proprie, sed per concomitantiam[^30], quia visio et complacentia, in qua est perfecta ratio fruitionis, inseparabiliter se habent.

4. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod fide et spe fruimur; dicendum, quod illud non est verum per se, quia quaelibet harum accipit rationem tendentiae[^31] a caritate; unde fruitio non est ex istis nisi per caritatem. Sola enim caritas considerat finem in ratione finis et obiecti. Unde non dicitur aliquis sperare Deum, sicut amare.

### Scholion

**I.** In solutione principali huius quaestionis, scilicet quod fruitio sit actus voluntatis, omnes antiqui doctores cum Seraphico conveniunt; attamen iam istis temporibus circa quasdam subtiles de hac re quaestiones erant controversiae.

Primo quoad ipsum conceptum fruitionis S. Bonaventura docet, fruitionem praesupponere quidem cognitionem sive visionem tanquam dispositionem, tamen, essentialiter loquendo, ipsam esse actum voluntatis, qui importet tum dilectionem tum delectationem, sive esse formaliter actionem voluntatis, nempe amorosam eius inhaesionem, ad quam sequitur inseparabiliter passio delectationis; cfr. hic ad 2. et dub. 1. 2; III. Sent. d. 29. a. 1. q. 2, et ibid. d. 35. a. 1. q. 1. in corp.; II. Sent. d. 38. a. 1. q. 2. ad 4. Hexaem. Serm. 12. in fine. Idem docet Scotus cum suis, Richard. a Med., B. Albert. M., Petr. a Tar., Henr. Gand. — Alii vero doctores volunt, fruitionem esse formaliter ipsam delectationem, non quidem qualemcumque, sed ultimatam, seu quae coniuncta est cum quietatione; ipsam vero non esse amorem nisi connotative et causaliter; ita discipuli S. Thomae, teste Caietano (ad S. I. II. q. 1. a. 1.), Dionys. Carth. aliique.

Secundo, quoad actum, in quo fruitio formaliter consistit, docet S. Bonav., hic ad 1. et 2; II. Sent. d. 24. p. I. a. 2. q. 4: omnem delectationem spiritualem ortum habere ex amore voluntatis, praesupposita perceptione convenientis. Consentiunt Alex. Hal., Henr. Gand., Richard. a Med. qui inter alia dicit: «Actum cognitivae non sequitur delectatio nisi mediante actu appetitivae.» Hoc negatur ab auctoribus secundae sententiae.

Tertio, hinc orta est celebris controversia, praecipue inter asseclas S. Thomae et Scoti agitata, utrum scilicet beatitudo in patria formaliter consistat in actu intellectus sive in visione, qui tum amorem tum delectationem inseparabiliter habet annexa, immo «virtualiter continet et causaliter exerit» (Caietanus), an potius, ut vult Scotus cum aliis multis, in actu voluntatis, qui visionem supponit et delectatione completur. Hanc quaestionem tangit S. Bonav., hic ad 3.; plura vide IV. Sent. d. 49. p. 1. q. 5. Haec controversia cohaeret cum doctrinis psychologicis praecipue de relatione inter intellectum et voluntatem.

**II.** De quaestione hic proposita: Alex. Hal., S. p. IV. q. 26. m. 4. — Scot., hic q. 3. in Oxon. et Report. — S. Thom., hic q. 1. a. 1; S. I. II. q. 11. a. 1. et 2. — B. Albert. M., hic a. 12. et 17. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 2. a. 1. — Richard. a Med., hic a. 2. q. 1. — Aegid. R., hic I. princ. q. 1. — Henr. Gand., S. a. 49. q. 6. n. 26.; de delectatione etiam agit S. a. 50. q. 2. — Durand., hic q. 1. — Dionys. Carth., hic q. 3. — Biel, hic q. 2.

## English

### Article II. *What enjoyment is.*

Having treated of *use* and *the useable*, the inquiry next concerns *enjoyment* and *the enjoyable*, and first, what *enjoyment* is essentially — namely, whether it is an act of the will, or of the other powers.

### Sole Question. *Whether enjoyment is an act of the will, or of the other powers.*

1. That it is an act of the will is shown by the first definition, which is: *To enjoy is to cleave by love,* etc.[^1]; but love is of the will: therefore *enjoyment* likewise. If you say that love is taken there commonly, according as it is in any power whatever with respect to its own act; against this: in all the other definitions the same is said. Hence it is said in what follows[^2]: «We enjoy things known, in which the will, delighted, comes to rest.» But rest is of the will.

<!-- page 36 -->

And in another[^9] likewise: «To enjoy is to use with joy.» But joy belongs properly to the will.

2. Likewise, it is shown by reason: because by fruition we are delighted[^10], according as the delightful is a difference of the good, since the good is the honorable, the useful, and the delightful; but the good is the object of the will: therefore *to enjoy* is of the will alone.

3. Likewise, we enjoy that by which we come to rest; but rest regards the formal character of an end, «and an end the formal character of the good[^11]», and «the good is the object of the will»: therefore fruition, which orders to that, likewise.

**On the contrary:**

1. Every power seeks to be united to its object, and, this being had, is delighted (if it knows) and comes to rest: therefore motion together with rest and delight belongs to all the powers: therefore, since fruition is such, fruition will be in all, not only in the will;

2. Likewise, the absence of the loved thing rather grieves the lover than delights him[^12]; for what delights him is the fact that he sees it and has it. If therefore the motion of fruition is with delight, or is delight itself: therefore this[^13] is so because he sees that in which is the delight itself; but vision regards the cognitive [power]: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, Augustine in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^14]: «This is the highest reward, that we may enjoy himself.» But upon that of the Psalm: *I will show him my salvation,* etc., the Gloss says[^15] that «vision is the whole reward»: therefore if fruition is the reward, fruition is vision essentially; but vision is in the cognitive [power] or in reason: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, by faith and hope we tend toward God: therefore either by enjoying or by using; but not by using, since God is not to be used: therefore by enjoying: therefore by faith and hope we enjoy; but faith is a habit of cognitive reason; but whose is the habit, his is the act: therefore the act of fruition is an act[^16] of reason; the same seems to be the case for the irascible [power]: therefore, etc.

### Conclusion.

> *To enjoy*, taken essentially, is an act of the will; taken dispositively, it is an act also of the other powers.

**I respond:** According to some[^17] *enjoyment* pertains to all the powers; and this they say, because all the powers will be rewarded; who say also that[^18] in glory it will be more properly of reason, because reason has a more immediate relation to delight.

But it seems must be said otherwise according to Augustine, namely that it is an act of the will itself. For since three definitions of *to enjoy* are given, all are given in terms of an act of the will, which can be considered in three ways. *First*, commonly, inasmuch as it signifies motion with delight; and thus Augustine defines it[^19]: «To enjoy is to use with joy.» *Secondly*, inasmuch as it signifies motion with rest; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: «To enjoy is to cleave by love to some thing for its own sake»; and in this way it is taken *properly*. *Thirdly*, it is taken inasmuch as it embraces both, namely rest and delight; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in book ten *On the Trinity*: «To enjoy is to come to rest in things known, the will being delighted for their own sake»; and so it is taken *most properly*.

Since, therefore, *to enjoy* according to every acceptation signifies delight or rest or both, and every such thing has the formal character of the good, and this is the object of the will: therefore, speaking essentially, *to enjoy* is an act of the will. But since the will is not delighted and does not come to rest except in that which it knows either by faith or by sight[^20], and in that which it has by hope or in reality, therefore the acts of the other powers dispose toward this, but are not *enjoyment* itself, speaking essentially.

From this is clear the solution[^21] to that which is commonly wont to be asked: why *enjoyment* is not defined by an act of cognition, as it is by [an act of] delight. Yet this[^22] ques-

<!-- page 37 -->

tion is founded upon a falsehood. For Augustine in one notification puts what is of cognition, when he says: «To enjoy is to come to rest in things known»; yet it falls in there as a disposition.

From this also is clear why it is not defined by faith and[^23] hope, as it is by charity, because charity is brought into the concupiscible [power], to which *enjoyment* belongs. Yet this question likewise is founded upon a falsehood, since the love by which *enjoyment* is defined is common to chaste love and to lustful love[^24], by which the miser enjoys gold — not proper to charity itself.

**To the opposites:**

1. To that, then, which is objected, that every power has [the capacity] to be delighted and to come to rest[^25] when it is united to its object; it must be said that the will itself, as Augustine[^26] holds, loves for itself and for the others. And as Anselm[^27] also says: «The will inclines the other powers and merits for the others»; therefore its rest and delight redound to the other powers. Hence, just as the will does not know for itself, but reason knows for itself and for the will, so the will is delighted for itself and for reason, and makes reason come to rest.

2. To that which is objected, that the lover does not enjoy unless he sees or has [the beloved]; it must be said that to see and to have are required for *enjoyment*, likewise also to love. For if anyone sees something and has it, he is never delighted unless he loves; but vision is required otherwise than love. For vision disposes, likewise too holding, but love offers the delights. Hence it is, as it were, a piercing sharpness[^28], and therefore it most befits it to unite and consequently to delight and to bring to rest: therefore essentially, not dispositively, it is fruition. On account of which it must be understood that the act of the will can be considered in two ways, namely by way of appetite and[^29] of complacency. In the first way it can precede the vision itself; in the second it follows, and in this is the perfect formal character of fruition itself, namely in the complacency of the thing seen and had.

3. To that: *Vision is the whole reward*; it must be said that this is not said properly, but by concomitance[^30], because vision and complacency, in which is the perfect formal character of fruition, are inseparably connected.

4. To that which is objected, that by faith and hope we enjoy; it must be said that this is not true *per se*, because each of these receives its character of tending[^31] from charity; whence fruition does not arise from these except through charity. For charity alone considers the end under the formal character of end and of object. Hence no one is said to *hope for* God, as [he is said to] *love* [Him].

### Scholion

**I.** On the principal solution of this question, namely that fruition is an act of the will, all the ancient doctors agree with the Seraphic; nevertheless already in these times concerning certain subtle questions about this matter there were controversies.

*First*, as to the very concept of fruition, St. Bonaventure teaches that fruition does presuppose cognition or vision as a disposition; yet, speaking essentially, it is itself an act of the will, which imports both love and delight, that is, it is formally an act of the will — namely its loving adhesion, upon which inseparably follows the passion of delight; cf. here ad 2 and dub. 1, 2; III Sent. d. 29 a. 1 q. 2, and ibid. d. 35 a. 1 q. 1 in corp.; II Sent. d. 38 a. 1 q. 2 ad 4; Hexaem. Sermon 12 at the end. The same is taught by Scotus with his followers, Richard of Mediavilla, Blessed Albert the Great, Peter of Tarantasia, Henry of Ghent. — But other doctors hold that fruition is formally delight itself — not, indeed, any sort, but the ultimate, that is, that which is conjoined with rest; and that fruition is not love except connotatively and causally; so the disciples of St. Thomas, on the testimony of Cajetan (on S. I-II q. 1 a. 1), Denis the Carthusian, and others.

*Secondly*, as to the act in which fruition formally consists, St. Bonav. teaches here, ad 1 and 2; II Sent. d. 24 p. I a. 2 q. 4: that every spiritual delight has its origin from the love of the will, supposing the perception of what is suitable. Alexander of Hales, Henry of Ghent, [and] Richard of Mediavilla agree, who among other things says: «An act of the cognitive [power] is not followed by delight except mediated by an act of the appetitive [power].» This is denied by the authors of the second opinion.

*Thirdly*, hence arose the famous controversy, agitated especially between the followers of St. Thomas and of Scotus, namely whether beatitude in the fatherland formally consists in the act of the intellect or in vision, which has both love and delight inseparably annexed — nay, «virtually contains and causally exerts [them]» (Cajetan); or rather, as Scotus with many others holds, in an act of the will, which presupposes vision and is completed by delight. This question is touched by St. Bonav. here, ad 3; see more in IV Sent. d. 49 p. 1 q. 5. This controversy coheres with psychological doctrines, especially concerning the relation between intellect and will.

**II.** On the question here proposed: Alex. Hal., S. p. IV q. 26 m. 4. — Scot., here q. 3 in Oxon. and Report. — St. Thom., here q. 1 a. 1; S. I-II q. 11 a. 1 and 2. — B. Albert M., here a. 12 and 17. — Peter of Tarantasia, here q. 2 a. 1. — Richard of Mediavilla, here a. 2 q. 1. — Giles of Rome, here I princ. q. 1. — Henry of Ghent, S. a. 49 q. 6 n. 26.; on delight he also treats in S. a. 50 q. 2. — Durandus, here q. 1. — Denis the Carthusian, here q. 3. — Biel, here q. 2.
