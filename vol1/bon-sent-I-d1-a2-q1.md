# I Sent., d. 1, a. 2, q. unica

## *Utrum frui sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 35 -->

### Articulus II. *Quid sit* frui.

Habito de *uti* et *utibili*, consequenter quaeritur de *frui* et *fruibili*, et primo, quid sit *frui* per essentiam, utrum scilicet sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium.

### Quaestio Unica. *Utrum frui sit actus voluntatis, an aliarum virium.*

1. Quod sit actus voluntatis, videtur per primam definitionem, quae est: *Frui est amore inhaerere* etc.[^1]; sed amor est voluntatis: ergo et *frui* similiter. Si dicas, quod amor sumitur ibi communiter, secundum quod est in qualibet vi respectu sui actus; contra: in omnibus aliis definitionibus idem dicitur. Unde dicitur in sequenti[^2]: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus voluntas delectata conquiescit». Quietatio autem voluntatis est.

<!-- page 36 -->

Et in alia[^3] similiter: «Frui est uti cum gaudio». Gaudium autem ad voluntatem proprie pertinet.

2. Item, ostenditur ratione, quia fruitione delectamur[^4], secundum quod delectabile est differentia boni, cum sit bonum honestum, conferens et delectabile; bonum autem obiectum est voluntatis: ergo *frui* est solius voluntatis.

3. Item, eo fruimur, quo quietamur; sed quietatio respicit rationem finis, «et finis rationem boni»[^5], et «bonum est obiectum voluntatis»: ergo fruitio, quae ordinat ad illud, similiter.

**Sed contra:**

1. Omnis virtus appetit uniri suo obiecto, quo habito delectatur, si cognoscit, et quiescit: ergo motus cum quietatione et delectatione est omnium virium: ergo cum talis sit fruitio, fruitio erit in omnibus, non tantum in voluntate.

2. Item, absentia rei amatae potius contristat amantem, quam delectat[^6]; quod enim delectat ipsum, hoc est, quia videt ipsam et habet. Si ergo motus fruitionis est cum delectatione vel est ipsa delectatio: ergo hoc est, quia videt illud in quo est ipsa delectatio; sed visio respicit cognitivam: ergo etc.

3. Item, Augustinus in libro de *Doctrina christiana*[^7]: «Haec est summa merces, ut ipso perfruamur». Sed super illud Psalmi: *Ostendam illi salutare* etc., dicit Glossa[^8], quod «visio est tota merces»: ergo si fruitio est merces, fruitio est visio per essentiam; sed visio est in cognitiva sive in ratione: ergo etc.

4. Item, fide et spe tendimus in Deum: aut ergo fruendo, aut utendo; sed non utendo, cum Deo non sit utendum: ergo fruendo: ergo fide et spe fruimur; sed fides est habitus rationis cognitivae; sed cuius est habitus, eius est actus: ergo actus[^9] fruitionis est actus rationis; similiter videtur de irascibili: ergo etc.

### Conclusio.

> *Frui sumtum essentialiter est actus voluntatis; sumtum dispositive est actus etiam aliarum potentiarum.*

**Respondeo:** Secundum aliquos[^10] *frui* pertinet ad omnes vires; et hoc dicunt, quia omnes vires remunerabuntur; qui dicunt etiam, quod[^11] in gloria erit magis proprie rationis, quia immediatius se habet ad delectationem.

Sed aliter videtur dicendum secundum Augustinum, scilicet quod sit actus ipsius voluntatis. Cum enim tres dentur definitiones de *frui*, omnes dantur penes actum voluntatis, quem tripliciter est considerare. Primo modo *communiter*, prout dicit motum cum delectatione; et sic definit Augustinus[^12]: «Frui est uti cum gaudio». Secundo modo, prout dicit motum cum quietatione; et hoc modo definitur ab Augustino *de Doctrina christiana*: «Frui est amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se ipsam»; et hoc modo accipitur *proprie*. Tertio modo accipitur, prout complectitur utrumque, scilicet quietationem et delectationem; et hoc modo definitur ab Augustino decimo *de Trinitate*: «Frui est quiescere in cognitis, voluntate propter se delectata», et sic accipitur *propriissime*.

Quia ergo *frui* secundum omnem acceptionem dicit delectationem vel quietem vel utrumque, et omne tale habet rationem boni, et hoc est obiectum voluntatis: ideo loquendo essentialiter, *frui* est actus voluntatis. Sed quia voluntas nec delectatur nec quietatur nisi in eo, quod cognoscit vel per fidem vel per speciem[^13], et in eo, quod habet per spem vel in re, ideo actus aliarum virium ad hunc disponunt, non tamen sunt ipsum *frui*, essentialiter loquendo.

Ex hoc patet solutio[^14] ad illud quod communiter solet quaeri, quare *frui* non definitur per actum cognitionis, sicut delectationis. Tamen haec quaestio fundata est super falsum.

<!-- page 37 -->

Augustinus enim ponit in notificatione una, quod est cognitionis, cum dicit: «Frui est quiescere in cognitis»; cadit tamen ibi sicut dispositio.

Ex hoc etiam patet, quare non definitur per fidem et[^15] spem, sicut per caritatem, quia caritas importat concupiscibilem, cuius est *frui*. Tamen ista quaestio similiter fundata est super falsum, quia amor, quo definitur *frui*, est communis ad amorem castum et libidinosum[^16], quo avarus fruitur auro, non proprius ipsius caritatis.

**Ad 1.** Ad illud ergo quod obiicitur, quod omnis virtus habet delectari et quietari[^17], cum unitur suo obiecto: dicendum, quod ipsa voluntas, ut vult Augustinus[^18], amat sibi et aliis. Et sicut etiam dicit Anselmus[^19]: «Voluntas inclinat alias vires et aliis meretur», ideo eius quietatio et delectatio redundat in alias vires. Unde sicut voluntas non sibi cognoscit, sed ratio cognoscit sibi et voluntati, ita voluntas sibi et rationi delectatur et ipsam quiescere facit.

**Ad 2.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod amans non fruitur, nisi videat vel habeat: dicendum, quod *videre* et *habere* requiruntur ad *frui*, similiter et *amare*. Nam si quis videt aliquid et habet, nunquam delectatur, nisi amet; aliter tamen requiritur visio quam amor. Nam visio disponit, similiter et tentio, sed amor delicias suggerit. Unde est quasi acumen penetrans[^20], et ideo ei maxime convenit unire et per consequens delectare et quietare: ideo essentialiter, non dispositive, est fruitio. Propter quod est intelligendum, quod actus voluntatis potest dupliciter considerari, scilicet per modum appetitus et[^21] complacentiae. Primo modo antecedere potest ipsam visionem; secundo vero consequitur, et in hoc est perfecta ratio ipsius fruitionis, scilicet in complacentia rei visae et habitae.

**Ad 3.** Ad illud: *Visio est tota merces*: dicendum, quod illud non dicitur proprie, sed per concomitantiam[^22], quia visio et complacentia, in qua est perfecta ratio fruitionis, inseparabiliter se habent.

**Ad 4.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod fide et spe fruimur: dicendum, quod illud non est verum per se, quia quaelibet harum accipit rationem tendentiae[^23] a caritate; unde fruitio non est ex istis nisi per caritatem. Sola enim caritas considerat finem in ratione finis et obiecti. Unde non dicitur aliquis sperare Deum, sicut amare.

### Scholion

**I.** In solutione principali huius quaestionis, scil. *quod fruitio sit actus voluntatis*, omnes antiqui doctores cum Seraphico conveniunt; attamen iam istis temporibus circa quasdam subtiles de hac re quaestiones erant controversiae.

*Primo* quoad ipsum conceptum fruitionis S. Bonaventura docet, fruitionem praesupponere quidem cognitionem sive visionem tanquam dispositionem, tamen, essentialiter loquendo, ipsam esse actum voluntatis, qui importet tum dilectionem tum delectationem, sive esse formaliter actionem voluntatis, nempe amorosam eius inhaesionem, ad quam sequitur inseparabiliter passio delectationis; cfr. hic ad 2. et dub. 12; III. *Sent.* d. 29. a. 1. q. 2, et ibid. d. 35. a. 1. q. 1. in corp.; II. *Sent.* d. 38. a. 1. q. 2. ad 4. *Hexaem.* Serm. 12. in fine. Idem docet Scotus cum suis, Richard. a Med., B. Albert. M., Petr. a Tar., Henr. Gand. — Alii vero doctores volunt, fruitionem esse formaliter ipsam delectationem, non quidem qualemcumque, sed ultimatam, seu quae coniuncta est cum quietatione; ipsam vero non esse amorem nisi connotative et causaliter; ita discipuli S. Thomae, teste Caietano (ad S. I. II. q. 1. a. 1.), Dionys. Carth. aliique.

*Secundo*, quoad actum, in quo fruitio formaliter consistit, docet S. Bonav., hic ad 1. et 2; II. *Sent.* d. 24. p. I. a. 2. q. 4: omnem delectationem spiritualem ortum habere ex amore voluntatis, praesupposita perceptione convenientis. Consentiunt Alex. Hal., Henr. Gand., Richard. a Med. qui inter alia dicit: «Actum cognitivae non sequitur delectatio nisi mediante actu appetitivae». Hoc negatur ab auctoribus secundae sententiae.

*Tertio*, hinc orta est celebris controversia, praecipue inter asseclas S. Thomae et Scoti agitata, utrum scil. beatitudo in patria formaliter consistat in actu intellectus sive in visione, qui tum amorem tum delectationem inseparabiliter habet annexa, immo «virtualiter continet et causaliter exerit» (Caietanus), an potius, ut vult Scotus cum aliis multis, in actu voluntatis, qui visionem supponit et delectatione completur. Hanc quaestionem tangit S. Bonav., hic ad 3.; plura vide IV. *Sent.* d. 49. p. 1. q. 5. Haec controversia cohaeret cum doctrinis psychologicis praecipue de relatione inter intellectum et voluntatem.

**II.** De quaestione hic proposita: Alex. Hal., *S.* p. IV. q. 26. m. 4. — Scot., hic q. 3. in *Oxon.* et *Report.* — S. Thom., hic q. 1. a. 1; *S.* I. II. q. 11. a. 1. et 2. — B. Albert. M., hic a. 12. et 17. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 2. a. 1. — Richard. a Med., hic a. 2. q. 1. — Aegid. R., hic I. princ. q. 1. — Henr. Gand., *S.* a. 49. q. 6. n. 26.; de delectatione etiam agit *S.* a. 50. q. 2. — Durand., hic q. 1. — Dionys. Carth., hic q. 3. — Biel, hic q. 2.

## English

<!-- page 35 -->

### Article II. *What* enjoyment *is.*

Having treated of *use* and the *usable*, we next ask about *enjoyment* and the *enjoyable*; and first, what *enjoyment* is in essence — namely, whether it is an act of the will, or of the other powers.

### Sole Question. *Whether enjoyment is an act of the will, or of the other powers.*

1. That it is an act of the will is shown from the first definition, which is: *To enjoy is to cling with love* etc.[^1]; but love is of the will: therefore *enjoyment* likewise [is of the will]. If you say that *love* is here taken commonly, insofar as it is found in any power with respect to its own act — on the contrary: in all the other definitions the same is said. Whence in the next [definition] it is said[^2]: "We enjoy the things known, in which the will, having been delighted, comes to rest." But rest is of the will.

<!-- page 36 -->

And in another[^3] likewise: "To enjoy is to use with joy." But joy properly pertains to the will.

2. Likewise, it is shown from reason: because we are delighted in enjoyment[^4], inasmuch as *delightful* is a differentia of the good, since the good is upright, useful, and delightful; but the good is the object of the will: therefore *enjoyment* is of the will alone.

3. Likewise, we enjoy that in which we come to rest; but rest regards the character of an end, "and the end the character of the good"[^5], and "the good is the object of the will": therefore enjoyment, which orders [us] toward that, likewise [is of the will].

**On the contrary:**

1. Every power desires to be united to its object, and once it has it, it is delighted (if it knows it) and rests: therefore motion with rest and delight belongs to all the powers: therefore, since enjoyment is such [a motion], enjoyment will be in all [powers], not only in the will.

2. Likewise, the absence of the beloved thing rather saddens the lover than delights him[^6]; for what delights him is that he sees and possesses it. If, then, the motion of enjoyment is with delight, or is the delight itself: therefore this is so because he sees that in which the delight itself is; but seeing regards the cognitive [power]: therefore etc.

3. Likewise, Augustine in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^7]: "This is the highest reward, that we may enjoy Him." But upon that [verse] of the Psalm: *I will show him my salvation* etc., the Gloss says[^8] that "the vision is the whole reward": therefore if enjoyment is the reward, enjoyment is vision in essence; but vision is in the cognitive [power] or in reason: therefore etc.

4. Likewise, by faith and hope we tend toward God: either then by enjoying or by using; but not by using, since God is not to be used: therefore by enjoying: therefore by faith and hope we enjoy; but faith is a habit of the cognitive reason; and whose is the habit, his is the act: therefore the act[^9] of enjoyment is an act of reason; the same seems [to hold] of the irascible [appetite]: therefore etc.

### Conclusion.

> *Enjoyment taken essentially is an act of the will; taken dispositively, it is also an act of the other powers.*

**I respond:** According to some[^10] *enjoyment* pertains to all the powers; and they say this because all the powers will be rewarded; and they also say that[^11] in glory it will more properly be of reason, because reason bears more immediately on delight.

But it seems rather to be said, with Augustine, that it is an act of the will itself. For since three definitions of *enjoyment* are given, all are given with respect to an act of the will, which can be considered in three ways. In the first way, *commonly*, insofar as it expresses motion with delight; and so Augustine defines it[^12]: "To enjoy is to use with joy." In the second way, insofar as it expresses motion with rest; and in this way it is defined by Augustine *On Christian Doctrine*: "To enjoy is to cling with love to some thing for its own sake"; and in this way it is taken *properly*. In the third way it is taken insofar as it embraces both, namely rest and delight; and in this way it is defined by Augustine in the tenth [book] *On the Trinity*: "To enjoy is to rest in things known, the will being delighted for their own sake"; and so it is taken *most properly*.

Since therefore *enjoyment* in every acceptation expresses delight or rest or both, and every such thing has the character of the good, and this is the object of the will: therefore, speaking essentially, *enjoyment* is an act of the will. But because the will is neither delighted nor at rest except in what it knows, whether by faith or by species[^13], and in what it has, whether by hope or in reality, therefore the acts of the other powers dispose to this [act], yet are not enjoyment itself, essentially speaking.

From this is plain the solution[^14] to that which is commonly asked, why *enjoyment* is not defined by the act of cognition, as it is by the act of delight. Yet this question is founded upon a falsehood.

<!-- page 37 -->

For Augustine puts in one statement of the [matter] what is of cognition, when he says: "To enjoy is to rest in things known"; yet there it falls in [only] as a disposition.

From this also is plain why it is not defined by faith and[^15] hope, as it is by charity, because charity involves the concupiscible [power], to which *enjoyment* belongs. Yet that question likewise is founded upon a falsehood, because the love by which *enjoyment* is defined is common to chaste love and to libidinous love[^16], by which the miser enjoys his gold — it is not proper to charity itself.

**To 1.** To that which is objected, that every power has [it] to be delighted and to rest[^17] when it is united to its object: it must be said that the will itself, as Augustine holds[^18], loves both for itself and for the others. And, as Anselm also says[^19]: "The will inclines the other powers, and merits for the others"; therefore its rest and delight redound upon the other powers. Whence, just as the will does not know for itself, but reason knows for itself and for the will, so the will is delighted for itself and for reason, and makes [reason] rest.

**To 2.** To that which is objected, that the lover does not enjoy, unless he see or have: it must be said that *seeing* and *having* are required for *enjoying*, and likewise *loving*. For if someone sees something and has it, he is never delighted unless he loves; yet vision is required otherwise than love. For vision disposes, and likewise possession; but love supplies the delights. Whence [love] is, as it were, a penetrating sharpness[^20], and therefore to it most of all does it belong to unite, and consequently to delight and to bring to rest: therefore enjoyment is essentially [in love], not dispositively. On account of which it must be understood that the act of the will can be considered in two ways, namely by way of appetite and[^21] of complacency. In the first way it can precede the vision itself; but in the second way it follows [it], and in this lies the perfect character of enjoyment itself, namely in the complacency in the thing seen and had.

**To 3.** To that [objection]: *Vision is the whole reward*: it must be said that this is not said properly, but by concomitance[^22], because vision and complacency, in which is the perfect character of enjoyment, are inseparably connected.

**To 4.** To that which is objected, that we enjoy by faith and hope: it must be said that this is not true *per se*, because each of these takes its character of tendency[^23] from charity; whence enjoyment is not from these except through charity. For charity alone considers the end under the character of end and of object. Whence one is not said to *hope for* God in the same way as to *love* [Him].

### Scholion

**I.** In the principal solution of this question — namely *that enjoyment is an act of the will* — all the ancient doctors agree with the Seraphic [Doctor]; nevertheless already in those times there were controversies concerning certain subtle questions about this matter.

*First*, as to the very concept of enjoyment, St. Bonaventure teaches that enjoyment indeed presupposes cognition or vision as a disposition, yet, essentially speaking, it is itself an act of the will, which involves both love and delight; or [equivalently,] it is formally an action of the will, namely its loving adherence, upon which the passion of delight inseparably follows; cf. here ad 2 and dub. 12; III *Sent.* d. 29, a. 1, q. 2, and ibid. d. 35, a. 1, q. 1, in the body; II *Sent.* d. 38, a. 1, q. 2, ad 4; *Hexaemeron* Sermon 12, at the end. The same is taught by Scotus with his followers, Richard of Mediavilla, Bl. Albert the Great, Peter of Tarentaise, Henry of Ghent. — Other doctors, however, hold that enjoyment is formally the delight itself — not, indeed, just any delight, but the ultimate one, that is, the one joined with rest; and that it is not love except connotatively and causally; thus the disciples of St. Thomas, on the testimony of Cajetan (on *S.[umma]* I–II, q. 1, a. 1), Dionysius the Carthusian, and others.

*Second*, as to the act in which enjoyment formally consists, St. Bonaventure teaches, here ad 1 and 2 and II *Sent.* d. 24, p. I, a. 2, q. 4, that all spiritual delight has its origin from the love of the will, on the presupposition of the perception of what is fitting. Alexander of Hales, Henry of Ghent, and Richard of Mediavilla agree, who among other things says: "Delight does not follow upon the act of the cognitive [power] except by means of an act of the appetitive [power]." This is denied by the authors of the second opinion.

*Third*, hence arose the famous controversy, agitated chiefly between the followers of St. Thomas and of Scotus — namely, whether beatitude in the fatherland formally consists in an act of the intellect, that is, in vision (which has both love and delight inseparably annexed, indeed "virtually contains and causally puts forth" — Cajetan), or rather, as Scotus with many others holds, in an act of the will, which presupposes vision and is completed by delight. St. Bonaventure touches this question here ad 3; for more, see IV *Sent.* d. 49, p. 1, q. 5. This controversy is bound up with the psychological doctrines, especially concerning the relation between intellect and will.

**II.** On the question here proposed: Alexander of Hales, *Summa* p. IV, q. 26, m. 4. — Scotus, here q. 3, in the *Oxoniense* and *Reportata*. — St. Thomas, here q. 1, a. 1; *Summa* I–II, q. 11, a. 1 and 2. — Bl. Albert the Great, here a. 12 and 17. — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 2, a. 1. — Richard of Mediavilla, here a. 2, q. 1. — Giles of Rome, here I *princ.* q. 1. — Henry of Ghent, *Summa* a. 49, q. 6, n. 26; on delight he also treats *Summa* a. 50, q. 2. — Durandus, here q. 1. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 3. — Biel, here q. 2.

This phrase is taken from the words of the Apostle, 2 Cor. 5:7: *For we walk by faith, and not by species [= sight].* Cf. also III *Sent.* d. 31, a. 2, q. 1, ad 5. — Next, cod. R after *tamen* adds *ipsi*.

[^14]: **La.** Contra mss. et sex primas edd. habet Vat. *resolutio*, et paulo post contra maiorem partem mss. ut A C F G H K L R S T U V etc. et ed. 1 *consuevit* pro *solet*. Dein multi codd. cum ed. 1 *dilectionis* loco *delectationis*, sed non bene, sicuti ex corp. quaestionis patet.

    **En.** Against the manuscripts and the first six editions, the Vatican has *resolutio*, and a little later, against the majority of codices (A C F G H K L R S T U V etc.) and ed. 1, *consuevit* for *solet*. Then many codices with ed. 1 [read] *dilectionis* for *delectationis*, but not well, as is plain from the body of the question.

[^15]: **La.** Vat. cum cod. cc, mutata interpunctione et posito *cum* pro *Tamen*, legit *delectationis, cum haec quaestio fundata sit super falsum*; sed minus apte, quia responsio cum ipsa quaestione incongrue coniungitur contra morem Seraphici; insuper est contra omnes antiquiores mss. et ed. 1.

    **En.** The Vatican with cod. cc, with the punctuation altered and *cum* set in place of *Tamen*, reads *delectationis, cum haec quaestio fundata sit super falsum* ("of delight, since this question is founded upon a falsehood"); but less aptly, because the answer is incongruously joined with the question itself, against the Seraphic Doctor's manner; moreover it is against all the older manuscripts and ed. 1.

[^16]: **La.** Cod. W *vel*. — Auctoritate mss. et ed. 1 removimus particulam *vel*, quae hic a Vat. male additur, quia verbum *libidinosum* large acceptum etiam concupiscentiam oculorum complectitur. Codd. P et Q post *caritatis* addunt *est*; cod. X autem legit: *ideo non est proprius ipsius caritatis*.

    **En.** Cod. W [reads] *vel*. — On the authority of the manuscripts and ed. 1, we have removed the particle *vel*, which is wrongly added here by the Vatican, because the word *libidinosum*, taken broadly, also embraces the concupiscence of the eyes. Codices P and Q after *caritatis* add *est*; cod. X moreover reads: *therefore it is not proper to charity itself*.

[^17]: **La.** Licet multi mss. ut A C G K L O S T U V W X Y Z omittant *et quietari*, retinendum tamen duximus, tum quia in aliquibus antiquioribus mss. ut F H I etc. invenitur, tum quia superius in ipsa obiectione exhibetur.

    **En.** Although many manuscripts (A C G K L O S T U V W X Y Z) omit *et quietari* ("and to rest"), we have nonetheless judged it should be retained, both because it is found in some older manuscripts (F H I etc.) and because it is given above in the objection itself.

[^18]: **La.** Colligitur ex XV. *de Trin.* c. 22, ubi ostendit, quod memoria, intellectus et amor seu voluntas non sint sua nec sibi, sed supposito agant, quidquid agunt. Et in praecedentibus capitulis trinitatem in anima repertam cum Trinitate divina comparando, c. 17 de Spiritu S. ait: *ipse autem et sibi et illis non nisi diligit.* — Plurimi codd. *ut vult* pro *ut dicit*.

    **En.** It is gathered from *On the Trinity* XV, c. 22, where he shows that memory, intellect, and love (or will) are not their own, nor for themselves, but act for the supposit whatever they act. And in the preceding chapters, comparing the trinity found in the soul with the divine Trinity, in c. 17 he says of the Holy Spirit: *but [the Holy Spirit] himself loves not save both for himself and for them.* — Very many codices [have] *ut vult* for *ut dicit*.

[^19]: **La.** Libr. *de Concordia praescientiae Dei cum libero arb.* c. 11. et 12, ubi dicit, quod voluntas movet omnia instrumenta, quae in nobis sunt, ut manus etc.; et quod ex ipsa «descendit omne meritum hominis». — De doctrina hic posita vide II *Sent.* d. 24. p. 1. a. 2. q. 1.

    **En.** [Anselm,] book *On the Harmony of God's Foreknowledge with Free Choice*, c. 11 and 12, where he says that the will moves all the instruments which are in us, such as the hands etc.; and that from it "descends every merit of man." — On the doctrine here laid down, see II *Sent.* d. 24, p. 1, a. 2, q. 1.

[^20]: **La.** Hic loquendi modus sumtus est ex Hugonis de S. Victore *Expos. in Dionysii libr. de Caelest. Hierarch.* c. 7.

    **En.** This manner of speaking is taken from Hugh of St. Victor's *Exposition on Dionysius's Book On the Celestial Hierarchy*, c. 7.

[^21]: **La.** Cod. X hic repetit *per modum*.

    **En.** Cod. X here repeats *per modum* ("by way of").

[^22]: **La.** Restituimus ex mss. et ed. 1 magis usitatum verbum *concomitantiam* loco *concomitationem*.

    **En.** We have restored, on the authority of the manuscripts and ed. 1, the more usual word *concomitantiam* in place of *concomitationem*.

[^23]: **La.** Cod. X *tendentis*. In fine responsionis cod. R addit *et propter hoc non valet*.

    **En.** Cod. X [reads] *tendentis*. At the end of the response cod. R adds *et propter hoc non valet* ("and on account of this [the argument] is not valid").
