## Latin

## LIBER PRIMUS SENTENTIARUM.

### DE DEI UNITATE ET TRINITATE.

### DISTINCTIO I.

#### Cap. I.

*Omnis doctrina est de rebus vel de signis.*

Veteris ac novae Legis continentiam diligenti indagine etiam atque etiam considerantibus nobis praevia Dei gratia innotuit, sacrae paginae tractatum circa res vel signa praecipue versari. Ut enim egregius doctor Augustinus ait in libro de Doctrina christiana[^1]: «Omnis doctrina vel rerum est, vel signorum. Sed res etiam per signa discuntur. Proprie autem hic res appellantur, quae non ad significandum aliquid adhibentur; signa vero, quorum usus est in significando». Eorum autem aliqua sunt, quorum omnis usus est in significando, non in iustificando, id est[^2], quibus non utimur nisi aliquid significandi gratia, ut aliqua Sacramenta legalia; alia, quae non solum significant, sed conferunt quod intus adiuvet, sicut evangelica Sacramenta. «Ex quo aperte intelligitur, quae hic appellentur signa, res illae videlicet, quae ad significandum aliquid adhibentur. Omne igitur signum etiam res aliqua est. Quod enim nulla res est, ut in eodem Augustinus ait, omnino nihil est; non autem e converso[^3] omnis res signum est», quia non adhibetur ad significandum aliquid. Cumque his intenderit theologorum speculatio studiosa atque modesta, divinam Scripturam formam praescriptam in doctrina tenere advertet. De his ergo nobis aditum ad res divinas aliquatenus intelligendas, Deo duce, aperire volentibus disserendum est; et primum de rebus, postea de signis disseremus.

#### Cap. II.

*De rebus quibus fruendum est, vel utendum, et de his quae fruuntur et utuntur.*

«Id ergo in rebus considerandum est, ut in eodem[^4] Augustinus ait, quod res aliae sunt, quibus fruendum est, aliae, quibus utendum est, aliae, quae fruuntur et utuntur. Illae, quibus fruendum est, nos beatos faciunt; istis, quibus utendum est, tendentes ad beatitudinem adiuvamur et quasi adminiculamur, ut ad illas res, quae nos beatos faciunt, pervenire eisque inhaerere possimus. Res vero, quae fruuntur et utuntur, nos sumus, quasi inter utrasque constituti», et Angeli et Sancti[^5]. «Frui autem est amore alicui rei inhaerere propter se ipsam; uti vero id quod in usum venerit referre ad obtinendum illud, quo fruendum est; alias abuti est, non uti. Nam usus illicitus abusus vel abusio nominari debet[^6]». «Res igitur, quibus fruendum est, sunt Pater et Filius et Spiritus sanctus. Eadem tamen Trinitas quaedam summa res est communisque omnibus fruentibus ea, si tamen res dici debet et non rerum omnium causa, si tamen et causa. Non enim facile potest inveniri nomen, quod tantae excellentiae conveniat, nisi quod melius dicitur Trinitas haec unus Deus». Res autem, quibus utendum est, mundus est et in eo creata. Unde Augustinus in eodem[^7]: «Utendum est hoc mundo, non fruendum, ut invisibilia Dei per ea quae facta sunt, intellecta, conspiciantur[^8], id est, ut de temporalibus aeterna capiantur». Item in eodem: «In omnibus rebus illae tantum sunt, quibus fruendum est, quae aeternae et incommutabiles sunt; ceteris autem utendum est, ut ad illarum perfruitionem[^9] perveniatur». Unde Augustinus in libro decimo de Trinitate[^10]: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus ipsis propter se voluntas delectata conquiescit; utimur vero eis quae ad aliud referimus, quo fruendum est».

#### Cap. III.

*Quid sit frui et uti.*

Notandum vero, quod idem Augustinus in libro decimo de Trinitate[^11], aliter quam supra accipiens uti et frui, sic dicit: «Uti est assumere aliquid in

<!-- page 27 -->

facultatem voluntatis; frui autem est uti cum gaudio non adhuc spei, sed iam rei; ideoque omnis, qui fruitur, utitur; assumit enim aliquid in facultatem voluntatis cum fine delectationis. Non autem omnis, qui utitur, et fruitur; si id quod in facultatem voluntatis assumit, non propter ipsum, sed propter aliud appetit».

Et attende, quod[^12] videtur Augustinus dicere, illos frui tantum, qui in re gaudent, non iam in spe; et ita in hac vita non videmur frui, sed tantum uti, ubi gaudemus in spe, cum supra dictum sit, frui amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se, qualiter etiam hic multi adhaerent Deo.

Haec ergo, quae sibi contradicere videntur, sic determinamus dicentes, nos et hic et in futuro frui, sed ibi proprie et perfecte et plene, ubi per speciem videbimus quo fruemur; hic autem, dum in spe ambulamus, fruimur quidem, sed non adeo plene. Unde Augustinus in libro decimo de Trinitate[^13]: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus ipsis voluntas propter se delectata conquiescit». Idem in libro de Doctrina christiana[^14] ait: «Angeli illo fruentes iam beati sunt, quo et nos frui desideramus; et quantum in hac vita iam fruimur vel per speculum vel in aenigmate, tanto nostram peregrinationem et tolerabilius sustinemus et ardentius finire cupimus».

Potest etiam dici, quod qui fruitur etiam in hac vita non tantum habet gaudium spei, sed etiam rei, quia iam delectatur in eo quod diligit, et ita iam rem aliquatenus tenet. Constat ergo, quia[^15] debemus Deo frui, non uti. «Illo enim, ut ait Augustinus[^16], frueris, quo efficieris beatus et in quo spem ponis, ut ad id pervenias». De hoc idem ait in libro de Doctrina christiana: «Dicimus ea re nos frui, quam diligimus propter se, et ea re nobis fruendum esse tantum, qua efficimur beati; ceteris vero utendum». Frequenter tamen dicitur frui cum delectatione uti. Cum enim adest quod diligitur, etiam delectationem secum gerit. Si tamen per eam transieris et ad illud, ubi permanendum est, eam retuleris, uteris ea et abusive, non proprie diceris frui. Si vero inhaeseris atque permanseris, finem in ea ponens laetitiae tuae, tunc vere et proprie frui dicendus es; quod non est faciendum nisi in illa Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono[^17]».

##### *Utrum homine sit fruendum, an utendum.*

Cum autem homines, qui fruuntur et utuntur aliis rebus, res aliquae sint, quaeritur, «utrum frui se debeant, an uti, an utrumque». Ad quod sic respondet Augustinus in libro de Doctrina christiana[^18]: «Si propter se homo diligendus est, fruimur eo; si propter aliud, utimur eo: videtur autem mihi propter aliud diligendus. Quod enim propter se diligendum est, in eo constituitur beata vita, cuius etiam spes hoc tempore nos consolatur. In homine autem spes ponenda non est, quia *Maledictus est qui hoc facit*. Ergo si liquide advertas, nec seipso quisquam frui debet, quia non se debet diligere propter se, sed propter illud quo fruendum est». Huic autem contrarium videtur quod Apostolus ad Philemonem[^19] loquens ait: *Ita, frater, ego te fruar in Domino*. Quod ita determinat Augustinus[^20]: «Si dixisset tantum *te fruar*, et non addidisset *in Domino*, videretur finem dilectionis ac spem constituisse in eo; sed quia illud addidit, in Domino se finem posuisse eodemque frui significavit». «Cum enim», ut idem Augustinus ait, «homine in Deo frueris, Deo potius quam homine frueris».

##### *Utrum Deus nobis fruatur, an utatur.*

Sed cum Deus diligat nos, ut frequenter Scriptura dicit, quae eius dilectionem erga nos multum commendat, quaerit Augustinus[^21], quomodo diligit, an ut utens, an ut fruens, et procedit ita: «Si fruitur nobis, eget bono nostro, quod nemo sanus dixerit; ait enim Propheta[^22]: *Bonorum meorum non eges*. Omne enim bonum nostrum vel ipse est, vel ab ipso est: non ergo fruitur nobis, sed utitur. Si enim nec fruitur nobis nec utitur, non invenio, quomodo diligat nos». «Neque tamen sic utitur nobis, ut nos aliis rebus. Nos enim res, quibus utimur, ad id referimus, ut Dei bonitate perfruamur; Deus vero ad suam bonitatem usum nostrum refert: ille enim miseretur nostri propter suam bonitatem, nos autem nobis invicem propter illius bonitatem; ille nostri miseretur, ut se perfruamur, nos vero invicem nostri miseremur, ut illo fruamur. Cum enim nos alicuius miseremur et alicui consulimus, ad eius quidem facimus utilitatem eamque intuemur, sed et nostra fit consequens, cum misericordiam, quam aliis impendimus, non relinquit Deus sine mercede. Haec autem merces summa est, ut ipso perfruamur[^23]». Item: «Quia bonus est, sumus, et inquantum sumus, boni sumus. Porro quia etiam iustus est, non impune mali sumus, et in quantum mali sumus, in tantum etiam

<!-- page 28 -->

minus sumus. Ille igitur usus, quo nobis utitur Deus, non ad eius, sed ad nostram utilitatem refertur, ad eius vero tantummodo bonitatem[^24]».

##### *Utrum virtutibus sit fruendum, an utendum.*

Hic considerandum est, utrum virtutibus sit utendum, an fruendum. Quibusdam videtur, quod eis sit utendum et non fruendum, et hoc confirmant auctoritate Augustini[^25], qui, ut praetaxatum est, dicit, non esse fruendum nisi Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono. Item dicunt, ideo non esse fruendum eis, quia propter se amandae non sunt, sed propter aeternam beatitudinem. Illud autem, quo fruendum est, propter se amandum est. Sed quod virtutes propter se amandae non sint, immo propter solam beatitudinem, probant auctoritate Augustini, qui in libro decimo tertio de Trinitate[^26] contra quosdam ait: «Forte virtutes, quas propter solam beatitudinem amamus, sic persuadere nobis audent, ut ipsam beatitudinem non amemus; quod si faciunt, etiam ipsas utique amare desistimus, quando illam, propter quam solam istas amavimus, non amamus». Ecce his verbis videtur Augustinus ostendere, quod virtutes non propter se, sed propter solam beatitudinem amandae sint. Quod si ita est, ergo eis fruendum non est. Aliis vero contra videtur, scilicet quod eis fruendum sit, quia[^27] propter se petendae et amandae sunt. Et hoc confirmant auctoritate Ambrosii[^28], qui ait super illum locum Epistolae ad Galatas: *Fructus autem spiritus est caritas, gaudium, pax, patientia* etc.: «Haec non nominat opera, sed fructus, quia propter se petenda sunt». Si vero propter se petenda sunt, ergo propter se amanda. Nos autem harum quae videtur[^29] auctoritatum repugnantiam de medio eximere cupientes, dicimus, quod virtutes propter se petendae et amandae sunt, et tamen propter solam beatitudinem. Propter se quidem amandae sunt, quia delectant sui possessores sincera et sancta delectatione et in eis pariunt gaudium spirituale. Verumtamen non est hic consistendum, sed ultra gradiendum. Non hic haereat dilectionis[^30] gressus, neque hic sit dilectionis terminus, sed referatur hoc ad illud summum bonum, cui soli omnino inhaerendum est, quia illud propter se tantum amandum est, et ultra illud nihil quaerendum est; illud est enim supremus finis. Ideo Augustinus dicit, quod eas diligimus propter solam beatitudinem, non quin[^31] eas propter se diligamus, sed quia id ipsum, quod eas diligimus, referimus ad illud summum bonum, cui soli inhaerendum est, et[^32] in eo permanendum finisque laetitiae ponendus; quare virtutibus non est fruendum. Sed dicet aliquis: Frui est amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se ipsam, ut praedictum est; si ergo virtutes propter se amandae sunt, et eis fruendum est. Ad quod dicimus: in illa descriptione, ubi dicitur *propter se ipsam*, intelligendum est tantummodo, ut scilicet ametur propter se ipsam tantum, ut non referatur ad aliud, sed ibi ponatur finis, ut supra ostendit Augustinus dicens[^33]: «Si inhaeseris atque permanseris, finem ponens laetitiae tuae, tunc vere et proprie frui dicendus es. Quod non est faciendum nisi in illa Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono». Utendum est ergo virtutibus et per eas fruendum summo bono; ita et de voluntate bona dicimus. Unde Augustinus in libro decimo de Trinitate[^34] ait: «Voluntas est per quam fruimur»; ita et per virtutes fruimur; non eis, nisi forte aliqua virtus sit Deus, ut caritas, de qua post tractabitur[^35].

##### *Epilogus.*

Omnium igitur, quae dicta sunt, ex quo de rebus specialiter tractavimus, haec summa est[^36]: quod aliae sunt, quibus fruendum est, aliae, quibus utendum est, aliae, quae fruuntur et utuntur; et inter eas, quibus utendum est, etiam quaedam sunt, per quas fruimur, ut virtutes et potentiae animi, quae sunt naturalia bona. De quibus omnibus, antequam de signis tractemus, agendum est, ac primum de rebus, quibus fruendum est, scilicet de sancta atque individua Trinitate.

## English

## BOOK ONE OF THE SENTENCES.

### ON THE UNITY AND TRINITY OF GOD.

### DISTINCTION I.

#### Chapter I.

*All teaching is about things or about signs.*

As we, by God's grace going before us, considered again and again with diligent investigation the contents of the Old and New Law, it became known to us that the treatment of sacred Scripture is chiefly concerned with things or with signs. For, as the eminent doctor Augustine says in his book *On Christian Doctrine*[^1]: «All teaching is either of things or of signs. But things also are learned through signs. Properly however things are here called those which are not employed to signify something; while signs are those whose use lies in signifying». But of these there are some whose whole use is in signifying, not in justifying — that is to say[^2], those which we use only for the sake of signifying something, like certain Sacraments of the Law; and others, which not only signify but also confer that which inwardly assists, as the evangelical Sacraments. «From this it is plainly understood what are here called signs, namely those things which are employed to signify something. Every sign therefore is also some thing. For that which is no thing, as in the same place Augustine says, is altogether nothing; not however conversely[^3] is every thing a sign», because it is not employed for signifying something. And when the studious and modest speculation of theologians applies itself to these matters, it will recognise that the divine Scripture observes a prescribed form in its teaching. Concerning these things, therefore, we who wish, God leading, to open up some access toward understanding divine things, must discourse; and we shall treat first of things, afterwards of signs.

#### Chapter II.

*On the things which are to be enjoyed or to be used, and on those which enjoy and use.*

«This therefore must be considered in things, as in the same place[^4] Augustine says, that some things are those which are to be enjoyed, others which are to be used, others which both enjoy and use. Those which are to be enjoyed make us blessed; by these which are to be used we are helped, while striving toward blessedness, and as it were supported, so that we may be able to reach and cleave to those things which make us blessed. The things which enjoy and use are we ourselves, constituted as it were between the two», both Angels and Saints[^5]. «But to enjoy is to cleave with love to some thing for its own sake; to use is to refer that which has come into use toward obtaining that which is to be enjoyed; otherwise it is to abuse, not to use. For an illicit use ought to be named an abuse or misuse[^6]». «The things therefore which are to be enjoyed are the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Yet this same Trinity is a certain supreme thing and common to all who enjoy it — if indeed it ought to be called a thing and not the cause of all things, if even cause. For a name cannot easily be found which befits such great excellence, unless that it is better said: This Trinity is one God». But the things which are to be used are the world and what is created in it. Whence Augustine in the same place[^7]: «This world must be used, not enjoyed, so that the invisible things of God may be seen, being understood through the things that are made[^8] — that is, that from temporal things eternal things may be grasped». Likewise in the same place: «Among all things, those alone are to be enjoyed which are eternal and unchangeable; the rest must be used, so that we may attain to the enjoyment of those[^9]». Whence Augustine in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^10]: «We enjoy those things known, in which the will, delighted for their own sake, comes to rest; but we use those things which we refer to something else, which is to be enjoyed».

#### Chapter III.

*What it is to enjoy and to use.*

But it must be noted that the same Augustine in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^11], taking *to use* and *to enjoy* otherwise than above, says thus: «To use is to take up something into

<!-- page 27 -->

the power of the will; but to enjoy is to use with joy not still of hope, but already of the reality; and therefore everyone who enjoys also uses, for he takes up something into the power of the will with the end of delight. But not everyone who uses also enjoys; if that which he takes up into the power of the will he seeks not on its own account, but for the sake of something else».

And note that[^12] Augustine seems to say that those alone enjoy who rejoice in the reality, not yet in hope; and so in this life we do not seem to enjoy, but only to use, where we rejoice in hope — even though it was said above that to enjoy is to cleave with love to some thing for its own sake, in which way also many here cleave to God.

These things, then, which seem to contradict one another, we resolve thus, saying that we enjoy both here and in the future, but there properly and perfectly and fully, where by sight we shall behold what we shall enjoy; here, however, while we walk in hope, we do indeed enjoy, but not so fully. Whence Augustine in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^13]: «We enjoy those things known, in which the will, delighted for their own sake, comes to rest». The same in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^14] says: «The Angels enjoying Him are already blessed by that whom we also desire to enjoy; and the more we already enjoy in this life — whether through a mirror or in a riddle — the more tolerably we bear our pilgrimage and the more ardently we desire to end it».

It may also be said that he who enjoys, even in this life, has not only the joy of hope but also of the reality, because he is already delighted in what he loves, and so already in some measure holds the thing. It stands therefore that[^15] we ought to enjoy God, not use Him. «For by that», as Augustine says[^16], «you enjoy by which you are made blessed and in which you place your hope, that you may come to it». On this the same says in the book *On Christian Doctrine*: «We say that we enjoy a thing which we love for its own sake, and that we are to enjoy a thing only by which we are made blessed; the rest, however, are to be used». Yet frequently it is said *to enjoy* with the sense of *to use with delight*. For when that which is loved is present, it brings delight with it. If, however, you pass through it and refer it to that wherein one must abide, you use it, and abusively; you are not properly said to enjoy. But if you cleave and abide, placing the end of your gladness in it, then you are truly and properly to be said to enjoy; which is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, the supreme and unchangeable good[^17]».

##### *Whether man is to be enjoyed or used.*

But since men, who enjoy and use other things, are themselves certain things, it is asked, «whether they ought to enjoy themselves, or use, or both». To which Augustine in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^18] thus replies: «If man is to be loved for his own sake, we enjoy him; if for the sake of another, we use him: to me, however, it seems that he is to be loved for the sake of another. For that which is to be loved for its own sake is that in which the blessed life is constituted, the hope of which also consoles us in this time. But hope is not to be placed in man, for *Cursed is he who does this*. Therefore, if you note clearly, no one ought even to enjoy himself, because one ought not to love oneself for one's own sake, but for the sake of that which is to be enjoyed». But to this seems contrary what the Apostle in speaking to Philemon[^19] says: *So, brother, I shall enjoy thee in the Lord*. Which Augustine thus settles[^20]: «If he had said only *I shall enjoy thee*, and had not added *in the Lord*, he would have seemed to set the end of love and of hope in him; but because he added that, he signified that he had set his end in the Lord and that he was enjoying the same». «For when», as the same Augustine says, «you enjoy a man in God, you enjoy God rather than man».

##### *Whether God enjoys us or uses us.*

But since God loves us, as Scripture frequently says, which much commends His love toward us, Augustine asks[^21] how He loves us, whether as one using or as one enjoying; and he proceeds thus: «If He enjoys us, He has need of our good, which no sane man would say; for the Prophet says[^22]: *Of my goods Thou hast no need*. For all our good either is Himself or is from Him: He does not therefore enjoy us, but uses us. For if He neither enjoys us nor uses us, I do not see how He loves us». «Yet He does not so use us as we use other things. For we refer the things which we use to this, that we may enjoy the goodness of God; but God refers our use to His own goodness: for He has mercy on us because of His own goodness, but we on one another because of His goodness; He has mercy on us, that we may enjoy Him fully, but we have mercy on one another, that we may enjoy Him. For when we have mercy on someone and counsel someone, we do it indeed for his usefulness and look to that, but ours follows as a consequence, since God does not leave without reward the mercy which we show to others. But this reward is the highest, that we may enjoy Him fully[^23]». Likewise: «Because He is good, we are; and inasmuch as we are, we are good. Further, because He is also just, we are not evil with impunity, and inasmuch as we are evil, by so much also

<!-- page 28 -->

we are less. That use therefore by which God uses us is referred not to His utility, but to ours; to His, however, only to His goodness[^24]».

##### *Whether the virtues are to be enjoyed or used.*

Here it must be considered whether the virtues are to be used or enjoyed. To some it seems that they are to be used and not enjoyed, and they confirm this by the authority of Augustine[^25], who, as has been noted above, says that none is to be enjoyed except the Trinity, that is, the supreme and unchangeable good. Likewise they say that they are not to be enjoyed because they are not to be loved for their own sake, but for the sake of eternal blessedness. But that which is to be enjoyed is to be loved for its own sake. And that the virtues are not to be loved for their own sake, but rather for blessedness alone, they prove by the authority of Augustine, who in the thirteenth book *On the Trinity*[^26] says against certain ones: «Perhaps the virtues, which we love only for the sake of blessedness, dare to persuade us thus, that we should not love blessedness itself; if they do this, we cease at any rate to love them also, when we do not love that for which alone we have loved them». Behold, by these words Augustine seems to show that the virtues are to be loved not for their own sake, but for blessedness alone. And if this is so, therefore they are not to be enjoyed. But to others on the contrary it seems, namely that they are to be enjoyed, because[^27] they are to be sought and loved for their own sake. And this they confirm by the authority of Ambrose[^28], who says, on that passage of the Epistle to the Galatians: *But the fruit of the Spirit is charity, joy, peace, patience* etc.: «He does not name these works, but fruits, because they are to be sought for their own sake». But if they are to be sought for their own sake, therefore they are to be loved for their own sake. We, however, desiring to remove from the midst the apparent[^29] repugnance of these authorities, say that the virtues are to be sought and loved for their own sake, and yet on account of blessedness alone. For their own sake indeed they are to be loved, because they delight their possessors with sincere and holy delight and bring forth in them spiritual joy. Nevertheless one must not stop here, but pass beyond. Let not the step of love[^30] adhere here, nor let the term of love be here, but let this be referred to that supreme good, to which alone wholly one must cleave, because that alone is to be loved for its own sake, and beyond it nothing is to be sought; for that is the supreme end. Therefore Augustine says that we love them for blessedness alone — not but that[^31] we love them for their own sake, but because that very fact, that we love them, we refer to that supreme good, to which alone we must cleave, and[^32] in which we must abide and place the end of gladness; wherefore the virtues are not to be enjoyed. But someone will say: To enjoy is to cleave with love to some thing for its own sake, as has been said; if therefore the virtues are to be loved for their own sake, they too are to be enjoyed. To which we say: in that description, where it is said *for its own sake*, it must be understood only thus, that namely it should be loved for its own sake alone, so that it should not be referred to another, but the end should be placed there, as Augustine showed above saying[^33]: «If you cleave and abide, placing the end of your gladness, then you are truly and properly to be said to enjoy. Which is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, the supreme and unchangeable good». Therefore the virtues are to be used and through them the supreme good is to be enjoyed; and so likewise we say of the good will. Whence Augustine in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^34] says: «The will is that by which we enjoy»; and so also through the virtues we enjoy; not them, unless perhaps some virtue be God, as charity, of which it will be treated afterwards[^35].

##### *Epilogue.*

Of all things therefore that have been said, since we have specially treated of things, this is the sum[^36]: that some are those which are to be enjoyed, others which are to be used, others which enjoy and use; and among those which are to be used there are also certain ones through which we enjoy, such as the virtues and powers of the soul, which are natural goods. Concerning all these, before we treat of signs, it must be dealt with, and first concerning those things which are to be enjoyed, namely concerning the holy and undivided Trinity.

[^5]: **La.** Omnes codd. cum edd. 1, 6, 8 *Angeli sancti*. Pro utraque lectione militant rationes; unde nihil immutavimus. Lectio *Angeli sancti* comprehendit sub *nos sumus* omnes homines bonos, sive sint in via, sive in patria; et addit *sancti* ad *Angeli*, ut excludantur daemones, cum damnati nullo modo Deo fruantur. Altera vero lectio ex una parte ponit *nos*, qui soli stricto sensu constituti sumus inter res, quibus fruendum, scil. Deo, et quibus utendum ut mediis ad Deum, ex altera parte beatos Angelos et Sanctos, qui proprie fruuntur Deo nec indigent uti mediis ad ultimum finem, sed tamen aliquo modo creaturis uti possunt ad alios fines.

    **En.** All the codices, together with edd. 1, 6, 8, read *Angeli sancti* ("holy Angels"). Reasons support each reading; hence we have altered nothing. The reading *Angeli sancti* comprehends under *we ourselves* all good men, whether they be on the way or in the fatherland, and adds *sancti* to *Angeli* in order to exclude the demons, since the damned in no way enjoy God. The other reading places on one side *we*, who alone in strict sense are constituted between the things to be enjoyed — namely God — and the things to be used as means to God; and on the other side the blessed Angels and Saints, who properly enjoy God and do not need to use means toward the ultimate end, yet can in some way use creatures for other ends.

[^28]: **La.** Super Epist. ad Galatas c. 5, 22: «Non dixit: *opera spiritus caritas est*, sed *fructus spiritus*». — Verba, quae sequuntur: «quia propter se petenda sunt», idem Lombardus habet *Collect. in Epist. Pauli ad Gal.* Patrolog. Lat. tom. CXCII, col. 160: «Fructus autem: quasi dicat, haec quae enumeravi, sunt opera carnis; sed ista sunt opera spiritus, quae sequuntur, quae tamen non nominat opera, sed *fructus*, quia propter se petenda sunt». Ex his confirmatur lectio codd. et ed. 1: *haec non nominat*, dum ceterae edd. legunt *Hic non nominat*; Vat. et ed. 4 *At hic* etc. Deinde pro *nominat* cod. D *nominantur*. Postea Vat. et edd. 2, 4, 7, 9 *ergo et propter*; omisimus *et*, postulantibus mss. et aliis edd. Denique edd. 1, 3, 8 adiungunt *amanda sunt*.

    **En.** On the Epistle to the Galatians c. 5, 22: «He did not say *the works of the Spirit is charity*, but *the fruit of the Spirit*». — The words which follow, «because they are to be sought for their own sake», the same Lombard has in his *Collectanea in Epist. Pauli ad Gal.*, PL tom. CXCII, col. 160: «*Fructus autem*: as if he said, these which I have enumerated are works of the flesh; but these which follow are works of the Spirit — which however he does not name *opera*, but *fructus*, because they are to be sought for their own sake». From these is confirmed the reading of the codices and ed. 1: *haec non nominat*, while the other editions read *Hic non nominat*; the Vatican edition and ed. 4 [read] *At hic* etc. Then for *nominat* codex D [reads] *nominantur*. Afterwards the Vatican edition and edd. 2, 4, 7, 9 read *ergo et propter*; we have omitted *et*, the mss. and other editions requiring it. Finally edd. 1, 3, 8 add *amanda sunt*.
