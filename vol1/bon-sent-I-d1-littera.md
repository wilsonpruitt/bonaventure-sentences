# I Sent., d. 1, Littera Magistri (Caps. I–III)
## *Text of the Master, Distinction I*

**Peter Lombard, *Sententiae in IV Libris Distinctae*, Book I, Dist. 1, Chapters 1–3.** This is Lombard's own text (not Bonaventure's commentary), which Bonaventure quotes and discusses throughout Dist. 1. The three chapters are drawn almost entirely from Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* I.2–4, 22, 31–37 and *On the Trinity* X.10–11.

---

## Latin

### DISTINCTIO I.

#### Cap. I.
*Omnis doctrina vel de rebus est vel de signis.*

Veteris ac novae Legis continentiam diligenti indagine etiam atque etiam considerantibus nobis, praevia Dei gratia, innotuit, sacrae paginae tractatum circa res vel signa praecipue versari. Ut enim egregius doctor Augustinus ait in libro *de Doctrina christiana*: «Omnis doctrina vel rerum est, vel signorum. Sed res etiam per signa discuntur. Proprie autem hic res appellantur, quae non ad significandum aliquid adhibentur; signa vero, quorum usus est in significando.» Eorum autem aliqua sunt, quorum omnis usus est in significando, non in iustificando, id est, quibus non utimur nisi aliquid significandi gratia, ut aliqua Sacramenta legalia; alia, quae non solum significant, sed conferunt quod intus adiuvet, sicut evangelica Sacramenta. «Ex quo aperte intelligitur, quae hic appellentur signa, res illae videlicet, quae ad significandum aliquid adhibentur. Omne igitur signum etiam res aliqua est. Quod enim nulla res est, ut in eodem Augustinus ait, omnino nihil est; non autem e converso omnis res signum est», quia non adhibetur ad significandum aliquid. Cumque his intenderit theologorum speculatio studiosa atque modesta, divinam Scripturam formam praescriptam in doctrina tenere advertit. De his ergo nobis aditum ad res divinas aliquatenus intelligendas, Deo duce, aperire volentibus disserendum est; et primum de rebus, postea de signis disseremus.

#### Cap. II.
*De rebus quibus fruendum est, vel utendum, et de his quae fruuntur et utuntur.*

«Id ergo in rebus considerandum est», ut in eodem Augustinus ait, «quod res aliae sunt, quibus fruendum est, aliae, quibus utendum est, aliae, quae fruuntur et utuntur. Illae, quibus fruendum est, nos beatos faciunt; istis, quibus utendum est, tendentes ad beatitudinem adiuvamur et quasi adminiculamur, ut ad illas res, quae nos beatos faciunt, pervenire eisque inhaerere possimus. Res vero, quae fruuntur et utuntur, nos sumus, quasi inter utrasque constituti», et Angeli et Sancti. «Frui autem est amore alicui rei inhaerere propter se ipsam; uti vero id quod in usum venerit referre ad obtinendum illud, quo fruendum est; alias abuti est, non uti. Nam usus illicitus abusus vel abusio nominari debet.» «Res igitur, quibus fruendum est, sunt Pater et Filius et Spiritus sanctus. Eadem tamen Trinitas quaedam summa res est communisque omnibus fruentibus ea, si tamen res dici debet et non rerum omnium causa, si tamen et causa. Non enim facile potest inveniri nomen, quod tantae excellentiae conveniat, nisi quod melius dicitur Trinitas haec unus Deus.» Res autem, quibus utendum est, mundus est et in eo creata. Unde Augustinus in eodem: «Utendum est hoc mundo, non fruendum, ut invisibilia Dei per ea quae facta sunt, intellecta, conspiciantur, id est, ut de temporalibus aeterna capiantur.» Item in eodem: «In omnibus rebus illae tantum sunt, quibus fruendum est, quae aeternae et incommutabiles sunt; ceteris autem utendum est, ut ad illarum perfruitionem perveniatur.» Unde Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate*: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus ipsis propter se voluntas delectata conquiescit; utimur vero eis quae ad aliud referimus, quo fruendum est.»

#### Cap. III.
*Quid sit frui et uti.*

Notandum vero, quod idem Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate*, aliter quam supra accipiens *uti* et *frui*, sic dicit: «Uti est assumere aliquid in facultatem voluntatis; frui autem est uti cum gaudio non adhuc spei, sed iam rei; ideoque omnis, qui fruitur, utitur; assumit enim aliquid in facultatem voluntatis cum fine delectationis. Non autem omnis, qui utitur, fruitur; si id quod in facultatem voluntatis assumit, non propter ipsum, sed propter aliud appetit.»

Et attende, quod videtur Augustinus dicere, illos frui tantum, qui in re gaudent, non iam in spe; et ita in hac vita non videmur frui, sed tantum uti, ubi gaudemus in spe, cum supra dictum sit, frui amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se, qualiter etiam hic multi adhaerent Deo.

Haec ergo, quae sibi contradicere videntur, sic determinamus dicentes, nos et hic et in futuro frui, sed ibi proprie et perfecte et plene, ubi per speciem videbimus quo fruemur; hic autem, dum in spe ambulamus, fruimur quidem, sed non adeo plene. Unde Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate*: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus ipsis voluntas propter se delectata conquiescit.» Idem in libro *de Doctrina christiana* ait: «Angeli illo fruentes iam beati sunt, quo et nos frui desideramus; et quantum in hac vita iam fruimur vel per speculum vel in aenigmate, tanto nostram peregrinationem et tolerabilius sustinemus et ardentius finire cupimus.»

Potest etiam dici, quod qui fruitur etiam in hac vita non tantum habet gaudium spei, sed etiam rei, quia iam delectatur in eo quod diligit, et ita iam rem aliquatenus tenet. Constat ergo, quia debemus Deo frui, non uti. «Illo enim», ut ait Augustinus, «frueris, quo efficieris beatus et in quo spem ponis, ut ad id pervenias.» De hoc idem ait in libro *de Doctrina christiana*: «Dicimus ea re nos frui, quam diligimus propter se, et ea re nobis fruendum esse tantum, qua efficimur beati; ceteris vero utendum.» Frequenter tamen dicitur frui cum delectatione uti. Cum enim adest quod diligitur, etiam delectationem secum gerit. Si tamen per eam transieris et ad illud, ubi permanendum est, eam retuleris, uteris ea et abusive, non proprie diceris frui. Si vero inhaeseris atque permanseris, finem in ea ponens laetitiae tuae, tunc vere et proprie frui dicendus es; quod non est faciendum nisi in illa Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono.

Cum autem homines, qui fruuntur et utuntur aliis rebus, res aliquae sint, quaeritur, «utrum frui se debeant, an uti, an utrumque». Ad quod sic respondet Augustinus in libro *de Doctrina christiana*: «Si propter se homo diligendus est, fruimur eo; si propter aliud, utimur eo: videtur autem mihi propter aliud diligendus. Quod enim propter se diligendum est, in eo constituitur beata vita, cuius etiam spes hoc tempore nos consolatur. In homine autem spes ponenda non est, quia *Maledictus* est *qui hoc facit*. Ergo si liquide advertas, nec seipso quisquam frui debet, quia non se debet diligere propter se, sed propter illud quo fruendum est.» Huic autem contrarium videtur, quod Apostolus ad Philemonem loquens ait: *Ita, frater, ego te fruar in Domino*. Quod ita determinat Augustinus: «Si dixisset tantum *te fruar*, et non addidisset *in Domino*, videretur finem dilectionis ac spem constituisse in eo; sed quia illud addidit, in Domino se finem posuisse eodemque frui significavit.» «Cum enim», ut idem Augustinus ait, «homine in Deo frueris, Deo potius quam homine frueris.»

Sed cum Deus diligat nos, ut frequenter Scriptura dicit, quae eius dilectionem erga nos multum commendat, quaerit Augustinus, quomodo diligit, an ut utens, an ut fruens, et procedit ita: «Si fruitur nobis, eget bono nostro, quod nemo sanus dixerit; ait enim Propheta: *Bonorum meorum non eges*. Omne enim bonum nostrum vel ipse est, vel ab ipso est: non ergo fruitur nobis, sed utitur. Si enim nec fruitur nobis nec utitur, non invenio, quomodo diligat nos.» «Neque tamen sic utitur nobis, ut nos aliis rebus. Nos enim res, quibus utimur, ad id referimus, ut Dei bonitate perfruamur; Deus vero ad suam bonitatem usum nostrum refert: ille enim miseretur nostri propter suam bonitatem, nos autem nobis invicem propter illius bonitatem; ille nostri miseretur, ut se perfruamur, nos vero invicem nostri miseremur, ut illo fruamur. Cum enim nos alicuius miseremur et alicui consulimus, ad eius quidem facimus utilitatem eamque intuemur, sed et nostra fit consequens, cum misericordiam, quam aliis impendimus, non relinquit Deus sine mercede. Haec autem merces summa est, ut ipso perfruamur.» Item: «Quia bonus est, sumus, et inquantum sumus, boni sumus. Porro quia etiam iustus est, non impune mali sumus, et in quantum mali sumus, in tantum etiam minus sumus. Ille igitur usus, quo nobis utitur Deus, non ad eius, sed ad nostram utilitatem refertur, ad eius vero tantummodo bonitatem.»

Hic considerandum est, utrum virtutibus sit utendum, an fruendum. Quibusdam videtur, quod eis sit utendum et non fruendum, et hoc confirmant auctoritate Augustini, qui, ut praetaxatum est, dicit, non esse fruendum nisi Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono. Item dicunt, ideo non esse fruendum eis, quia propter se amandae non sunt, sed propter aeternam beatitudinem. Illud autem, quo fruendum est, propter se amandum est. Sed quod virtutes propter se amandae non sint, immo propter solam beatitudinem, probant auctoritate Augustini, qui in libro decimo tertio *de Trinitate* contra quosdam ait: «Forte virtutes, quas propter solam beatitudinem amamus, sic persuadere nobis audent, ut ipsam beatitudinem non amemus; quod si faciunt, etiam ipsas utique amare desistimus, quando illam, propter quam solam istas amavimus, non amamus.» Ecce his verbis videtur Augustinus ostendere, quod virtutes non propter se, sed propter solam beatitudinem amandae sint. Quod si ita est, ergo eis fruendum non est. Aliis vero contra videtur, scilicet quod eis fruendum sit, quia propter se petendae et amandae sunt. Et hoc confirmant auctoritate Ambrosii, qui ait super illum locum Epistolae ad Galatas: *Fructus autem spiritus est caritas, gaudium, pax, patientia* etc.: «Haec non nominat *opera*, sed *fructus*, quia propter se petenda sunt.» Si vero propter se petenda sunt, ergo propter se amanda. Nos autem harum quae videtur auctoritatum repugnantiam de medio eximere cupientes, dicimus, quod virtutes propter se petendae et amandae sunt, et tamen propter solam beatitudinem. Propter se quidem amandae sunt, quia delectant sui possessores sincera et sancta delectatione et in eis pariunt gaudium spirituale. Verumtamen non est hic consistendum, sed ultra gradiendum. Non hic haereat dilectionis gressus, neque hic sit dilectionis terminus, sed referatur hoc ad illud summum bonum, cui soli omnino inhaerendum est, quia illud propter se tantum amandum est, et ultra illud nihil quaerendum est; illud est enim supremus finis. Ideo Augustinus dicit, quod eas diligimus propter solam beatitudinem, non quin eas propter se diligamus, sed quia id ipsum, quod eas diligimus, referimus ad illud summum bonum, cui soli inhaerendum est, et in eo permanendum finisque laetitiae ponendus; quare virtutibus non est fruendum. Sed dicet aliquis: Frui est amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se ipsam, ut praedictum est; si ergo virtutes propter se amandae sunt, et eis fruendum est. Ad quod dicimus: in illa descriptione, ubi dicitur *propter se ipsam*, intelligendum est tantummodo, ut scilicet ametur propter se ipsam tantum, ut non referatur ad aliud, sed ibi ponatur finis, ut supra ostendit Augustinus dicens: «Si inhaeseris atque permanseris, finem ponens laetitiae tuae, tunc vere et proprie frui dicendus es. Quod non est faciendum nisi in illa Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono.» Utendum est ergo virtutibus et per eas fruendum summo bono; ita et de voluntate bona dicimus. Unde Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate* ait: «Voluntas est per quam fruimur»; ita et per virtutes fruimur; non eis, nisi forte aliqua virtus sit Deus, ut caritas, de qua post tractabitur.

*Epilogus.* Omnium igitur, quae dicta sunt, ex quo de rebus specialiter tractavimus, haec summa est: quod aliae sunt, quibus fruendum est, aliae, quibus utendum est, aliae, quae fruuntur et utuntur; et inter eas, quibus utendum est, etiam quaedam sunt, per quas fruimur, ut virtutes et potentiae animi, quae sunt naturalia bona. De quibus omnibus, antequam de signis tractemus, agendum est, ac primum de rebus, quibus fruendum est, scilicet de sancta atque individua Trinitate.

## English

### DISTINCTIO I.

#### Ch. 1.
*All doctrine is either of things or of signs.*

In attentively considering, again and again, the content of the Old and New Law, it has become clear to us, by the preceding grace of God, that the treatment of sacred Scripture turns chiefly upon *things* and *signs*. For, as the distinguished doctor Augustine says in his book *On Christian Doctrine*: "All doctrine is either of things or of signs. But things too are learned through signs. Here, properly, those are called *things* which are not employed to signify anything else; and those are called *signs* whose use is in signifying." Of these signs, some are such that their whole use lies in signifying and not in justifying — that is, we use them only for the sake of signifying something, as is the case with certain legal Sacraments. Others not only signify but also confer that which aids inwardly, as do the Sacraments of the Gospel. "From which it is plainly understood that those things are here called signs which are employed for the sake of signifying something. Every sign, therefore, is also a thing of some sort. For what is no thing, as Augustine himself says in the same place, is altogether nothing. Not, however, conversely is every thing a sign," since not every thing is employed for the sake of signifying something. And since the studious and modest inquiry of theologians has intended these things, it perceives that divine Scripture keeps to a set form in its teaching. Our task, therefore — since we wish, with God's help, to open some way into the understanding of divine things — is to treat these matters; and first we shall treat of things, and afterward of signs.

#### Ch. 2.
*Of things which are to be enjoyed, or to be used, and of those which both enjoy and use.*

"This, therefore, is to be considered among things," as Augustine says in the same book: "some things are to be enjoyed, some are to be used, and some both enjoy and use. Those which are to be enjoyed make us blessed; by those which are to be used we are helped and as it were sustained as we tend toward beatitude, so that we may come to and cleave to the things which make us blessed. The things which both enjoy and use are we ourselves, constituted as it were between the two classes" — we, the angels, and the saints. "To *enjoy* is to cleave in love to some thing for its own sake; to *use* is to refer what has come into use to obtaining that which is to be enjoyed. Otherwise [the act] is *misuse*, not use. For an unlawful use ought to be called *abuse*." "The things, therefore, which are to be enjoyed are the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Yet the same Trinity is one certain supreme Thing common to all who enjoy it — if indeed it ought to be called a thing and not rather the cause of all things, if it is to be called a cause at all. For it is not easy to find a name that suits so great an excellence, except that it is better said that this Trinity is one God." The things to be used are the world and the created things within it. Whence Augustine says, in the same book: "This world is to be used, not enjoyed, that the invisible things of God may be understood and seen through the things that have been made — that is, that from the temporal we may grasp the eternal." Again, in the same: "Of all things, those alone are to be enjoyed which are eternal and unchangeable; the rest are to be used, so that through their use we may come to the full enjoyment of those [eternal] things." Whence Augustine says in Book X *On the Trinity*: "We enjoy the things we know, in which the will, delighted for their own sake, comes to rest; we use, on the other hand, those things that we refer to something else, which is to be enjoyed."

#### Ch. 3.
*What* enjoyment *and* use *are.*

It should be noted, however, that the same Augustine, in Book X *On the Trinity*, taking *use* and *enjoyment* in a different sense than above, speaks thus: "To *use* is to take something into the power of the will; to *enjoy*, on the other hand, is to use with the joy no longer of hope but now of the reality itself. And so everyone who enjoys also uses — for he takes something into the power of the will with the end of delight; but not everyone who uses enjoys, if what he takes into the power of the will he seeks not for its own sake but for something else."

And observe that Augustine seems to say that only those enjoy who rejoice in the reality, not still in hope — and on this reckoning we do not seem to enjoy in this life, but only to use, where we rejoice in hope: since it was said above that *to enjoy* is to cleave in love to some thing for its own sake, in which manner many do cleave even here to God.

These things, therefore, which seem to contradict one another, we reconcile by saying that we enjoy both *here* and *in the future life* — but *there* properly and perfectly and fully, where we shall see through the *species* that which we shall enjoy; *here*, however, while we walk in hope, we enjoy indeed, but not yet so fully. Hence Augustine in Book X *On the Trinity*: "We enjoy the things we know, in which the will itself, delighted for their own sake, comes to rest." The same, in *On Christian Doctrine*: "The angels, enjoying that [God] whom we too desire to enjoy, are already blessed; and the more we already enjoy him in this life — whether through a glass or in a riddle — the more tolerably do we bear our pilgrimage and the more ardently do we long to end it."

It may also be said that one who enjoys even in this life has not merely the joy of hope but also the joy of the reality, because he is already delighted in what he loves, and so already holds the reality in some measure. It is certain, therefore, that we ought to *enjoy* God, not *use* him. "For by that," as Augustine says, "you enjoy by which you are made blessed and in which you place your hope, so as to attain it." Of this the same Augustine says in *On Christian Doctrine*: "We say that we *enjoy* that thing which we love for its own sake, and that that alone is to be enjoyed by which we are made blessed; the rest is to be used." Frequently, however, *to enjoy* is said to mean *to use with delight*: for when what is loved is present, it brings delight with it. If, however, you pass through that [delight] and refer it to that in which one must remain, you are using it, and to say that you *enjoy* is abusive speech, not proper. But if you cleave and remain, setting the end of your joy in it, then you are truly and properly said to enjoy — which is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, the highest and unchangeable good.

And since men — who both enjoy and use other things — are themselves things of a sort, the question is asked: "whether they ought to enjoy themselves, or to use themselves, or both." To this Augustine replies in *On Christian Doctrine*: "If a man is to be loved for his own sake, we enjoy him; if for something else, we use him. It seems to me, however, that he is to be loved for something else. For that which is to be loved for its own sake is that in which the blessed life consists, the hope of which consoles us in this time. But hope is not to be placed in man, because *Cursed* is *he who does this*. Therefore, if you clearly mark it, no one ought to enjoy even himself, because one ought not to love oneself for one's own sake, but for the sake of that which is to be enjoyed." Yet against this seems to stand what the Apostle says to Philemon: *Thus, brother, may I enjoy you in the Lord*. Augustine resolves it thus: "If he had said only *may I enjoy you*, and not added *in the Lord*, he would have seemed to place the end and hope of his love in him; but because he added the former, he signified that he placed his end *in the Lord* and that he enjoyed him." "For," as the same Augustine says, "when you enjoy a man in God, you enjoy God rather than man."

But since God loves us — as Scripture frequently says, commending his love for us greatly — Augustine asks in what way he loves us: whether as using or as enjoying. He proceeds thus: "If he enjoys us, he needs our good, which no sane person would say. For the Prophet says: *You have no need of my goods.* Every good of ours is either himself or from him; therefore he does not enjoy us, but uses us. And if he neither enjoys us nor uses us, I do not see how he loves us." "Yet he does not use us as we use other things. For we refer the things we use to this end — that we may fully enjoy God's goodness. But God refers our use [of us] to his own goodness: for he has mercy on us for the sake of his own goodness, while we have mercy on each other for the sake of his goodness. He has mercy on us that we may fully enjoy him; we have mercy on each other that we may enjoy him. For when we have mercy on someone and help him, we do so for his benefit and with an eye to it, but our own benefit follows, since God does not leave without reward the mercy we show to others. This reward is supreme: that we enjoy Him himself." Again: "Because he is good, we are; and insofar as we are, we are good. Further, because he is also just, we are not *with impunity* evil; and insofar as we are evil, so much the less we even are. That use, therefore, by which God uses us is referred not to his utility but to ours — and only to his goodness."

Here it must be considered whether the virtues are to be used or enjoyed. Some think they are to be used and not enjoyed, and they confirm this by the authority of Augustine, who — as was noted above — says that nothing is to be enjoyed except the Trinity, that is, the highest and unchangeable good. They say further that the virtues are not to be enjoyed, because they are not to be loved for their own sake but for the sake of eternal beatitude: for that which is to be enjoyed is to be loved for its own sake. And they prove by the authority of Augustine that the virtues are not to be loved for their own sake but only on account of beatitude. For in Book XIII *On the Trinity*, against certain persons, he says: "Perhaps the virtues, which we love only for beatitude's sake, dare to persuade us that we do not love beatitude itself. If they do this, we certainly cease to love them too, once we cease to love that for the sake of which alone we loved them." Look: by these words Augustine seems to show that the virtues are to be loved not for their own sake but only for the sake of beatitude. And if this is so, then they are not to be enjoyed. Others, however, think the contrary: namely, that the virtues are to be enjoyed, because they are to be sought and loved for their own sake. And they confirm this by the authority of Ambrose, who says, on the verse of the Epistle to the Galatians, *But the fruit of the Spirit is charity, joy, peace, patience, etc.*: "He does not here name them *works* but *fruits*, because they are to be sought for their own sake." And if they are to be sought for their own sake, then they are to be loved for their own sake. We, desiring to remove this apparent conflict of authorities, say that the virtues are to be sought and loved both for their own sake *and* for the sake of beatitude alone. They are to be loved for their own sake because they delight their possessors with a sincere and holy delight and give birth in them to spiritual joy. Yet one must not stop here but go further. Here let the step of love not pause, nor here let the term of love be set; but let it be referred to that highest good, to which alone one must absolutely cleave — for that alone is to be loved for its own sake, and beyond it nothing is to be sought: for that is the supreme end. So Augustine says that we love the virtues "for beatitude's sake alone" — not as though we do not love them for their own sake, but because the very fact that we love them we refer to that highest good, to which alone one must cleave, in which one must remain, and in which the end of one's gladness must be placed. Therefore the virtues are not to be enjoyed. But someone may say: *To enjoy* is to cleave in love to some thing *for its own sake*, as was said above; if, therefore, the virtues are to be loved for their own sake, then they are to be enjoyed. To this we say: in that description, where one reads *for its own sake*, one must understand *only insofar as one loves it for its own sake alone*, so that it not be referred to anything else but the end be set in it — as Augustine shows above when he says: "If you cleave and remain, setting the end of your joy in it, then you are truly and properly said to enjoy; which is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, the highest and unchangeable good." Therefore the virtues are to be *used*, and through them we are to *enjoy* the highest good. The same we say of the good will. Whence Augustine in Book X *On the Trinity*: "The will is that by which we enjoy"; and so we enjoy through the virtues, but not the virtues themselves — unless perhaps some virtue is God, as is charity, of which we shall treat later.

*Epilogue.* The sum, therefore, of what has been said — now that we have treated of things specifically — is this: some things are to be enjoyed, some are to be used, and some both enjoy and use. Among those which are to be used, there are also some *through which we enjoy* — namely, the virtues and the powers of the mind, which are natural goods. Concerning all of these, before we treat of signs, something must be said — and first of the things which are to be enjoyed, namely, the holy and undivided Trinity.

## Notes

- **Chunk scope.** This chunk contains the three chapters of Distinction 1 from Peter Lombard's *Sentences* (Bk. I, d. 1, cc. 1–3), as reprinted at the head of Bonaventure's commentary in the Quaracchi edition (pp. 26–28). The three chapters cover: (1) Lombard's general statement that all doctrine is either of things or of signs; (2) the Augustinian distinction among things to be enjoyed, things to be used, and those that both enjoy and use; and (3) the reconciliation of Augustine's two senses of *uti / frui* (the one from *De doctrina christiana*, the other from *De Trinitate* X), followed by the Epilogue that frames the rest of Distinction 1.

- **Nature of the chunk.** This is *Lombard's text*, not Bonaventure's commentary. Bonaventure's own treatment of Dist. 1 appears in the companion chunks `bon-sent-I-d1-a1-q1` through `bon-sent-I-d1-a3-q2` and the `bon-sent-I-d1-dubia` file. The Littera Magistri is included at the head of every distinction so that readers can see the text Bonaventure is commenting on.

- **Sources cited by Lombard.** Augustine, *On Christian Doctrine* I.2.2, 3.3, 4.4, 22.20–21, 31–33, 34, 37; Augustine, *On the Trinity* X.10.13, 11.17, 13.22, and XIII.8.11; Ambrose on Galatians 5:22. The debate on the virtues in Ch. 3 reproduces Augustine's *De Trinitate* XIII.8.11 verbatim.

- **Key terms** (per project glossary, shared with the accompanying Bonaventure chunks): *frui / uti / fruibile / utibile* → enjoy / use / enjoyable / usable; *abuti* → misuse / abuse; *cleave* is used consistently for *inhaerere*; *beatus* → blessed; *per speciem* → through the species (i.e. in direct vision, as opposed to in hope).

- **Transcription status.** Lightly cleaned from the raw OCR (`raw/bonaventure_vol1_raw.txt`, lines 12780–13213). Major typos fixed; word-level verification against the PDF is still pending. The English translation is a first-pass rendering of Lombard's text and is intended to give readers unfamiliar with Latin access to the text that Bonaventure is commenting on throughout the remaining chunks of Dist. 1.
