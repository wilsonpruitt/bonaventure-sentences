# I Sent., d. 1, Littera Magistri (Caps. I–III)
## *Text of the Master of the Sentences, Distinction I*

**Peter Lombard, *Sententiae in IV Libris Distinctae*, Book I, Dist. 1, Chapters 1–3.** This is Lombard's own text (not Bonaventure's commentary), printed at the head of Bonaventure's commentary in the Quaracchi edition. The three chapters are drawn almost entirely from Augustine, *De doctrina christiana* I.2–4, 22, 31–37 and *De Trinitate* X.10–11, XIII.8.

## Latin

<!-- page 26 -->

### LIBER PRIMUS SENTENTIARUM

### *De Dei unitate et trinitate.*

### DISTINCTIO I.

#### Cap. I.

*Omnis doctrina est de rebus vel de signis.*

Veteris ac novae Legis continentiam diligenti indagine etiam atque etiam considerantibus nobis, praevia Dei gratia, innotuit, sacrae paginae tractatum circa res vel signa praecipue versari. Ut enim egregius doctor Augustinus ait in libro *de Doctrina christiana*[^1]: «Omnis doctrina vel rerum est, vel signorum. Sed res etiam per signa discuntur. Proprie autem hic res appellantur, quae non ad significandum aliquid adhibentur; signa vero, quorum usus est in significando». Eorum autem aliqua sunt, quorum omnis usus est in significando, non in iustificando, id est[^2], quibus non utimur nisi aliquid significandi gratia, ut aliqua Sacramenta legalia; alia, quae non solum significant, sed conferunt quod intus adiuvet, sicut evangelica Sacramenta. «Ex quo aperte intelligitur, quae hic appellentur signa, res illae videlicet, quae ad significandum aliquid adhibentur. Omne igitur signum etiam res aliqua est. Quod enim nulla res est, ut in eodem Augustinus ait, omnino nihil est; non autem e converso[^3] omnis res signum est», quia non adhibetur ad significandum aliquid. Cumque his intenderit theologorum speculatio studiosa atque modesta, divinam Scripturam formam praescriptam in doctrina tenere advertet[?]. De his ergo nobis aditum ad res divinas aliquatenus intelligendas, Deo duce, aperire volentibus disserendum est; et primum de rebus, postea de signis disseremus.

#### Cap. II.

*De rebus quibus fruendum est, vel utendum, et de his quae fruuntur et utuntur.*

«Id ergo in rebus considerandum est», ut in eodem[^4] Augustinus ait, «quod res aliae sunt, quibus fruendum est, aliae, quibus utendum est, aliae, quae fruuntur et utuntur. Illae, quibus fruendum est, nos beatos faciunt; istis, quibus utendum est, tendentes ad beatitudinem adiuvamur et quasi adminiculamur, ut ad illas res, quae nos beatos faciunt, pervenire eisque inhaerere possimus. Res vero, quae fruuntur et utuntur, nos sumus, quasi inter utrasque constituti», et Angeli et Sancti[^5]. «Frui autem est amore alicui rei inhaerere propter se ipsam; uti vero id quod in usum venerit referre ad obtinendum illud, quo fruendum est; alias abuti est, non uti. Nam usus illicitus abusus vel abusio nominari debet[^6]». «Res igitur, quibus fruendum est, sunt Pater et Filius et Spiritus sanctus. Eadem tamen Trinitas quaedam summa res est communisque omnibus fruentibus ea, si tamen res dici debet et non rerum omnium causa, si tamen et causa. Non enim facile potest inveniri nomen, quod tantae excellentiae conveniat, nisi quod melius dicitur Trinitas haec unus Deus». Res autem, quibus utendum est, mundus est et in eo creata. Unde Augustinus in eodem[^7]: «Utendum est hoc mundo, non fruendum, ut invisibilia Dei per ea quae facta sunt, intellecta, conspiciantur[^8], id est, ut de temporalibus aeterna capiantur. Item in eodem: «In omnibus rebus illae tantum sunt, quibus fruendum est, quae aeternae et incommutabiles sunt; ceteris autem utendum est, ut ad illarum perfruitionem[^9] perveniatur». Unde Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate*[^10]: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus ipsis propter se voluntas delectata conquiescit; utimur vero eis quae ad aliud referimus, quo fruendum est».

#### Cap. III.

*Quid sit frui et uti.*

Notandum vero, quod idem Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate*[^11], aliter quam supra accipiens *uti* et *frui*, sic dicit: «Uti est assumere aliquid in

<!-- page 27 -->

facultatem voluntatis; frui autem est uti cum gaudio non adhuc spei, sed iam rei; ideoque omnis, qui fruitur, utitur; assumit enim aliquid in facultatem voluntatis cum fine delectationis. Non autem omnis, qui utitur, fruitur[^12]; si id quod in facultatem voluntatis assumit, non propter ipsum, sed propter aliud appetit».

Et attende, quod[^13] videtur Augustinus dicere, illos frui tantum, qui in re gaudent, non iam in spe; et ita in hac vita non videmur frui, sed tantum uti, ubi gaudemus in spe, cum supra dictum sit, frui amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se, qualiter etiam hic multi adhaerent Deo.

Haec ergo, quae sibi contradicere videntur, sic determinamus dicentes, nos et hic et in futuro frui, sed ibi proprie et perfecte et plene, ubi per speciem videbimus quo fruemur; hic autem, dum in spe ambulamus, fruimur quidem, sed non adeo plene. Unde Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate*[^14]: «Fruimur cognitis, in quibus ipsis voluntas propter se delectata conquiescit». Idem in libro *de Doctrina christiana*[^15] ait: «Angeli illo fruentes iam beati sunt, quo et nos frui desideramus; et quantum in hac vita iam fruimur vel per speculum vel in aenigmate, tanto nostram peregrinationem et tolerabilius sustinemus et ardentius finire cupimus».

Potest etiam dici, quod qui fruitur etiam in hac vita non tantum habet gaudium spei, sed etiam rei, quia iam delectatur in eo quod diligit, et ita iam rem aliquatenus tenet. Constat ergo, quia[^16] debemus Deo frui, non uti. «Illo enim, ut ait Augustinus[^17], frueris, quo efficieris beatus et in quo spem ponis, ut ad id pervenias». De hoc idem ait in libro *de Doctrina christiana*[^18]: «Dicimus ea re nos frui, quam diligimus propter se, et ea re nobis fruendum esse tantum, qua efficimur beati; ceteris vero utendum». Frequenter tamen dicitur frui cum delectatione uti. Cum enim adest quod diligitur, etiam delectationem secum gerit. Si tamen per eam transieris et ad illud, ubi permanendum est, eam retuleris, uteris ea et abusive, non proprie diceris frui. Si vero inhaeseris atque permanseris, finem in ea ponens laetitiae tuae, tunc vere et proprie frui dicendus es; quod non est faciendum nisi in illa Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono.

*Utrum homo sit fruendum, an utendum.*

Cum autem homines, qui fruuntur et utuntur aliis rebus, res aliquae sint, quaeritur, «utrum frui se debeant, an uti, an utrumque». Ad quod sic respondet Augustinus in libro *de Doctrina christiana*[^19]: «Si propter se homo diligendus est, fruimur eo; si propter aliud, utimur eo: videtur autem mihi propter aliud diligendus. Quod enim propter se diligendum est, in eo constituitur beata vita, cuius etiam spes hoc tempore nos consolatur. In homine autem spes ponenda non est, quia *Maledictus* est *qui hoc facit*. Ergo si liquide advertas, nec seipso quisquam frui debet, quia non se debet diligere propter se, sed propter illud quo fruendum est». Huic autem contrarium videtur, quod Apostolus ad Philemonem[^20] loquens ait: *Ita, frater, ego te fruar in Domino*. Quod ita determinat Augustinus[^21]: «Si dixisset tantum *te fruar*, et non addidisset *in Domino*, videretur finem dilectionis ac spem constituisse in eo; sed quia illud addidit, in Domino se finem posuisse eodemque frui significavit». «Cum enim», ut idem Augustinus ait, «homine in Deo frueris, Deo potius quam homine frueris».

*Utrum Deus nobis fruatur, an utatur.*

Sed cum Deus diligat nos, ut frequenter Scriptura dicit, quae eius dilectionem erga nos multum commendat, quaerit Augustinus[^22], quomodo diligit, an ut utens, an ut fruens, et procedit ita: «Si fruitur nobis, eget bono nostro, quod nemo sanus dixerit; ait enim Propheta[^23]: *Bonorum meorum non eges*. Omne enim bonum nostrum vel ipse est, vel ab ipso est: non ergo fruitur nobis, sed utitur. Si enim nec fruitur nobis nec utitur, non invenio, quomodo diligat nos». «Neque tamen sic utitur nobis, ut nos aliis rebus. Nos enim res, quibus utimur, ad id referimus, ut Dei bonitate perfruamur; Deus vero ad suam bonitatem usum nostrum refert: ille enim miseretur nostri propter suam bonitatem, nos autem nobis invicem propter illius bonitatem; ille nostri miseretur, ut se perfruamur, nos vero invicem nostri miseremur, ut illo fruamur. Cum enim nos alicuius miseremur et alicui consulimus, ad eius quidem facimus utilitatem eamque intuemur, sed et nostra fit consequens, cum misericordiam, quam aliis impendimus, non relinquit Deus sine mercede. Haec autem merces summa est, ut ipso perfruamur[^24]». Item: «Quia bonus est, sumus, et inquantum sumus, boni sumus. Porro quia etiam iustus est, non impune mali sumus, et in quantum mali sumus, in tantum etiam

<!-- page 28 -->

minus sumus. Ille igitur usus, quo nobis utitur Deus, non ad eius, sed ad nostram utilitatem refertur, ad eius vero tantummodo bonitatem[^25]».

*Utrum virtutibus sit utendum vel fruendum.*

Hic considerandum est, utrum virtutibus sit utendum, an fruendum. Quibusdam videtur, quod eis sit utendum et non fruendum, et hoc confirmant auctoritate Augustini[^26], qui, ut praetaxatum est, dicit, non esse fruendum nisi Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono. Item dicunt, ideo non esse fruendum eis, quia propter se amandae non sunt, sed propter aeternam beatitudinem. Illud autem, quo fruendum est, propter se amandum est. Sed quod virtutes propter se amandae non sint, immo propter solam beatitudinem, probant auctoritate Augustini, qui in libro decimo tertio *de Trinitate*[^27] contra quosdam ait: «Forte virtutes, quas propter solam beatitudinem amamus, sic persuadere nobis audent, ut ipsam beatitudinem non amemus; quod si faciunt, etiam ipsas utique amare desistimus, quando illam, propter quam solam istas amavimus, non amamus». Ecce his verbis videtur Augustinus ostendere, quod virtutes non propter se, sed propter solam beatitudinem amandae sint. Quod si ita est, ergo eis fruendum non est. Aliis vero contra videtur, scilicet quod eis fruendum sit, quia[^28] propter se petendae et amandae sunt. Et hoc confirmant auctoritate Ambrosii[^29], qui ait super illum locum Epistolae ad Galatas: *Fructus autem spiritus est caritas, gaudium, pax, patientia* etc.: «Haec non nominat *opera*, sed *fructus*, quia propter se petenda sunt». Si vero propter se petenda sunt, ergo propter se amanda. Nos autem harum quae videtur[^30] auctoritatum repugnantiam de medio eximere cupientes, dicimus, quod virtutes propter se petendae et amandae sunt, et tamen propter solam beatitudinem. Propter se quidem amandae sunt, quia delectant sui possessores sincera et sancta delectatione et in eis pariunt gaudium spirituale. Verumtamen non est hic consistendum, sed ultra gradiendum. Non hic haereat dilectionis[^31] gressus, neque hic sit dilectionis terminus, sed referatur hoc ad illud summum bonum, cui soli omnino inhaerendum est, quia illud propter se tantum amandum est, et ultra illud nihil quaerendum est; illud est enim supremus finis. Ideo Augustinus dicit, quod eas diligimus propter solam beatitudinem, non quin[^32] eas propter se diligamus, sed quia id ipsum, quod eas diligimus, referimus ad illud summum bonum, cui soli inhaerendum est, et[^33] in eo permanendum finisque laetitiae ponendus; quare virtutibus non est fruendum.

*Obiectio.* Sed dicet aliquis: Frui est amore inhaerere alicui rei propter se ipsam, ut praedictum est; si ergo virtutes propter se amandae sunt, et eis fruendum est.

*Solutio.* Ad quod dicimus: in illa descriptione, ubi dicitur *propter se ipsam*, intelligendum est tantummodo, ut scilicet ametur propter se ipsam tantum, ut non referatur ad aliud, sed ibi ponatur finis, ut supra ostendit Augustinus dicens[^34]: «Si inhaeseris atque permanseris, finem ponens laetitiae tuae, tunc vere et proprie frui dicendus es. Quod non est faciendum nisi in illa Trinitate, id est, summo et incommutabili bono». Utendum est ergo virtutibus et per eas fruendum summo bono; ita et de voluntate bona dicimus. Unde Augustinus in libro decimo *de Trinitate*[^35] ait: «Voluntas est per quam fruimur»; ita et per virtutes fruimur; non eis, nisi forte aliqua virtus sit Deus, ut caritas, de qua post tractabitur[^36].

*Epilogus.* Omnium igitur, quae dicta sunt, ex quo de rebus specialiter tractavimus, haec summa est: quod aliae sunt, quibus fruendum est, aliae, quibus utendum est, aliae, quae fruuntur et utuntur; et inter eas, quibus utendum est, etiam quaedam sunt, per quas fruimur, ut virtutes et potentiae animi, quae sunt naturalia bona. De quibus omnibus, antequam de signis tractemus, agendum est, ac primum de rebus, quibus fruendum est, scilicet de sancta atque individua Trinitate.

## English

### BOOK ONE OF THE SENTENCES

### *On the unity and trinity of God.*

### DISTINCTION I.

#### Ch. 1.

*All doctrine is of things or of signs.*

In attentively considering, again and again, the content of the Old and New Law, it has become clear to us, by the prevenient grace of God, that the treatment of sacred Scripture turns chiefly upon things or signs. For, as the distinguished doctor Augustine says in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^1]: "All doctrine is either of things or of signs. But things too are learned through signs. Here, properly, those are called *things* which are not employed to signify anything; while *signs* are those whose use is in signifying." Of these latter, however, some are such that their whole use is in signifying, not in justifying — that is[^2], such as we use only for the sake of signifying something, like certain legal Sacraments; others, which not only signify but also confer that which aids inwardly, like the Sacraments of the Gospel. "From which it is plainly understood what are here called signs — namely, those things which are employed for the sake of signifying something. Every sign, therefore, is also some thing. For what is no thing, as Augustine says in the same place, is altogether nothing; not however conversely[^3] is every thing a sign," since not every thing is employed for the sake of signifying something. And when the studious and modest speculation of theologians has bent itself toward these matters, it perceives[?] that divine Scripture holds in its teaching to a prescribed form. Of these, therefore — since we, with God as our guide, wish to open some way for understanding divine things — we must treat; and first we shall treat of things, afterward of signs.

#### Ch. 2.

*Of things which are to be enjoyed, or to be used, and of those which both enjoy and use.*

"This, therefore, is to be considered among things," as in the same place[^4] Augustine says, "that some things are to be enjoyed, others to be used, and others both enjoy and use. Those which are to be enjoyed make us blessed; by those which are to be used we, tending toward beatitude, are helped and as it were sustained, so that we may come to and cleave to those things which make us blessed. The things which both enjoy and use are we ourselves, constituted as it were between the two," along with the Angels and the Saints[^5]. "Now to *enjoy* is to cleave in love to some thing for its own sake; to *use*, however, is to refer that which has come into use to obtaining what is to be enjoyed; otherwise it is to *misuse*, not to use. For an unlawful use ought to be called *abuse* or *misusage*[^6]." "The things, therefore, which are to be enjoyed are the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit. Yet the same Trinity is one certain supreme Thing, common to all who enjoy it — if indeed it ought to be called a thing and not rather the cause of all things, if indeed even *cause*. For a name cannot easily be found which befits so great an excellence, except that it is better said: this Trinity is one God." But the things which are to be used are the world and what is created in it. Whence Augustine in the same[^7]: "This world is to be used, not enjoyed, that the invisible things of God may be understood and seen through the things that have been made[^8] — that is, that from temporal things eternal things may be grasped." Likewise in the same: "Among all things, those alone are which are to be enjoyed which are eternal and unchangeable; the rest are to be used, so that through them one may arrive at the full enjoyment[^9] of those." Whence Augustine in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^10]: "We enjoy things known, in which the will, delighted for their own sake, comes to rest; we use, on the other hand, those things which we refer to something else which is to be enjoyed."

#### Ch. 3.

*What it is to enjoy and to use.*

It must be noted, however, that the same Augustine, in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^11], taking *use* and *enjoyment* otherwise than above, speaks thus: "To *use* is to take something into the power of the will;

<!-- page 27 -->

to *enjoy* is to use with the joy now no longer of hope but of the reality itself. And so everyone who enjoys uses; for he takes something into the power of the will with the end of delight. But not everyone who uses enjoys[^12], if what he takes into the power of the will he seeks not for its own sake but for something else."

And mark that[^13] Augustine seems to say that only those enjoy who rejoice in the reality, not still in hope; and so in this life we do not seem to enjoy but only to use, where we rejoice in hope — though it was said above that *to enjoy* is to cleave in love to some thing for its own sake, in which manner many do cleave even here to God.

These things, therefore, which seem to contradict one another, we so determine, saying that we enjoy both *here* and *in the future*, but *there* properly and perfectly and fully, where we shall see by the species that which we shall enjoy; *here*, however, while we walk in hope, we enjoy indeed, but not so fully. Whence Augustine in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^14]: "We enjoy things known, in which the will itself, delighted for their own sake, comes to rest." The same, in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^15], says: "The angels, enjoying that [God] whom we too desire to enjoy, are already blessed; and the more we already enjoy in this life — whether through a glass or in a riddle — by so much do we both more tolerably bear our pilgrimage and more ardently desire to end it."

It can also be said that one who enjoys, even in this life, has not only the joy of hope but also of the reality, because he is already delighted in what he loves, and so already in some measure holds the reality. It is established, therefore, that[^16] we ought to enjoy God, not to use him. "For by that," as Augustine says[^17], "you enjoy by which you are made blessed and in which you place your hope, in order to attain it." Of this same thing he says in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^18]: "We say that we *enjoy* that thing which we love for its own sake; and that that alone is to be enjoyed by which we are made blessed; the rest is to be used." Frequently, however, *to enjoy* is said to mean *to use with delight*: for when what is loved is present, it brings delight with it. If, however, you pass through that [delight] and refer it to that in which one must remain, you use it, and abusively — you cannot properly be said to enjoy. But if you cleave and remain, setting the end of your gladness in it, then you are truly and properly said to enjoy — which is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, the highest and unchangeable good.

*Whether man is to be enjoyed, or to be used.*

And since men, who enjoy and use other things, are themselves things of a sort, the question is asked, "whether they ought to enjoy themselves, or to use themselves, or both." To this Augustine replies thus, in the book *On Christian Doctrine*[^19]: "If a man is to be loved for his own sake, we enjoy him; if for the sake of another, we use him: but it seems to me that he is to be loved for the sake of another. For that which is to be loved for its own sake is that in which the blessed life consists, the hope of which also at this time consoles us. But hope is not to be placed in man, because *Cursed* is *he who does this*. Therefore, if you mark it clearly, no one ought to enjoy even himself; for one ought not to love oneself for one's own sake, but for the sake of that which is to be enjoyed." Yet against this seems to stand what the Apostle, speaking to Philemon[^20], says: *Thus, brother, may I enjoy you in the Lord*. Augustine settles it thus[^21]: "If he had said only *may I enjoy you*, and had not added *in the Lord*, he would have seemed to set the end and the hope of his love in him; but because he added the latter, he signified that he had set his end *in the Lord* and that he enjoyed him." "For," as the same Augustine says, "when you enjoy a man in God, you enjoy God rather than the man."

*Whether God enjoys us, or uses us.*

But since God loves us — as Scripture frequently says, which much commends his love toward us — Augustine asks[^22] in what way he loves: whether as one using, or as one enjoying; and he proceeds thus: "If he enjoys us, he needs our good, which no sane person would say; for the Prophet says[^23]: *You have no need of my goods*. For every good of ours is either himself or from him: therefore he does not enjoy us but uses us. For if he neither enjoys us nor uses us, I do not see how he loves us." "Yet he does not so use us as we use other things. For we refer the things we use to this end — that we may fully enjoy God's goodness; but God refers our use [of us] to his own goodness: for he has mercy on us for the sake of his own goodness, while we have mercy on one another for the sake of his goodness; he has mercy on us so that we may fully enjoy him, but we have mercy on one another so that we may enjoy him. For when we have mercy on someone and counsel him, we do it for his benefit and have an eye to it; but our own benefit also follows, since God does not leave without reward the mercy we show to others. And this reward is supreme: that we fully enjoy him himself[^24]." Likewise: "Because he is good, we are; and insofar as we are, we are good. Further, because he is also just, we are not *with impunity* evil; and the more evil we are, by so much the less also we are.

<!-- page 28 -->

That use, therefore, by which God uses us, is not referred to his own benefit but to ours, and only to his goodness[^25]."

*Whether the virtues are to be used or to be enjoyed.*

Here it is to be considered whether the virtues are to be used or to be enjoyed. To some it seems that they are to be used and not enjoyed, and they confirm this by the authority of Augustine[^26], who, as was set forth above, says that nothing is to be enjoyed except the Trinity, that is, the highest and unchangeable good. They say also that they are not to be enjoyed because they are not to be loved for their own sake, but on account of eternal beatitude; but that which is to be enjoyed is to be loved for its own sake. And that the virtues are not to be loved for their own sake but on account of beatitude alone, they prove by the authority of Augustine, who in the thirteenth book *On the Trinity*[^27] against certain persons says: "Perhaps the virtues, which we love only for the sake of beatitude, dare so to persuade us that we do not love beatitude itself; if they do this, then we certainly cease to love them as well, when we cease to love that for the sake of which alone we loved them." Behold, by these words Augustine seems to show that the virtues are to be loved not for their own sake, but for the sake of beatitude alone. And if so, then they are not to be enjoyed. To others, on the contrary, it seems that they are to be enjoyed, because[^28] they are to be sought and loved for their own sake. And this they confirm by the authority of Ambrose[^29], who says, on that passage of the Epistle to the Galatians, *But the fruit of the Spirit is charity, joy, peace, patience*, etc.: "He does not call these *works*, but *fruits*, because they are to be sought for their own sake." But if they are to be sought for their own sake, then they are also to be loved for their own sake. We, however, wishing to remove the apparent[^30] conflict of these authorities, say that the virtues are to be sought and loved for their own sake, and yet for the sake of beatitude alone. They are indeed to be loved for their own sake, because they delight their possessors with a sincere and holy delight and beget in them spiritual joy. Yet one must not stop here but go further. Let the step of love[^31] not pause here, nor be the term of love here, but let this be referred to that highest good, to which alone one must absolutely cleave, because that alone is to be loved for its own sake, and beyond it nothing is to be sought; for that is the supreme end. Therefore Augustine says that we love them for the sake of beatitude alone — not as though[^32] we do not love them for their own sake, but because the very fact that we love them, we refer to that highest good, to which alone one must cleave, and[^33] in which one must remain, and in which the end of one's gladness must be set; wherefore the virtues are not to be enjoyed.

*Objection.* But someone will say: To enjoy is to cleave in love to a thing for its own sake, as was said before; therefore if the virtues are to be loved for their own sake, they are also to be enjoyed.

*Solution.* To this we say: in that description, where it is said *for its own sake*, it is to be understood only thus, namely, that it be loved for its own sake alone, so that it not be referred to anything else, but the end be set in it — as Augustine showed above, saying[^34]: "If you cleave and remain, setting the end of your gladness in it, then you are truly and properly to be said to enjoy. Which is not to be done except in that Trinity, that is, the highest and unchangeable good." The virtues, therefore, are to be used, and through them the highest good is to be enjoyed; so we say also of the good will. Whence Augustine in the tenth book *On the Trinity*[^35] says: "The will is that by which we enjoy"; and so we enjoy through the virtues; not the virtues themselves, unless perchance some virtue be God, as is charity, of which it will be treated later[^36].

*Epilogue.* The sum, therefore, of all that has been said — since we have specifically treated of things — is this: that some are to be enjoyed, others to be used, and others both enjoy and use; and among those which are to be used, there are also some through which we enjoy, namely the virtues and the powers of the soul, which are natural goods. Of all these, before we treat of signs, we must speak — and first of those which are to be enjoyed, namely, of the holy and undivided Trinity.

## Notes

- **Chunk scope.** Three chapters of Distinction 1 from Peter Lombard's *Sentences* (Bk. I, d. 1, cc. 1–3), as reprinted at the head of Bonaventure's commentary in the Quaracchi edition (pp. 26–28). Cap. I gives Lombard's general statement that all doctrine is of things or of signs; Cap. II the Augustinian distinction among things to be enjoyed, things to be used, and those that both enjoy and use; Cap. III reconciles Augustine's two senses of *uti / frui* (the one from *De doctrina christiana*, the other from *De Trinitate* X.11), treats the question whether man, God, and the virtues fall under enjoyment or use, and closes with the Epilogue framing the rest of Distinction 1.

- **Nature of the chunk.** This is *Lombard's text*, not Bonaventure's commentary. Bonaventure's own treatment of Dist. 1 appears in the companion chunks (`bon-sent-I-d1-a1-q1` ff. and `bon-sent-I-d1-dubia`). The Littera Magistri is included at the head of every distinction so that readers can see the text Bonaventure is commenting on.

- **Sources cited by Lombard.** Augustine, *De doctrina christiana* I.2.2, 3.3, 4.4, 22.20–21, 30–33, 31.34, 32.35, 33.37; Augustine, *De Trinitate* X.10.13, 11.17, XIII.8.11; Ambrose on Galatians 5:22; Romans 1:20; Psalm 15(16):2; Jeremiah 17:5; Philemon 20; 1 Corinthians 13:12.
