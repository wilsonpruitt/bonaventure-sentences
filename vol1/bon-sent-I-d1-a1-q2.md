# I Sent., d. 1, a. 1, q. 2
## *Utrum omni creato utendum sit*

---

## Latin
<!-- page 32 -->

### Articulus I. Quaestio II. *Utrum omni creato utendum sit.*

Secundo, viso quid sit *uti* per essentiam, quaeritur de *utibili*, utrum scilicet omni creato sit utendum. Et quod sic, videtur hoc modo.

1. *Proverbiorum* decimo sexto[^1]: *Universa propter semetipsum operatus est Deus*: ergo omnia sunt ordinabilia in Deum; sed omne ordinabile in Deum contingit ordinare; sed ordinando ad Deum ordinabile recte utimur: ergo si omnia contingit ordinare, omnibus est utendum.

2. Item, ens et bonum convertuntur, sicut vult Dionysius[^2]; sed omnia sunt entia: ergo omnia sunt bona. Sed omne bonum diligendum: ergo omne creatum est diligendum; sed non est diligendum propter se: ergo propter aliud; sed hoc est uti: ergo uti contingit omni creato.

3. Item, omni virtute contingit recte uti[^3]: ergo opus omnis virtutis est rectus usus; sed usus alicuius virtutis est respectu mali poenae, ut patientiae, alicuius respectu mali culpae, ut poenitentiae: ergo malis omnibus, scilicet culpae et poenae, contingit recte uti: ergo multo fortius bonis: ergo etc.

4. Item, «Opposita nata sunt fieri circa idem[^4]»; sed omnibus contingit abuti: ergo omnibus contingit recte uti: ergo utendum est omni creato.

**Contra:**

1. Caritas est, per quam recte utimur, quia omnia ordinantur in finem per caritatem quaecumque recte ordinantur; sed sicut dicit Augustinus *de Doctrina christiana*[^5]: «Tantum quatuor diligenda sunt ex caritate et tria tantum creata, ut nos, proximus et corpus proprium»: ergo tantum tria referuntur sive ordinantur in finem per caritatem, cum non sit uti recte nisi per caritatem: ergo tantum tribus est utendum, non ergo omni creato.

2. Item, omnia, quibus utimur, subiacent nostrae voluntati; sed quaedam sunt, quae non subiacent nostrae voluntati, ut necessaria et perpetua: ergo illis non est utendum.

3. Item, omne illud, quo recte utimur, bono fine facimus; sed quaedam sunt, quae nullo bono fine possunt fieri, ut mentiri et talia, «quae mox nominata coniuncta sunt malo[^6]»: ergo talibus non est utendum.

4. Item, virtute non contingit male uti, sicut patet ex eius definitione[^7]: ergo ab oppositis malo culpae sive vitiis non contingit recte uti: ergo non omnibus aliis a Deo est utendum.

### Conclusio.

> Non omni re creata possumus uti ut instrumento neque ut habitu neque ut actu, omni tamen re uti possumus ut obiecto, sed quadrupliciter, vel operando, vel acceptando, vel tolerando, vel respuendo.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod uti aliquo dicitur quatuor modis: aut sicut instrumento, quo operamur, sicut aliquis utitur[^8] instrumento vel organo; et sic non omnibus contingit uti, utpote potentiis, quae sunt pure naturales, accipiendo *uti* proprie, sicut accipit Augustinus[^9]; aut sicut habitu, quo regulamur, utpote virtute; et sic non omnibus contingit recte uti, ut habitu vitii, quo potius contingit obliquari; aut sicut actu, quo movemur; et sic non omni actu contingit bene[^10] uti, sicut illis

<!-- page 33 -->

quae mox nominata coniuncta sunt malo; aut sicut obiecto, ad quod inclinamur; et sic omnibus contingit uti et abuti. Omnia enim possunt sumi in facultatem voluntatis ad approbandum vel respuendum; et hoc patet per Augustinum libro *octoginta trium Quaestionum*[^11]: «Utimur assumendis vel respuendis ad valetudinem, tolerandis ad patientiam, ordinandis ad iustitiam».

Item, Augustinus decimo *de Trinitate*[^12]: «Voluntas totam intelligentiam capit, dum utor omni eo quod intelligo»; sed mala intelliguntur: ergo contingit uti malis.

Aliter tamen utimur bonis, aliter malis. Ratio enim ordinationis in bonis non tantum est ex parte ordinantis, sed etiam ex parte ordinati, quia per se bona sunt utibilia et ordinabilia, et de sui natura; sed in malis ratio ordinationis non est ex parte ordinatorum, sed potius ex parte ordinantis; et ideo mala non dicuntur utibilia.

Unde nota, quod, cum *uti* sit assumere aliquid in facultatem voluntatis, hoc potest esse quadrupliciter: vel in facultatem voluntatis operantis[^13], sicut est de his quae proprie pertinent ad usum humanum; vel in facultatem voluntatis acceptantis, sicut est dilectio proximi et huiusmodi; vel in facultatem voluntatis tolerantis, sicut sunt mala poenae; vel in facultatem voluntatis respuentis, et sic assumuntur mala culpae et ordinantur. Primis duobus modis intelligitur illud: «Cuius usus bonus etc.».

**Ad opposita:**

1. Ad illud ergo quod obiicitur de caritate, quod tantum tria creata[^14] sunt diligenda; dicendum, quod diligi aliquid ex caritate hoc est ordinari in summum bonum, quod per se caritas diligit. Hoc autem contingit dupliciter: aut enim contingit ordinare quod natum est devenire in finem; et sic tantum tria creata diligenda sunt ex caritate; aut illud per quod devenire est in summum bonum; et quia hoc potest esse suo modo per omnem creaturam, omnis creatura potest diligi ex caritate, et omni creatura contingit uti.

2. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod non omnia subiacent voluntati; dicendum, quod aliquid[^15] dicitur subesse dominio voluntatis dupliciter: aut quantum ad esse ipsius rei; et sic non omnia subiacent; aut quantum ad actum voluntatis, qui est in respuendo ipsam rem vel approbando, et hoc[^16] propter se vel propter aliud; et tali modo omnia subiacent voluntati nostrae, scilicet quantum ad actum appetitionis vel respuitionis, licet non primo modo.

3. 4. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod quaedam nullo bono fine possunt fieri, similiter, quod semper vitiis abutimur; dicendum, quod obiicitur secundum viam, qua contingit uti aliquo ut actu medio; et sic non contingit[^17] uti peccato; vel sicut habitu regulante, similiter; contingit tamen eo uti sicut obiecto recte per motum contritionis et detestationis.

### Scholion

**I.** Hanc et sequentem quaestionem antiqui doctores sub una quaestione et minus accurate tractare solent. — Ostensum iam supra est, quod *uti* in sensu activo sit solius voluntatis; in secunda hac quaestione inquiritur, ad quae obiecta se extendat usus voluntatis, sive quibus rebus *uti* passive conveniat. In responsione S. Doctor primo ponit quadruplicem distinctionem circa obiecta voluntatis ordinanda et resolvit quaestionem in genere. Quoad primum membrum huius distinctionis, patet quod voluntas non possit omnia assumere in facultatem suam, ut iis utatur sicut instrumento. Nam aliquae potentiae ipsius animae non subiacent imperio voluntatis; unde vocantur pure naturales, ut potentiae vegetativae. Secundo, incidendo in specialem quaestionem, scil. quo sensu quis malis uti possit, iterum quadrupliciter distinguit modum voluntatis ordinantis, scil. operantis, acceptantis, tolerantis et respuentis. Hic usus mali ex parte ordinantis est «usus per accidens», ut bene dicit Richard. (hic q. 2. ad ult.), «eo quod ordinatum aliunde accipit ordinabilitatem, scil. a voluntate». — Circa hanc quaestionem de usu mali S. Thom. (hic loc. cit.) aliis verbis quam S. Bonaventura loquitur, sed in sententia nulla est differentia, immo in S. II. II. q. 78. a. 4. Angelicus etiam in modo loquendi cum illo convenit.

**II.** Alex. Hal., S. p. III. q. 61. m. 1. — Scot., hic q. 3. et 5. et Quodl., q. 17. — S. Thom., hic q. 2. a. 3; S. I. II. q. 16. a. 3. — B. Albert. M., hic a. 10. et 11. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 1. a. 2. ita presse sequitur S. Doctorem, ut Dionys. Carth. de ipso dicat: «Petrus, qui scripsit post Bonaventuram, ex ipso Bonaventura suam responsionem videtur sumsisse». — Richard., hic a. 1. q. 2. — Aegid. R., hic 2. princ. q. 3. — Henr. Gand., S. a. 72. q. 3. n. 11. — Durand., hic q. 4. — Dionys., hic q. 2. — Biel, hic q. 1.

## English

### Article I. Question II. *Whether every created thing is to be used.*

Secondly, having seen what *to use* is per essence, the question is asked about the *useable*, namely whether every created thing is to be used. And that it is so, is shown in this way.

1. *Proverbs* sixteen[^1]: *God has worked all things for his own sake*: therefore all things are orderable to God; but whatever is orderable to God can be ordered; but in ordering to God we rightly use the orderable: therefore if all things can be ordered, all things are to be used.

2. Likewise, being and good are convertible, as Dionysius holds[^2]; but all things are beings: therefore all things are good. But every good is to be loved: therefore every created thing is to be loved; but it is not to be loved for its own sake: therefore for the sake of another; but this is to use: therefore it can be used in the case of every created thing.

3. Likewise, every virtue can be rightly used[^3]: therefore the work of every virtue is right use; but the use of a certain virtue is with respect to the evil of penalty, as patience, of another with respect to the evil of fault, as penitence: therefore all evils, namely of fault and of penalty, can be rightly used: therefore much more strongly, goods: therefore etc.

4. Likewise, «Opposites are naturally to arise about the same thing[^4]»; but all things can be misused: therefore all things can be rightly used: therefore every created thing is to be used.

**On the contrary:**

1. Charity is that by which we rightly use, because all things are ordered to the end through charity, whatever things are rightly ordered; but as Augustine says in *On Christian Doctrine*[^5]: «Only four things are to be loved out of charity, and only three created, namely ourselves, our neighbor, and our own body»: therefore only three are referred or ordered to the end through charity, since there is no right using except through charity: therefore only three are to be used, therefore not every created thing.

2. Likewise, all things which we use are subject to our will; but there are certain things which are not subject to our will, such as necessary and perpetual things: therefore those are not to be used.

3. Likewise, everything that we rightly use, we do with a good end; but there are certain things which can be done with no good end, such as lying and the like, «which, as soon as named, are conjoined with evil[^6]»: therefore such things are not to be used.

4. Likewise, a virtue cannot be ill used, as is clear from its definition[^7]: therefore from things opposite to the evil of fault, that is, from vices, one cannot rightly use: therefore not all things other than God are to be used.

### Conclusion.

> Not every created thing can we use as an instrument, nor as a habit, nor as an act; but every thing we can use as an object, yet in fourfold manner: either by operating, or by accepting, or by tolerating, or by spurning.

**I respond:** It must be said that *to use* something is said in four modes: either as an instrument, by which we operate, just as someone uses[^8] an instrument or organ; and thus it is not possible to use all things, namely the powers which are purely natural, taking *to use* properly, as Augustine takes it[^9]; or as a habit, by which we are regulated, namely virtue; and thus it is not possible to rightly use all things, as the habit of vice, by which rather one can be turned aside; or as an act, by which we are moved; and thus it is not possible to use well[^10] every act, such as those

<!-- page 33 -->

which, as soon as named, are conjoined with evil; or as an object, to which we are inclined; and thus all things can be used and misused. For all things can be taken up into the faculty of the will for approving or spurning; and this is clear from Augustine in the book *On Eighty-Three Questions*[^11]: «We use things to-be-taken-up or to-be-spurned for health, things to-be-tolerated for patience, things to-be-ordered for justice».

Likewise, Augustine in book ten *On the Trinity*[^12]: «The will grasps the whole intellect, while I use all of that which I understand»; but evils are understood: therefore evils can be used.

Yet otherwise we use goods, and otherwise evils. For the ground of the ordering in goods is not only on the part of the one ordering, but also on the part of the thing ordered, because of themselves goods are useable and orderable, and from their own nature; but in evils the ground of ordering is not on the part of the things ordered, but rather on the part of the one ordering; and therefore evils are not called useable.

Hence note that, since *to use* is to take up something into the faculty of the will, this can be in a fourfold manner: either into the faculty of the operating will[^13], as is the case with those things which properly pertain to human use; or into the faculty of the accepting will, as is the love of neighbor and the like; or into the faculty of the tolerating will, as are the evils of penalty; or into the faculty of the spurning will, and thus evils of fault are taken up and ordered. By the first two modes is understood that saying: «Whose use is good etc.».

**To the opposing arguments:**

1. To that which is objected concerning charity, that only three created things[^14] are to be loved; it must be said that for something to be loved out of charity is for it to be ordered to the highest good, which charity loves *per se*. But this happens in two ways: either it is possible to order that which is naturally fitted to come to the end; and thus only three created things are to be loved out of charity; or that through which one comes to the highest good; and because this can be in its own way through every creature, every creature can be loved out of charity, and every creature can be used.

2. To that which is objected, that not all things are subject to the will; it must be said that something[^15] is said to be under the dominion of the will in two ways: either as to the being of the thing itself; and thus not all things are subject; or as to the act of the will, which is in spurning the thing itself or approving, and this[^16] either for its own sake or for the sake of another; and in such a way all things are subject to our will, namely as to the act of seeking-after or spurning, although not in the first way.

3. 4. To that which is objected, that certain things can be done with no good end, similarly, that we always misuse vices; it must be said that the objection proceeds along the way in which one can use something as a middle act; and thus[^17] one cannot use sin; or as a regulating habit, similarly; yet it can be used as an object rightly through the motion of contrition and detestation.

### Scholion

**I.** This and the following question the ancient doctors are accustomed to treat under one question and less accurately. — It has already been shown above, that *to use* in the active sense belongs only to the will; in this second question it is asked, to what objects the use of the will extends, or with what things *to use* in the passive sense agrees. In the response the holy Doctor first sets down a fourfold distinction concerning the objects of the will to be ordered and resolves the question in general. As to the first member of this distinction, it is clear that the will cannot take up all things into its faculty, so as to use them as an instrument. For some powers of the soul itself are not subject to the command of the will; whence they are called purely natural, such as the vegetative powers. Secondly, falling into a special question, namely in what sense one can use evils, again he distinguishes in fourfold manner the mode of the ordering will, namely operating, accepting, tolerating, and spurning. This use of evil on the part of the one ordering is a «use *per accidens*», as Richard well says (here q. 2. ad ult.), «because the ordered thing receives its orderability from elsewhere, namely from the will». — Concerning this question of the use of evil, St. Thomas (here at the cited place) speaks in other words than St. Bonaventure, but in substance there is no difference; indeed in *Summa* II–II, q. 78, a. 4, the Angelic Doctor also agrees with him even in the mode of speaking.

**II.** Alex. of Hales, *Summa* p. III, q. 61, m. 1. — Scotus, here q. 3 and 5, and *Quodl.*, q. 17. — St. Thomas, here q. 2, a. 3; *Summa* I–II, q. 16, a. 3. — Bl. Albert the Great, here a. 10 and 11. — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 1, a. 2, so closely follows the holy Doctor that Dionysius the Carthusian says of him: «Peter, who wrote after Bonaventure, seems to have taken his response from Bonaventure himself». — Richard, here a. 1, q. 2. — Giles of Rome, here 2. princ. q. 3. — Henry of Ghent, *Summa* a. 72, q. 3, n. 11. — Durandus, here q. 4. — Dionysius [the Carthusian], here q. 2. — Biel, here q. 1.
