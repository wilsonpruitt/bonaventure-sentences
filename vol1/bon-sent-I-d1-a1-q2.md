## *Utrum omni creato utendum sit*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 32 -->

### Quaestio II. *Utrum omni creato utendum sit.*

Secundo, viso quid sit *uti* per essentiam, quaeritur de *utibili*, utrum scilicet omni creato sit utendum. Et quod sic, videtur hoc modo.

1. Proverbiorum decimo sexto[^1]: «Universa propter semetipsum operatus est Deus»; ergo omnia sunt ordinabilia in Deum; sed omne ordinabile in Deum contingit ordinare; sed ordinando ad Deum ordinabile recte utimur: ergo si omnia contingit ordinare, omnibus est utendum.

2. Item, ens et bonum convertuntur, sicut vult Dionysius[^2]; sed omnia sunt entia: ergo omnia sunt bona. Sed omne bonum diligendum: ergo omne creatum est diligendum; sed non est diligendum propter se: ergo propter aliud; sed hoc est *uti*: ergo *uti* contingit omni creato.

3. Item, omni virtute contingit recte uti[^3]: ergo opus omnis virtutis est rectus usus; sed usus alicuius virtutis est respectu mali poenae, ut patientiae, alicuius respectu mali culpae, ut poenitentiae: ergo malis omnibus, scilicet culpae et poenae, contingit recte uti: ergo multo fortius bonis: ergo etc.

4. Item, «Opposita nata sunt fieri circa idem»[^4]; sed omnibus contingit abuti: ergo omnibus contingit recte uti: ergo utendum est omni creato.

**Contra:**

1. Caritas est, per quam recte utimur, quia omnia ordinantur in finem per caritatem quaecumque recte ordinantur; sed sicut dicit Augustinus *de Doctrina christiana*[^5]: «Tantum quatuor diligenda sunt ex caritate et tria tantum creata, ut nos, proximus et corpus proprium»: ergo tantum tria referuntur sive ordinantur in finem per caritatem, cum non sit uti recte nisi per caritatem: ergo tantum tribus est utendum, non ergo omni creato.

2. Item, omnia, quibus utimur, subiacent nostrae voluntati; sed quaedam sunt, quae non subiacent nostrae voluntati, ut necessaria et perpetua: ergo illis non est utendum.

3. Item, omne illud, quo recte utimur, bono fine facimus; sed quaedam sunt, quae nullo bono fine possunt fieri, ut mentiri et talia, «quae mox nominata coniuncta sunt malo»[^6]: ergo talibus non est utendum.

4. Item, virtute non contingit male uti, sicut patet ex eius definitione[^7]: ergo ab oppositis malo culpae sive vitiis non contingit recte uti: ergo non omnibus aliis a Deo est utendum.

> **Conclusio.**
>
> *Non omni re creata possumus uti ut instrumento neque ut habitu neque ut actu, omni tamen re uti possumus ut obiecto, sed quadrupliciter, vel operando, vel acceptando, vel tolerando, vel respuendo.*

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod *uti aliquo* dicitur quatuor modis: aut sicut instrumento, quo operamur, sicut aliquis utitur[^8] instrumento vel organo; et sic non omnibus contingit uti, utpote potentiis, quae sunt pure naturales, accipiendo *uti* proprie, sicut accipit Augustinus[^9]; aut sicut habitu, quo regulamur, utpote virtute; et sic non omnibus contingit recte uti, ut habitu vitii, quo potius contingit obliquari; aut sicut actu, quo movemur; et sic non omni actu contingit bene[^10] uti, sicut illis

<!-- page 33 -->

quae mox nominata coniuncta sunt malo; aut sicut obiecto, ad quod inclinamur; et sic omnibus contingit uti et abuti. Omnia enim possunt sumi in facultatem voluntatis ad approbandum vel respuendum; et hoc patet per Augustinum libro octoginta trium *Quaestionum*[^11]: «Utimur assumendis vel respuendis ad valetudinem, tolerandis ad patientiam, ordinandis ad iustitiam».

Item, Augustinus decimo *de Trinitate*[^12]: «Voluntas totam intelligentiam capit, dum utor omni eo quod intelligo»; sed mala intelliguntur: ergo contingit uti malis.

Aliter tamen utimur bonis, aliter malis. Ratio enim ordinationis in bonis non tantum est ex parte ordinantis, sed etiam ex parte ordinati, quia per se bona sunt utibilia et ordinabilia, et de sui natura; sed in malis ratio ordinationis non est ex parte ordinatorum, sed potius ex parte ordinantis; et ideo mala non dicuntur utibilia.

Unde nota, quod, cum *uti* sit assumere aliquid in facultatem voluntatis, hoc potest esse quadrupliciter: vel in facultatem voluntatis operantis[^13], sicut est de his quae proprie pertinent ad usum humanum; vel in facultatem voluntatis acceptantis, sicut est dilectio proximi et huiusmodi; vel in facultatem voluntatis tolerantis, sicut sunt mala poenae; vel in facultatem voluntatis respuentis, et sic assumuntur mala culpae et ordinantur. Primis duobus modis intelligitur illud: «Cuius usus bonus etc.».

**Ad 1.** Ad illud ergo quod obiicitur de caritate, quod tantum tria creata[^14] sunt diligenda; dicendum, quod diligi aliquid ex caritate hoc est ordinari in summum bonum, quod per se caritas diligit. Hoc autem contingit dupliciter: aut enim contingit ordinare quod natum est devenire in finem; et sic tantum tria creata diligenda sunt ex caritate; aut illud per quod devenire est in summum bonum; et quia hoc potest esse suo modo per omnem creaturam, omnis creatura potest diligi ex caritate, et omni creatura contingit uti.

**Ad 2.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod non omnia subiacent voluntati; dicendum, quod aliquid[^15] dicitur subesse dominio voluntatis dupliciter: aut quantum ad esse ipsius rei; et sic non omnia subiacent; aut quantum ad actum voluntatis, qui est in respuendo ipsam rem vel approbando, et hoc[^16] propter se vel propter aliud; et tali modo omnia subiacent voluntati nostrae, scilicet quantum ad actum appetitionis vel respuitionis, licet non primo modo.

**Ad 3, 4.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod quaedam nullo bono fine possunt fieri, similiter, quod semper vitiis abutimur; dicendum, quod obiicitur secundum viam, qua contingit uti aliquo ut actu medio; et sic non contingit[^17] uti peccato; vel sicut habitu regulante, similiter; contingit tamen eo uti sicut obiecto recte per motum contritionis et detestationis.

### Scholion

**I.** Hanc et sequentem quaestionem antiqui doctores sub una quaestione et minus accurate tractare solent. — Ostensum iam supra est, quod *uti* in sensu activo sit solius voluntatis; in secunda hac quaestione inquiritur, ad quae obiecta se extendat usus voluntatis, sive quibus rebus uti passive conveniat. In responsione S. Doctor primo ponit quadruplicem distinctionem circa obiecta voluntatis ordinanda et resolvit quaestionem in genere. Quoad primum membrum huius distinctionis, patet quod voluntas non possit omnia assumere in facultatem suam, ut iis utatur sicut instrumento. Nam aliquae potentiae ipsius animae non subiacent imperio voluntatis; unde vocantur pure naturales, ut potentiae vegetativae. Secundo, incidendo in specialem quaestionem, scil. quo sensu quis malis uti possit, iterum quadrupliciter distinguit modum voluntatis ordinantis, scil. operantis, acceptantis, tolerantis et respuentis. Hic usus mali ex parte ordinantis est «usus per accidens», ut bene dicit Richard. (hic q. 2. ad ult.), «eo quod ordinatum aliunde accipit ordinabilitatem, scil. a voluntate». — Circa hanc quaestionem de usu mali S. Thom. (hic loc. cit.) aliis verbis quam S. Bonaventura loquitur, sed in sententia nulla est differentia, immo in S. II. II. q. 78. a. 4. Angelicus etiam in modo loquendi cum illo convenit.

**II.** Alex. Hal., S. p. III. q. 61. m. 1. — Scot., hic q. 3. et 5. et *Quodl.* q. 17. — S. Thom., hic q. 2. a. 3; S. I. II. q. 16. a. 3. — B. Albert. M., hic a. 10. et 11. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 1. a. 2. ita presse sequitur S. Doctorem, ut Dionys. Carth. de ipso dicat: «Petrus, qui scripsit post Bonaventuram, ex ipso Bonaventura suam responsionem videtur sumsisse». — Richard., hic a. 1. q. 2. — Aegid. R., hic 2. princ. q. 3. — Henr. Gand., S. a. 72. q. 3. n. 11. — Durand., hic q. 4. — Dionys., hic q. 2. — Biel, hic q. 1.

### Question II. *Whether every created thing is to be used.*

In the second place, having seen what *use* is in essence, the question is asked concerning the *usable*, namely whether every created thing is to be used. That it is, seems to follow in this way.

1. Proverbs 16[^1]: "The Lord has made all things for his own sake"; therefore all things are orderable toward God; but whatever is orderable toward God can be ordered; and in ordering the orderable toward God we rightly use it: therefore if all things can be ordered, all things are to be used.

2. Likewise, being and good are convertible, as Dionysius holds[^2]; but all things are beings: therefore all things are good. But every good is to be loved: therefore every created thing is to be loved; but it is not to be loved for its own sake: therefore for the sake of something else; but this is *to use*: therefore every created thing admits of *use*.

3. Likewise, every virtue admits of right use[^3]: therefore the work of every virtue is right use; but the use of some virtue is with respect to the evil of punishment, as of patience, [the use] of another with respect to the evil of guilt, as of penitence: therefore all evils, namely of guilt and of punishment, admit of right use: therefore much more so the goods: therefore etc.

4. Likewise, "Opposites are by nature concerned with the same thing"[^4]; but all things admit of misuse: therefore all things admit of right use: therefore every created thing is to be used.

**On the contrary:**

1. Charity is that by which we rightly use, since whatever things are rightly ordered are ordered to their end through charity; but as Augustine says in *On Christian Doctrine*[^5]: "Only four things are to be loved out of charity, and only three of them are created — namely ourselves, our neighbor, and our own body": therefore only three things are referred or ordered to the end through charity, since there is no right use except through charity: therefore only three things are to be used, and so not every created thing.

2. Likewise, all things which we use are subject to our will; but there are some things which are not subject to our will, such as the necessary and the perpetual: therefore these are not to be used.

3. Likewise, everything we rightly use we do for a good end; but there are some things which can be done for no good end, such as lying and the like, "which, as soon as named, are conjoined with evil"[^6]: therefore such things are not to be used.

4. Likewise, a virtue cannot be used badly, as is clear from its definition[^7]: therefore the things opposed to a virtue, namely the evil of guilt or vices, cannot be rightly used: therefore not all things other than God are to be used.

> **Conclusion.**
>
> *Not every created thing can we use as an instrument, nor as a habit, nor as an act; yet every thing we can use as an object — but in four ways: either by operating, by accepting, by enduring, or by refusing.*

**I respond:** It must be said that *to use something* is said in four ways: either as an instrument, by which we operate, as one uses[^8] an instrument or organ — and in this way not all things admit of use, e.g. the powers which are purely natural, taking *use* properly, as Augustine takes it[^9]; or as a habit, by which we are regulated, e.g. virtue — and in this way not all things admit of right use, as in the case of the habit of vice, by which one is rather warped; or as an act, by which we are moved — and in this way not every act admits of being well[^10] used, as those

<!-- page 33 -->

which, as soon as named, are conjoined with evil; or as an object, toward which we are inclined — and in this way all things admit of *use* and *misuse*. For all things can be taken into the power of the will for approval or for refusal; and this is clear from Augustine, in the *Book of Eighty-Three Questions*[^11]: "We use things to be taken up or refused for health, things to be endured for patience, things to be ordered for justice."

Likewise, Augustine in Book X *On the Trinity*[^12]: "The will takes the whole intelligence, while I use everything I understand"; but evils are understood: therefore evils admit of *use*.

We use goods in one way, however, and evils in another. For the ground of ordering in the case of goods lies not only on the side of the one ordering, but also on the side of what is ordered, since good things are of themselves *usable* and *orderable*, and so by their own nature; but in the case of evils the ground of ordering lies not on the side of the things ordered, but rather on the side of the one ordering; and therefore evils are not called *usable*.

Hence note that, since *to use* is to take something into the power of the will, this can be in four ways: either into the power of the *operating* will[^13], as in things which properly pertain to human use; or into the power of the *accepting* will, as is the love of neighbor and the like; or into the power of the *enduring* will, as are the evils of punishment; or into the power of the *refusing* will — and so are the evils of guilt taken up and ordered. The first two ways are meant in that saying: "Whose use is good, etc."

**To 1.** To that which is objected from charity, that only three created[^14] things are to be loved: it must be said that to love something out of charity is to be ordered to the highest good, which charity loves of itself. This, however, can happen in two ways: either it can order that which is by nature fitted to come to the end — and in this way only three created things are to be loved out of charity; or that *through which* coming to the highest good occurs; and since this can be in its own way through every creature, every creature can be loved out of charity, and every creature admits of *use*.

**To 2.** To that which is objected, that not all things are subject to the will: it must be said that something[^15] is said to be subject to the dominion of the will in two ways: either with respect to the very being of the thing — and in this way not all things are subject; or with respect to the act of the will, which consists in refusing the thing itself or in approving, and this[^16] for its own sake or for the sake of another — and in this manner all things are subject to our will, namely with respect to the act of desiring or refusing, although not in the first way.

**To 3, 4.** To that which is objected, that some things can be done for no good end, and likewise that we always misuse vices: it must be said that the objection proceeds along the way in which we can use something as an act in the middle — and in this way we cannot[^17] use sin; or as a regulating habit — likewise; yet we can use it as an object rightly, through the motion of contrition and detestation.

### Scholion

**I.** The older doctors are accustomed to treat this and the following question under a single question, and less carefully. — It has already been shown above that *use* in the active sense belongs to the will alone; in this second question it is asked to which objects the use of the will extends, or to which things it is fitting that *use* be applied passively. In his reply the holy Doctor first lays down a fourfold distinction concerning the objects to be ordered by the will, and resolves the question in general. As to the first member of this distinction, it is clear that the will cannot take all things into its power so as to use them as an instrument. For some powers of the soul itself are not subject to the command of the will; whence they are called *purely natural*, like the vegetative powers. Second, falling into a special question — namely, in what sense one can use evils — he again distinguishes in fourfold fashion the mode of the ordering will, namely operating, accepting, enduring, and refusing. This use of evil on the side of the one ordering is "use by accident," as Richard rightly says (here q. 2 *ad ult.*): "for the thing ordered receives its orderability from elsewhere, namely from the will." — Concerning this question of the use of evil, Thomas (here *loc. cit.*) speaks in different words from Bonaventure, but in substance there is no difference; indeed in *Summa* II–II, q. 78, a. 4, the Angelic Doctor even agrees with him in manner of speech.

**II.** Alexander of Hales, *Summa* p. III, q. 61, m. 1. — Scotus, here q. 3 and 5, and *Quodl.* q. 17. — Thomas Aquinas, here q. 2, a. 3; *Summa* I–II, q. 16, a. 3. — Albert the Great, here a. 10 and 11. — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 1, a. 2 — who follows the holy Doctor so closely that Dionysius the Carthusian says of him: "Peter, who wrote after Bonaventure, seems to have taken his answer from Bonaventure himself." — Richard [of Mediavilla], here a. 1, q. 2. — Giles of Rome, here 2nd *princ.* q. 3. — Henry of Ghent, *Summa* a. 72, q. 3, n. 11. — Durandus, here q. 4. — Dionysius [the Carthusian], here q. 2. — Biel, here q. 1.

## Notes

- **Chunk scope.** This chunk covers Q. II in its entirety on printed pp. 32–33: the four *videtur quod* arguments on p. 32 (below the scholion for Q. I), the four *contra* arguments, the *conclusio*, the *respondeo* (which bridges pp. 32–33), the four *ad*-replies, and the two-part scholion that closes Q. II on p. 33 (before the Q. III header).

- **Title.** The printed title on p. 32 is **"Utrum omni creato utendum sit"** ("Whether every created thing is to be used"). The question of *using evils* is one strand within this question (especially fundamenta 3–4 and ad-replies 3–4), not its framing.

- **Apparatus numbering.** Quaracchi numbers footnotes restart per page (p. 32: 1–10; p. 33: 1–7). For chunk-internal consistency we have renumbered them sequentially 1–17 across the chunk, preserving content verbatim. The blockquote header at the top of the apparatus indicates the page boundary.

- **Marginal glosses stripped.** OCR shows several inline marginal-gloss bleeds (`Fundamenta`, `Ad opposita.`, `Quatuor modi utendi`, `Diversimode utimur bonis et malis`, `Alia distinctio modum utendi`, `Solutio oppositorum`) that are editorial side-notes, not Bonaventure's text. These have been omitted per the OCR-cleanup convention for marginal apparatus.

- **Key terms** (per project glossary): *uti* → use; *utibile* → usable; *abuti* → misuse; *ordinare / referre* → order / refer; *facultas voluntatis* → power of the will; *operantis / acceptantis / tolerantis / respuentis* (of the will) → operating / accepting / enduring / refusing; *mala poenae / mala culpae* → evils of punishment / evils of guilt; *usus per accidens* → "use by accident."
