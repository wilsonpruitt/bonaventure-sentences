# I Sent., d. 1, a. 1, q. 2
## *Utrum omni creato utendum sit*

---

## Latin

<!-- page 32 -->

**QUAESTIO II.** *Utrum omni creato utendum sit.*

Secundo, viso quid sit *uti* per essentiam, quaeritur de *utibili*, utrum scilicet omni creato sit utendum. Et quod sic, videtur hoc modo.

1. Proverbiorum decimo sexto[^1]: «Universum propter semetipsum operatus est Deus»; ergo omnia ordinabilia sunt in Deum; sed omne ordinabile in Deum contingit ordinare; sed ordinando in Deum ordinabile recte utimur eo: ergo omni creato contingit uti; ergo omnibus est utendum.

2. Item, ens et bonum convertuntur[^2]; sed omne bonum creatum est diligendum; sed ex diligendo est *uti*, cum semper sit ad finem: ergo omni creato est utendum.

3. Item, omni virtute contingit recte uti: ergo et e contrario vitiis malisque uti contingit, sicut aliqua virtus respicit mala poenae, ut patientia, et aliqua mala culpae, ut poenitentia; ergo contingit recte uti malis, sive poenae sive culpae: ergo etc.

4. Item, «Opposita nata sunt fieri circa idem»[^3]; sed contingit recte uti bonis creatis: ergo et malis creatis: ergo omnibus est utendum.

**Sed contra.**

1. Caritas est per quam recte utimur et diligimus; sed, sicut dicit Augustinus in *Doctrina christiana*[^4]: «Tantum quatuor diligenda sunt ex caritate, scilicet Deus, nos ipsi, proximus et corpus nostrum»; ergo tantum illa quatuor sunt diligenda, quorum tria sunt creata; ergo non omni creato est utendum.

2. Item, non omne quod subest voluntati subest dominio voluntatis quantum ad usum — ut patet de rebus naturae necessariis et huiusmodi —: ergo non omne creatum est utibile.

3. Item, Augustinus in libro de *Doctrina christiana*[^5]: «Aliqua nullo bono fine possunt fieri», ut peccata; sed quibus nullo bono fine utendum est, illis non est utendum: ergo non omnibus est utendum.

4. Item, omni malo semper abutimur, quia nullus est ipsius usus bonus; sed abusio non est usus: ergo non omni creato, quod est malum, contingit recte uti: ergo non omnibus est utendum.

**Conclusio.**

*Non omni ex creato possumus uti ut instrumento, neque ut habitu, neque ut actu medio; omnibus tamen ex iis possumus uti ut obiecto, quod approbandum vel respuendum est, vel approbando, vel acceptando, vel tolerando, vel respuendo.*

**Respondeo.** Dicendum, quod *aliquo uti* dicitur quatuor modis: aut sicut instrumento, quo mediante operamur; aut sicut habitu, quo informamur; aut sicut actu medio, quo ad ultimum tendimus, ut est habitus fidei, quo tendimus in ultimum; aut sicut obiecto, ad quod inclinamur; et sic omnibus contingit uti et abuti. Omnia enim possunt sumi in facultatem voluntatis ad approbandum vel respuendum; et hoc patet per Augustinum libro octoginta trium *Quaestionum*[^6]: «Utimur assumendis vel respuendis ad valetudinem, tolerandis ad patientiam, ordinandis ad iustitiam».

Item, Augustinus decimo *de Trinitate*[^7]: «Voluntas totam intelligentiam capit, dum utor omni eo quod intelligo»; sed mala intelliguntur: ergo contingit uti malis.

<!-- page 33 -->

Aliter tamen utimur bonis, aliter malis. Ratio enim ordinationis in bonis non tantum est ex parte ordinantis, sed etiam ex parte ordinati, quia per se bona sunt utibilia et ordinabilia, et de sui natura; sed in malis ratio ordinationis non est ex parte ordinatorum, sed potius ex parte ordinantis; et ideo mala non dicuntur utibilia.

Unde nota, quod, cum *uti* sit assumere aliquid in facultatem voluntatis, hoc potest esse quadrupliciter: vel in facultatem voluntatis operantis[^8], sicut est de his quae proprie pertinent ad usum humanum; vel in facultatem voluntatis acceptantis, sicut est dilectio proximi et huiusmodi; vel in facultatem voluntatis tolerantis, sicut sunt mala poenae; vel in facultatem voluntatis respuentis, et sic assumuntur mala culpae et ordinantur. Primis duobus modis intelligitur illud: «Cuius usus bonus etc.».

**Ad 1.** Ad illud ergo quod obiicitur de caritate, quod tantum tria creata sunt diligenda: dicendum, quod diligi aliquid ex caritate hoc est ordinari in summum bonum, quod per se caritas diligit. Hoc autem contingit dupliciter: aut enim contingit ordinare quod natum est devenire in finem — et sic tantum tria creata diligenda sunt ex caritate —; aut illud per quod devenire est in summum bonum; et quia hoc potest esse suo modo per omnem creaturam, omnis creatura potest diligi ex caritate, et omni creatura contingit uti.

**Ad 2.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod non omnia subiacent voluntati: dicendum, quod aliquid dicitur subesse dominio voluntatis dupliciter: aut quantum ad esse ipsius rei, et sic non omnia subiacent; aut quantum ad actum voluntatis, qui est in respuendo ipsam rem vel approbando, et hoc propter se vel propter aliud; et tali modo omnia subiacent voluntati nostrae, scilicet quantum ad actum appetitionis vel respuitionis, licet non primo modo.

**Ad 3–4.** Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod quaedam nullo bono fine possunt fieri, similiter, quod semper vitiis abutimur: dicendum, quod obiicitur secundum viam qua contingit uti aliquo ut actu medio, et sic non contingit uti peccato; vel sicut habitu regulante, similiter; contingit tamen eo uti sicut obiecto recte per motum contritionis et detestationis.

### Scholion

I. Hanc et sequentem quaestionem antiqui doctores sub una quaestione et minus accurate tractare solent. — Ostensum iam supra est, quod *uti* in sensu activo sit solius voluntatis; in secunda hac quaestione inquiritur, ad quae obiecta se extendat usus voluntatis, sive quibus rebus uti passive conveniat. In responsione S. Doctor primo ponit quadruplicem distinctionem circa obiecta voluntatis ordinanda et resolvit quaestionem in genere. Quoad primum membrum huius distinctionis, patet quod voluntas non possit omnia assumere in facultatem suam ut iis utatur sicut instrumento. Nam aliquae potentiae ipsius animae non subiacent imperio voluntatis; unde vocantur pure naturales, ut potentiae vegetativae. Secundo, incidendo in specialem quaestionem, scilicet quo sensu quis malis uti possit, iterum quadrupliciter distinguit modum voluntatis ordinantis, scilicet operantis, acceptantis, tolerantis et respuentis. Hic usus mali ex parte ordinantis est «usus per accidens», ut bene dicit Richardus (hic q. 2 ad ult.), «eo quod ordinatum aliunde accipit ordinabilitatem, scilicet a voluntate». — Circa hanc quaestionem de usu mali S. Thom. (hic loc. cit.) aliis verbis quam S. Bonaventura loquitur, sed in sententia nulla est differentia, immo in S. II. II. q. 78, a. 4. Angelicus etiam in modo loquendi cum illo convenit.

II. Alex. Hal., S. p. III, q. 61, m. 1. — Scot., hic q. 3 et 5, et *Quodl.* q. 17. — S. Thom., hic q. 2, a. 3; S. I. II. q. 16, a. 3. — B. Albert. M., hic a. 10 et 11. — Petr. a Tar., hic q. 1, a. 2, ita presse sequitur S. Doctorem, ut Dionys. Carth. de ipso dicat: «Petrus, qui scripsit post Bonaventuram, ex ipso Bonaventura suam responsionem videtur sumsisse». — Richard., hic a. 1, q. 2. — Aegid. R., hic 2. princ. q. 3. — Henr. Gand., S. a. 72, q. 3, n. 11. — Durand., hic q. 4. — Dionys., hic q. 2. — Biel, hic q. 1.

## English

<!-- page 32 -->

**Question 2.** *Whether every created thing is to be used.*

In the second place, having seen what *use* is in essence, the question is asked concerning the *usable*, namely whether every created thing is to be used. That it is, seems to follow in this way.

1. Proverbs 16[^1]: "The Lord has made all things for his own sake"; therefore all things are orderable toward God; but whatever is orderable toward God can be ordered; and in ordering the orderable toward God, we rightly use it: therefore every created thing admits of use; therefore every created thing is to be used.

2. Again, being and good are convertible[^2]; but every created good is to be loved; and to love something is to use it, since love always tends toward an end: therefore every created thing is to be used.

3. Again, we can rightly use every virtue: therefore, conversely, we can also use vices and evils — as some virtue bears upon the evils of punishment (as patience does) and another bears upon the evils of guilt (as repentance does); therefore we can rightly use evils, whether of punishment or of guilt: therefore etc.

4. Again, "Opposites are by nature concerned with the same thing"[^3]; but we can rightly use created goods: therefore we can rightly use created evils as well: therefore every created thing is to be used.

**On the contrary.**

1. Charity is that by which we rightly use and love; but, as Augustine says in *On Christian Doctrine*[^4]: "Only four things are to be loved out of charity — namely, God, ourselves, our neighbor, and our own body"; therefore only those four are to be loved and referred to the end; and of these, only three are created: therefore not every created thing is to be used.

2. Again, not everything that is subject to the will is subject to the will's dominion with respect to use — as is clear in the case of the necessities of nature and the like: therefore not every created thing is usable.

3. Again, Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*[^5]: "Some things can be done for no good end" — such as sins; but what cannot be done for a good end cannot be used [for a good end]: therefore not every created thing is to be used.

4. Again, we always *misuse* any evil, since it has no good use at all; but misuse is not use: therefore a created evil cannot be rightly used: therefore not every created thing is to be used.

**Conclusion.**

*Not every created thing can we use as an instrument, nor as a habit, nor as an act in the middle [of willing]; but every created thing we can use as an object — an object to be either approved or refused, by approving, by accepting, by enduring, or by refusing.*

**Reply.** I answer that *to use something* is said in four ways: either as an instrument, by whose mediation we act; or as a habit, by which we are informed; or as an act in the middle, by which we advance toward the end (as the habit of faith is that by which we tend toward our ultimate end); or as an object, toward which we are inclined. In this last sense, everything admits of both use and misuse, for all things can be taken up into the power of the will either for approval or for refusal. And this is clear from Augustine in the *Book of Eighty-Three Questions*[^6]: "We use things to be taken up or refused for health, things to be endured for patience, things to be ordered for justice."

Again, Augustine in Book X *On the Trinity*[^7]: "The will takes up the whole intelligence, while I use everything I understand"; but evils are understood: therefore we can use evils.

<!-- page 33 -->

We use goods in one way, however, and evils in another. For the reason of ordering in the case of goods lies not only on the side of the orderer but also on the side of what is ordered, since good things are by their own nature usable and orderable; whereas in the case of evils the reason of ordering does not lie on the side of the things ordered but rather on the side of the orderer — and this is why evils are not called *usable*.

Note, then, that since *to use* is to take something into the power of the will, this can happen in four ways: either into the power of the *operating* will[^8], as with the things that properly pertain to human use; or into the power of the *accepting* will, as with love of neighbor and the like; or into the power of the *enduring* will, as with the evils of punishment; or into the power of the *refusing* will — and so the evils of guilt are taken up and ordered. The first two ways are meant in the [Philosopher's] dictum: "That whose use is good, etc."

**Reply to 1.** To the objection from charity — that only three created things are to be loved —: it must be said that to love something out of charity is to refer it to the highest good, which charity itself loves for its own sake. This can happen in two ways: either by ordering that which is of itself fitted to reach the end — and in this way only three created things are to be loved out of charity —; or by ordering that *through* which one reaches the highest good. And since this latter can happen in its own way through every creature, every creature can be loved out of charity, and every creature admits of use.

**Reply to 2.** To the objection that not all things are subject to the will: it must be said that something is said to be subject to the dominion of the will in two ways: either with respect to the very being of the thing — and in this way not all things are subject —; or with respect to the act of the will, which consists in refusing or approving a thing (and this either for its own sake or for something else) — and in this way all things are subject to our will, namely with respect to the act of desiring or refusing, even though not in the former way.

**Reply to 3–4.** To the objections that some things can be done for no good end, and that we always misuse vices: it must be said that the objection is raised in terms of the way in which we can use something as an act in the middle — and in this way we cannot use sin; or as a regulating habit — similarly so. But we can still use it *as an object* rightly, through the act of contrition and detestation.

### Scholion

**I.** The older doctors are accustomed to treat this and the following question together as a single question, and less accurately. — We have already shown above that *use* in its active sense belongs to the will alone; in this second question Bonaventure asks to which objects the use of the will extends, or to what things it is fitting that use be applied *passively*. In his reply the holy Doctor first sets down a fourfold distinction regarding the objects to be ordered by the will, and resolves the question in a general way. As for the first member of the distinction, it is clear that the will cannot take all things into its power so as to use them as an instrument. For certain powers of the soul itself are not subject to the command of the will — and hence are called *purely natural*, like the vegetative powers. Second, turning to the special question of the sense in which one can use evils, he again distinguishes the will's mode of ordering in four ways: as operating, as accepting, as enduring, and as refusing. This use of evil on the side of the one ordering is a "use by accident," as Richard [of Mediavilla] rightly says (here q. 2 *ad ult.*): "for what is ordered receives its orderability from elsewhere — namely from the will." — On this question concerning the use of evil, Thomas (*loc. cit.*) speaks in different words from Bonaventure, but there is no difference in the substance; indeed in *Summa theologiae* II–II, q. 78, a. 4, the Angelic Doctor even agrees with him in manner of expression.

**II.** Alexander of Hales, *Summa* III, q. 61, m. 1. — Scotus, here q. 3 and 5, and *Quodlibet* q. 17. — Thomas Aquinas, here q. 2, a. 3; *Summa theologiae* I–II, q. 16, a. 3. — Albert the Great, here aa. 10–11. — Peter of Tarentaise, here q. 1, a. 2 — who follows Bonaventure so closely that Dionysius the Carthusian says of him: "Peter, who wrote after Bonaventure, seems to have taken his own reply from Bonaventure himself." — Richard of Mediavilla, here a. 1, q. 2. — Giles of Rome, here 2nd principium, q. 3. — Henry of Ghent, *Summa* a. 72, q. 3, n. 11. — Durandus, here q. 4. — Dionysius the Carthusian, here q. 2. — Biel, here q. 1.
