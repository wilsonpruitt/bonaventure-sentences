### Capitulum VIII. *De passione Christi quantum ad statum patientis.*

Postquam circa Verbum incarnatum considerata est unio naturarum, considerata nihilominus plenitudo charismatum, deinde consideranda est *tolerantia passionum*; circa quam considerandus est *status patientis*, *modus patiendi* et *exitus passionis*[^p248-6].

De *statu patientis* haec tenenda sunt, scilicet quod Christus assumsit non tantum humanam naturam, sed etiam *defectus* circa naturam. Assumsit enim poenalitates *corporales*, ut famem, sitim et lassitudinem; assumsit etiam *spirituales*, ut tristitiam, gemitum et timorem; nec tamen *omnes corporales* assumsit, sicut sunt defectus aegritudinum multiformium. nec *omnes spirituales*, sicut sunt ignorantia et rebelliones carnis ad spiritum; nec *qualitercumque*, quia sic necessitatem patiendi suscepit, ut nihil pati posset *invite*, nec secundum voluntatem *Deitatis* nec secundum voluntatem *rationis*, licet passio fuerit contra voluntatem *sensualitatis et carnis*, sicut exprimit oratio Salvatoris, quae dicit: *Non sicut ego volo, sed sicut tu vis*[^p248-7].

Ratio autem ad intelligentiam praedictorum haec est: quia, cum principium reparativum in reconciliando necessario habeat mediatoris officium; ideo necesse est, quod habeat convenientiam cum utroque extremorum non solum quantum ad *naturam*, verum etiam quantum ad *ea quae sunt circa naturam*. Quoniam ergo Deus est iustus et beatus, impassibilis et immortalis; homo vero lapsus est peccator et miser, passibilis et mortalis: necesse fuit, mediatorem *Dei et hominum*[^p248-8], ut hominem posset reducere ad Deum, cum Deo communicare in iustitia et beatitudine, cum homine vero in passibilitate et mortalitate; ut sic, habendo « mortalitatem transeuntem et beatitudinem permanentem », hominem reduceret de praesenti miseria ad vitam beatam; sicut econtra angelus malus, habendo immortalitatem cum miseria et iniustitia, fuit mediator faciens cadere in culpam et miseriam per suggestionem suam. Quoniam ergo Christus mediator debuit habere innocentiam et beatitudinem fruitionis cum mortalitate et passibilitate; hinc est, quod simul debuit esse *viator* et *comprehensor*. — De omni enim statu aliquid habuit in se, secundum quod dicitur[^p249-1] assumsisse de statu *innocentiae* peccati immunitatem, de statu *naturae lapsae* mortalitatem, de statu vero *gloriae* beatitudinem fruitionis perfectae.

<!-- page 249 -->

Rursus, quoniam poenalitates vitiosae, sicut sunt illa quatuor propter peccatum originale inflicta, scilicet ignorantia, infirmitas, malitia et concupiscentia[^p249-2], stare non possunt cum perfectissima innocentia; hinc est, quod has nec assumere debuit nec assumsit. — Quia vero poenae, quae sunt *exercitativae* virtutis perfectae et *testificativae* humanitatis verae, non simulatae, potissime illae sunt, quae respiciunt naturam in communi, sicut fames et sitis in absentia alimenti, tristitia et timor in praesentia nocumenti; hinc est, quod illas debuit assumere et assumsit.

Postremo, quia nullus innocens debet invitus aliquam poenam sustinere[^p249-3], quia hoc esset contra ordinem divinae iustitiae; nullus etiam mortalis vult mori et pati secundum *appetitum naturae*, quae naturaliter refugit mortem: hinc est, quod Christus habere debuit huiusmodi poenalitates, sic tamen, ut nihil posset pati *invite secundum rationem*, non solum propter beatitudinem et Deitatem omnipotentem sibi unitam, per quam poterat repellere omnia; sed etiam propter perfectissimam innocentiam, quae[^p249-4] secundum ordinem naturalis iustitiae nihil permittitur pati invite; sic etiam pateretur, ut tamen hoc esset *contra naturalem inclinationem* et appetitum naturae, qui est in sensualitate et carne. — Et hinc est, quod Christus, orans secundum rationem, voluntatem *carnis* exprimebat, qua passionem refugiebat, cum dicebat: *Transeat a me calix iste*[^p249-5]; voluntatem tamen *rationis* voluntati Patris conformabat et appetitui carnis praeponebat, cum dicebat: *Non mea voluntas, sed tua fiat*. — Et sic una voluntas non erat alteri contraria: quia « secundum voluntatem *divinam* quod iustum erat voluit, secundum voluntatem *rationis* iustitiae consensit, sed secundum *voluntatem carnis* poenam recusavit, sed tamen iustitiam non accusavit. Et sic unaquaeque voluntas quod suum erat operabatur et quod ad se pertinebat sequebatur: voluntas *divina* iustitiam, voluntas *rationalis* obedientiam, voluntas *carnis* naturam[^p249-6] »; ac per hoc non erat in Christo colluctatio et pugna, sed pacata ordinatio et tranquillitas ordinata.

## English

### Chapter VIII. *On the passion of Christ as regards the state of the sufferer.*

After the union of the natures has been considered concerning the incarnate Word, and the fullness of the charisms considered no less, next there is to be considered the *endurance of the sufferings*; and concerning this there is to be considered the *state of the sufferer*, the *mode of suffering* and the *outcome of the passion*[^p248-6].

Concerning the *state of the sufferer* these things are to be held, namely that Christ assumed not only human nature, but also the *defects* about the nature. For he assumed *bodily* penalties, such as hunger, thirst and weariness; he assumed also *spiritual* ones, such as sadness, groaning and fear; nor yet did he assume *all bodily* ones, such as are the defects of manifold sicknesses. nor *all spiritual* ones, such as are ignorance and the rebellions of the flesh against the spirit; nor *in any manner whatsoever*, since he took upon himself the necessity of suffering in such a way that he could suffer nothing *unwillingly*, neither according to the will of the *Deity* nor according to the will of *reason*, although the passion was against the will of the *sensuality and of the flesh*, as the prayer of the Saviour expresses, which says: *Not as I will, but as thou wilt*[^p248-7].

Now the reason for the understanding of the foresaid is this: since, seeing that the reparative principle in reconciling necessarily has the office of a mediator, it is therefore necessary that it have a fittingness with both of the extremes not only as regards *nature*, but also as regards *those things which are about nature*. Since therefore God is just and blessed, impassible and immortal, while man, fallen, is a sinner and wretched, passible and mortal, it was necessary that there be a mediator *of God and of men*[^p248-8], so that he might be able to lead man back to God, having in common with God justice and beatitude, but with man passibility and mortality; so that thus, having « a passing mortality and an abiding beatitude », he might lead man back from the present misery to the blessed life; just as, on the contrary, the evil angel, having immortality together with misery and injustice, was a mediator making him fall into fault and misery through his own suggestion. Since therefore Christ the mediator had to have innocence and the beatitude of fruition together with mortality and passibility, hence it is that he had to be at once *wayfarer* and *comprehensor*. — For of every state he had something in himself, according to which he is said[^p249-1] to have assumed from the state of *innocence* immunity from sin, from the state of *fallen nature* mortality, but from the state of *glory* the beatitude of perfect fruition.

Again, since vicious penalties, such as are those four inflicted on account of original sin, namely ignorance, infirmity, malice and concupiscence[^p249-2], cannot stand together with a most perfect innocence, hence it is that these he neither had to assume nor did assume. — But since those penalties which are *exercitative* of perfect virtue and *testificative* of a true, not simulated, humanity are chiefly those which regard nature in common, such as hunger and thirst in the absence of nourishment, sadness and fear in the presence of harm, hence it is that those he had to assume and did assume.

Lastly, since no innocent person ought unwillingly to undergo any penalty[^p249-3], because this would be against the order of divine justice; and since no mortal wills to die and to suffer according to the *appetite of nature*, which naturally flees death: hence it is that Christ had to have penalties of this sort, yet in such a way that he could suffer nothing *unwillingly according to reason*, not only on account of the beatitude and the omnipotent Deity united to him, through which he was able to repel all things; but also on account of that most perfect innocence, which[^p249-4] according to the order of natural justice is permitted to suffer nothing unwillingly; so also he would suffer, yet in such a way that this was *against the natural inclination* and the appetite of nature, which is in the sensuality and the flesh. — And hence it is that Christ, praying according to reason, expressed the will of the *flesh*, by which he fled the passion, when he said: *Let this chalice pass from me*[^p249-5]; yet he conformed the will of *reason* to the will of the Father and set it before the appetite of the flesh, when he said: *Not my will, but thine be done*. — And thus the one will was not contrary to the other: since « according to the *divine* will he willed what was just, according to the will of *reason* he consented to justice, but according to the *will of the flesh* he refused the penalty, yet nevertheless did not accuse justice. And thus each will did what was its own and followed what pertained to itself: the *divine* will justice, the *rational* will obedience, the will *of the flesh* nature[^p249-6] »; and through this there was in Christ no wrestling and struggle, but a peaceful ordering and an ordered tranquillity.
