# Quaestiones disputatae de mysterio Trinitatis — Quaestio II, Articulus I
## *Utrum divinum esse sit summe unum.*

---

## Latin
<!-- page 59 -->

### Quaestio II. *Utrum possit simul stare trinitas personarum cum naturae unitate.*

Supposito, quod Deum esse trinum sit verum credibile, quaeritur consequenter, utrum simul possit stare trinitas personarum cum naturae unitate[^p59-1]. Et circa hoc duo quaeruntur.

Primum est, utrum divinum *esse* sit summe unum.

Secundo quaeritur, utrum summa unitas naturae possit esse cum trinitate personarum.

### Articulus I. *Utrum divinum esse sit summe unum.*

Circa primum sic proceditur et ostenditur, quod divinum *esse* sit summe unum. Et quod ita sit, satis intonat *fides* et *Scriptura divina*, Exodi vigesimo[^p59-2]: *Deus tuus, Deus unus est*; et Deuteronomii trigesimo secundo: *Videte ergo, quod ego sim solus, et non sit alius Deus praeter me*; et David in Psalmo: *Non erit in te Deus recens, neque adorabis Deum alienum*; et hoc satis praedicat Scriptura divina. — Et hoc ipsum intentandum est *rationibus* demonstrare. Omnium enim mens ratione utentium supponit, Deum esse, quo maius et melius nihil excogitari possit, ac per hoc excellentissimum *natura* et *potestate*, *sapientia* et *bonitate*, *influentia* et *causalitate*. Si enim in aliquo horum pateretur defectum, iam non esset summus, ac per hoc non esset Deus. Supponantur ergo ista sex sicut vera et certissima.

1. Ex parte *sublimitatis naturae* arguitur sic. Sublimius est quod omnibus superfertur, quam quod non; ergo si omnibus superferri non potest competere nisi uni[^p59-3], impossibile est, Deum esse nisi unum, immo etiam nec intelligere. Nam si plures intelliguntur, ponitur ex hoc, quod idem ipse superferatur alii, et e converso; ergo si uterque est Deus, neuter est Deus: aut ergo Deus non est, aut si est, unus est.

2. Ex parte *omnipotentiae* sic. Omnipotens vere est non solum qui omnia potest, sed etiam a quo est omne *posse*; sed omnia posse et omnem potentiam causare non potest nisi uni soli competere — si enim pluribus competeret, neutri conveniret — si ergo omnipotentia convenit Deo, impossibile est, Deum esse nisi unum, immo etiam nec intelligere: quia, si uterque est omnipotens, uterque potest facere, ut nihil possit omne aliud *posse*: ergo si uterque est omnipotens, neuter est omnipotens[^p59-4].

3. Ex parte *summae sapientiae* arguitur sic. Summe sapientis est omnia nosse[^p59-5]: si ergo plures essent dii, aut uterque alterum *ignoraret*, et ita neuter esset summe sapiens, immo vere insipiens; aut *nosset*, et hoc vel per *speciem*, vel per *essentiam* vel per *se ipsum*. Sed si per *speciem* vel per *essentiam*, aliquid reciperet ab alio et informaretur; si per *se ipsum*, esset illius principium, ac per hoc aut ipse, aut ille non esset Deus. Ergo summa sapientia non permittit, plures deos esse, immo etiam nec aestimare, quia, si plures cogitantur, aut habent &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;*ndem* sapientiam, aut *diversas*. Si *diversas*: ergo vel altera superfluit, vel utraque imperfecta est; si *eandem*: cum unaquaeque sit summa sapientia ergo non habebunt iam diversam naturam: ergo iam non sunt duo.

4. Ex parte *summae bonitatis* sic. Summum bonum est summe amandum et propter se: si ergo plura sunt summe bona, ergo aut unum *amat* alterum propter se, aut *non*. Si *non*: ergo neutrum est bonum; si *sic*: ergo unum beatificat alterum: ergo

<!-- page 60 -->

neutrum est summum: ergo impossibile est, quod sit summum bonum multiplicatum; immo etiam hoc est contra intellectum, quia utrumque aut est *maius* bonum, aut non; si *non*: ergo alterum superfluit; si *sic*: ergo neutrum est summum. Ergo si intelliguntur esse plura summe bona, intelliguntur non esse summe bona[^p60-1].

5. Ex parte *influentiae* arguitur sic. Deus influit in omnes res perfectissime: ergo dat *totum* esse, non partem[^p60-2]; sed si essent plures dii, aut unus nihil daret, et ita non esset Deus; aut uterque partem, aut nihil daret, et ita neuter esset Deus; aut uterque idem daret, et tunc uterque simul esset in eodem per essentiam et potentiam et praesentiam; et quia hoc sequitur ad summam influentiam: ergo vel unus esset in altero, vel uterque circumscriptus, et ita neuter esset Deus.

6. Ex parte *summae causalitatis* arguitur sic. Deus est causa prima, ultima et perfectissima, ac per hoc causa, in qua status est per omnem modum, et secundum rationem *efficiendi*, *exemplandi* et *finiendi*[^p60-3]: ergo si plures dii essent, uterque ad alterum reduceretur: ergo neuter esset primus, ultimus et perfectissimus, ac per hoc neuter esset Deus.

7. Ex parte *omnium praedictorum* sic arguitur: quia, si plures dii essent, aut omnino *convenirent* in omnibus conditionibus praedictis, aut omnino *differrent*, aut *partim convenirent* et *partim differrent*. Si *omnino convenirent*: ergo non essent plures dii; si *omnino differrent*: ergo si unus esset Deus, alter non esset Deus; si vero *partim sic* et *partim non*: ergo in quolibet esset aliquid et aliquid: ergo neuter simplex, ac per hoc neuter Deus. — Ex his arguitur, quod divinum *esse* non potest multiplicari nec *re* nec *aestimatione*.

*Ad oppositum* arguitur primo contra rationes praedictas:

1. Quoniam non videntur *verum* concludere. Cum enim Deum esse unum sit articulus, in quo erraverunt pagani, idololatrae et infideles et haeretici, qui omnes habent usum rationis; non solum potest cogitari, sed etiam aestimari et credi, quod plures sint dii; aut si non potest contrarium aestimari, non est articulus fidei.

2. Item, non videntur *necessario* concludere: quoniam eisdem rationibus potest probari, quod non sint in divino *esse* plures personae, sicut patet pertractanti, sive ex parte *sublimitatis naturae*, sive *potentiae*, sive *sapientiae*, sive *bonitatis*, sive *influentiae*, sive *causalitatis*. Quodsi rationes procedant ex eisdem mediis ad concludendum falsum, non ergo necessario concludunt.

3. Item, si ex praedictis rationibus necessario convincitur et probatur, *Deum esse unum*; cum « fides non habeat meritum, cui humana ratio praebet experimentum[^p60-4] »: ergo meritum fidei evacuari videtur: ergo aut erunt fragiles, aut inutiles.

Ex se ipsis obiicitur contra ipsas:

4. Et primo ex *sublimitate*. Maioris sublimitatis est superferri rebus similis naturae quam dissimilis, et rebus magnis quam parvis: si ergo creatura non est consimilis naturae cum Deo et comparatione Dei quasi nihil est, non intelligitur Deus *summus* et *excellentissimus*, si superfertur solum *esse* creato. Ad divinam igitur videtur spectare excellentiam, ponere plures naturas increatas, et iterum unam supra ceteras.

5. Ex parte *omnipotentiae* sic. « Perfectae potestatis est, ut dicit Hilarius[^p60-5], id naturam posse facientis, quod potest significare sermo dicentis »; et rursum, ratio dictat, quod tanta virtus in tantum, et maior in maius, et maxima in maximum; sed Deus omnipotentissimus est: ergo cum nos possimus significare, plures deos esse, potest Deus facere, quod sint; et cum possit minus facere esse, potest maius et maximum: ergo potest facere aliud a se tantum, quantus est ipse: consonat ergo omnipotentiae, plures deos esse.

6. Ex parte *sapientiae* sic: quia competit summae sapientiae aliud a se nosse, et tanto perfectior est sapientia, quanto perfectius est cognoscibile: si ergo nihil est perfectum omnino nisi[^p60-6] ens, quod est Deus, et divina sapientia novit aeternaliter aliud a se, et hoc cognitione perfectissima; videtur, quod aeternaliter fuerit aliud a Deo, quod tamen esset Deus: ergo divina sapientia non privat, sed ponit plures divinas essentias.

7. Ex parte *bonitatis* arguitur sic. « Bonum est maxime diffusivum », et « plura bona paucioribus sunt eligenda[^p60-7] »; utraque harum est per se vera; sed summa diffusio divini *esse* non potest esse in creatura: est ergo in aliam naturam increatam, qua posita, melior est tota divina essentia, propter hoc quod erunt plura bona. Ergo si omne melius est Deo attribuendum ratione summae bonitatis, videtur, quod summa bonitas non excludat, sed exigat plures divinas naturas.

8. Ex parte *influentiae summae* sic. Perfectio influentiae exigit necessario, quod influentia non sit

<!-- page 61 -->

sine divina essentia, praesentia et potentia: ergo pari ratione multiplicari non potest sine multiplicatione essentiae et potentiae et praesentiae: ergo cum sint multae influentiae divinae, multae sunt et essentiae.

9. Ex parte *causalitatis* sic. Causa prima est perfectissima, est prima et immediata, ergo et propria; sed multiplicato effectu, necesse est, multiplicari causam proximam et propriam[^p61-1]: ergo necesse est, quod multiplicatio ponatur in causa prima, cum multitudo ponatur et sit in effectibus causatis ab ipsa.

10. Item, ex omnibus obiicitur contra omnes: quia perfectissima sublimitas in *natura* et *potentia*, *sapientia* et *bonitate*, *influentia* et *causalitate* est ratio ponendi multitudinem in effectibus causatis: ergo si idem non est causa oppositorum[^p61-2], videtur, quod potius ducant ad ponendum multitudinem quam unitatem ex parte efficientis, et sic potius esse ad oppositum quam ad propositum.

**CONCLUSIO.**

*Deum esse unum est verum non tantum credibile, sed etiam intelligibile.*

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod hoc verum, *Deum esse unum*, est verum non tantum *credibile*, verum etiam *intelligibile*; quoniam est necessarium et certum non solum ex *testimonio Scripturae* et *illustratione gratiae*, quod reperitur in fide; sed etiam certum est *ex se* et *ex testimonio creaturae*[^p61-3]. — *Ex se* ergo: quoniam divinum *esse* propter singularem sublimitatem et sublimem singularitatem omnimodam habet unitatem. Cum enim Deus omnem perfectionem in se habeat, et hoc in summo termino et excellentissimo, non solum ex *sublimitate naturae* et *sapientiae*, *potestatis* et *bonitatis* et *influentiae* et *causalitatis* convincitur esse unus, immo ex *omnibus suis conditionibus* et proprietatibus nobilibus, quae sibi attribuuntur in summo. Unde omnes conditiones attestantur unitati essentiae summae.

Ex *testimonio* etiam *creaturae*: quoniam omnis creatura, sicut habet naturalem *bonitatem*, sic et convincitur habere *unitatem*. « Nihil enim potest esse, nisi sit unum », ut dicit Boethius[^p61-4] et Augustinus, et docet sensus et intellectus. Sicut ergo omnis creatura per suam *bonitatem* clamat, in Deo esse veram bonitatem et summam; sic per suam *unitatem* clamat, omnium causam esse in se unam. — Nec huic testimonio obviat rerum *diversitas*. Omnis enim rerum diversitas intra unam universitatem est comprehensa, quae in se quidem est finita et limitata et perfecta[^p61-5]. Hoc autem non esset, nisi illa pluralitas reduceretur ad aliquid, in quo esset status; ac per hoc necessarium est, omnia reduci ad unum finem ultimum et unum principium primum, alioquin esset abire in infinitum. Testificatur igitur ipsa rerum universitas, Deum esse unum; unde, sicut unius circumferentiae impossibile est esse nec rationabiliter excogitare nisi unum centrum, a quo fluunt lineae et ad quod reducuntur sicut ad terminum; sic in uno universo non est ponere nec intelligere nisi unum Deum et solum.

Est igitur hoc necessarium et notum, et adeo notum, quod nullus dubitat de hoc habens rationis usum, si sciat, « quid est quod per nomen dicitur[^p61-6] ». Si enim per hoc nomen *Deus* significatur omnium rerum principium primum et summum; cum in hoc claudatur universitas omnis, qui hoc intelligit per consequens dicit, Deum esse unum. Unde sicut principia sunt per se nota, quia statim sciuntur per terminorum notitiam[^p61-7]; sic omni intelligenti terminorum significatum indubitanter certum est, Deum esse unum.

Multi tamen erraverunt circa deorum numerum; ignorantia enim significati peperit deceptionem circa suppositum, et hoc introduxit haeresim de pluralitate deorum. Quia enim homines sunt opinati, illud esse Deum, quod superexcedit humanum *posse*, aut humanum *scire*, et hoc viderunt posse daemones; ideo crediderunt, eos esse deos; et erraverunt et excaecati sunt et incurvati ad idolorum cultum; quod quidem est absurdissimum, nec mirum, quia « parvus error in principio magnus est in fine[^p61-8] ». Ab hoc errore eripit philosophia, sed multo melius fides christiana; quae communiter in hoc concurrunt, quod divinum *esse* sit summe unum. — Unde concedendae sunt rationes, quae hoc ostendunt, quia verum concludunt et per medium necessarium.

1. Ad illud quod obiicitur de conclusionis falsitate, per hoc quod plures sunt opinati, plures esse

<!-- page 62 -->

deos; iam patet responsio: quia aliter utuntur supposito et significato huius termini *Deus*, quam habet eius institutio; et ideo non est ibi contradictio. Unde sicut hoc nomen *Deus* secundum propriam institutionem non habet plurale, licet habeat, secundum quod accipitur *nuncupative*[^p62-1]; sic et in proposito intelligendum est; et quia propter mentis excaecationem falsum frequenter accipiebatur pro vero, ideo fuit necessaria fidei illuminatio. — Posset tamen dici, quod *Deum esse unum* habet probari per rationem, in quantum eius unitas consideratur absolute; sed necessaria est fidei illuminatio, in quantum comparatur ad personarum pluralitatem.

2. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod similiter possent concludere de personis; dicendum, quod non est simile: quia in omnibus personis una est natura et potentia et sapientia et bonitas et influentia et causalitas; non sic autem esset, si essent diversae naturae divinae; et ideo nihil repugnant iis plures personae.

3. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod evacuatur fidei meritum; dicendum, quod hoc verum esset, si solum propter rationes huic vero assentiretur; nunc autem, dum non assentitur propter rationes principaliter, sed propter ipsam veritatem, ideo potest habere meritum propter fidelem assensum. Nihil enim prohibet, secundum alium et alium respectum et considerationem idem esse *scitum* et *creditum*[^p62-2].

4. Ad illud quod obiicitur de *sublimitate* respectu magnorum et consimilium; dicendum, quod illud haberet locum, si divinae sublimitati accresceret aliquid ex aliorum subiectione; hoc autem non habet veritatem, quia ita sublimis est Deus in se ipso et solus existens, sicut si dominaretur mille mundis. — *Praeterea*, hoc non esset ei ad sublimitatem, si aliquid consimilis naturae haberet conditionem servilem; unde etsi talis sublimitas aliquo modo videatur facere ad exaltationem, tamen secundum veritatem facit ad depressionem.

5. Ad illud quod obiicitur de *potestate*, dicendum, quod utrumque verbum potest intelligi de eo quod quidem *posse* est potentiae. Nam quoddam *posse* potius est infirmitatis quam sublimitatis; posse namque habere naturam aequalem a se differentem, hoc dignitatis est potentiae limitatae, ac per hoc repugnat omnipotentiae; ideo, licet possit sermone dici, non tamen Deo attribui; nec secundum quantitatem huius possibilis attenditur quantitas divinae virtutis, sed secundum dignitatem actus proprii, qui est immensus, secundum quod potentia est immensa[^p62-3].

6. Ad illud quod obiicitur de *sapientia*, quod cognoscit aliud; dicendum, quod licet Deus aliud a se cognoscat aeternaliter, et hoc competat summae sapientiae; non tamen competit ei scire aliud *per aliud*, sed *per se*; et hoc non potest nisi aliud[^p62-4], quod natum est ab ipso Deo procedere, quod est in ipso sicut in principio causante. Nam etsi malum cognoscatur a Deo, hoc est per ideam boni sibi oppositi[^p62-5]. Unde ad perfectionem divinae cognitionis non requiritur, quod cognoscibile sit *ens actu*; nec requiritur, quod sit *ens summum*, cum cognoscat terrena nobilissime et optime per se ipsum. Illud autem locum habet in scientia, quae ortum habet a rebus, qualem scientiam Deus non habet.

7. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod summo bono competit summa diffusio et bonorum multitudo; dicendum, quod summa diffusio in aliquid *extra* non competit, secundum quod *summum* dicitur *simpliciter*; sed *intra* competit summa communicatio. Non enim debet esse nisi unum summum bonum, quod quidem non potest accrescere ex multitudine bonorum. Unde quod dicitur, quod summum bonum est summe diffusivum; intelligendum est, quod veritatem habet, secundum quod illud *summe* determinat diffusivum ratione *potentiae*, non ratione *actus*[^p62-6]. Et quod dicitur, quod plura bona magis eligenda; dicendum, quod habet veritatem in bonis finitis, quorum unum addit ad aliud, et quorum unicuique aliquid deficit de bono, quod suppletur per bonum adiunctum.

8. Ad illud quod obiicitur de influentia, quod non potest esse sine Deo, ergo nec multiplicari; dicendum, quod illud non valet nec est simile: quia de perfectione agentis est, quod non possit effectus esse sine eius praesentia, quia totaliter pendet ab illo, et agens nobilius agit immediate agendo quam per medium[^p62-7]; non sic autem est de numero et multitudine. Hoc enim non est de perfectione agentis, ut multiplicetur secundum multitudinem effectuum, sed potius eius contrarium, scilicet quod unum in se manens sit causa diversorum.

9. Ad illud quod obiicitur de causa proxima et immediata, quod multiplicatur; dicendum, quod illud habet veritatem in causa agente per modum naturae, cuius etiam virtus est arctata, et sic est causa propria, quod non universalis. Prima autem causa agit per modum artis et voluntatis in creaturarum productione; propterea est causa liberrima et ad nihil arctata et simul universalis et propria, quia quid-

<!-- page 63 -->

quid nobilitatis est in creatura, necessario oportet quod ponatur in creatrice essentia[^p63-1].

10. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod illa sunt media ad probandum multitudinem et diversitatem; dicendum, quod non probant quamcumque diversitatem in creaturis, sed diversitatem cum ordine et connexione et inclinatione ad unitatem[^p63-2], et ita diversitatem in unitate, et ratione illius unitatis inferunt in causa unitatem; nec tamen sunt via ad opposita, quia unitas in causa non repugnat multitudini in causatis, sicut patet in unitate, a qua procedunt infinitae species numerorum. Sic etiam summa sapientia et potentia et bonitas, licet sint unum, quia tamen non sufficienter manifestantur per unum effectum; ideo diversitatem inferunt in effectibus causatis ab ipsa. — Et sic patet responsio ad omnia obiecta.

## English

### Question II. *Whether the trinity of persons can stand together with unity of nature.*

Granted that it is a credible truth that God is three, it is next asked whether the trinity of persons can stand together with unity of nature[^p59-1]. And about this two things are asked.

The first is, whether the divine *being* is one in the highest degree.

Secondly it is asked, whether the highest unity of nature can be together with a trinity of persons.

### Article I. *Whether the divine being is one in the highest degree.*

About the first the procedure is as follows, and it is shown that the divine *being* is one in the highest degree. And that it is so, *faith* and *divine Scripture* sound out plainly enough, Exodus twenty[^p59-2]: *Thy God, God is one*; and Deuteronomy thirty-two: *See ye therefore that I alone am, and there is no other God besides me*; and David in the Psalm: *There shall be no new god in thee, neither shalt thou adore a strange god*; and divine Scripture preaches this plainly enough. — And this same thing is to be attempted by demonstration from *reasons*. For the mind of all who use reason supposes God to be that than which nothing greater and better can be thought out, and thereby most excellent in *nature* and *power*, in *wisdom* and *goodness*, in *inflowing* and *causality*. For if he suffered a defect in any of these, he would already not be highest, and thereby would not be God. Let these six, therefore, be supposed as true and most certain.

1. On the side of the *sublimity of nature* the argument runs thus. That which is borne above all things is more sublime than that which is not; therefore if to be borne above all things cannot belong except to one[^p59-3], it is impossible for God to be more than one, nay, it is impossible even to understand it. For if several are understood, it is thereby posited that the same one is borne above the other, and conversely; therefore if each is God, neither is God: either therefore God is not, or, if he is, he is one.

2. On the side of *omnipotence*, thus. He is truly omnipotent who not only can do all things, but from whom also is every *being-able*; but to be able to do all things and to cause every power cannot belong except to one alone — for if it belonged to several, it would suit neither — if therefore omnipotence suits God, it is impossible for God to be more than one, nay, it is impossible even to understand it: because, if each is omnipotent, each can bring it about that nothing should have every other *being-able*: therefore if each is omnipotent, neither is omnipotent[^p59-4].

3. On the side of the *highest wisdom* the argument runs thus. It belongs to one supremely wise to know all things[^p59-5]: if therefore there were several gods, either each would be *ignorant* of the other, and so neither would be supremely wise, but rather truly unwise; or he would *know* him, and this either through a *species*, or through his *essence*, or through *himself*. But if through a *species* or through his *essence*, he would receive something from the other and be informed by it; if through *himself*, he would be that other's principle, and thereby either he himself, or the other, would not be God. Therefore the highest wisdom does not allow that there be several gods, nay, it does not even allow the supposing of it, because, if several are thought of, they either have the &nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;&nbsp;s[*sic*]ame wisdom, or *different* ones. If *different*: then either the one is superfluous, or both are imperfect; if *the same*: since each one is the highest wisdom, therefore they will not now have a different nature: therefore they are not now two.

4. On the side of the *highest goodness*, thus. The highest good is to be loved in the highest degree and for its own sake: if therefore there are several things supremely good, then either the one *loves* the other for its own sake, or *not*. If *not*: then neither is good; if *so*: then the one beatifies the other: therefore

<!-- page 60 -->

neither is highest: therefore it is impossible that the highest good be multiplied; nay, this is even against understanding, because either one of them is the *greater* good, or not; if *not*: then the one is superfluous; if *so*: then neither is highest. Therefore if several things are understood to be supremely good, they are understood not to be supremely good[^p60-1].

5. On the side of *inflowing* the argument runs thus. God flows into all things most perfectly: therefore he gives the *whole* being, not a part[^p60-2]; but if there were several gods, either one would give nothing, and so would not be God; or each would give a part, or nothing, and so neither would be God; or each would give the same, and then each would be simultaneously in the same thing by essence and by power and by presence; and since this follows upon the highest inflowing: therefore either the one would be in the other, or each would be circumscribed, and so neither would be God.

6. On the side of the *highest causality* the argument runs thus. God is the first, last and most perfect cause, and thereby a cause in which there is a stopping-point in every mode, and according to the account of *efficiency*, of *exemplarity* and of *finality*[^p60-3]: therefore if there were several gods, each would be led back to the other: therefore neither would be first, last and most perfect, and thereby neither would be God.

7. On the side of *all the foregoing* the argument runs thus: because, if there were several gods, either they would *agree* altogether in all the aforesaid conditions, or they would altogether *differ*, or they would *partly agree* and *partly differ*. If they *agreed altogether*: then they would not be several gods; if they *differed altogether*: then if one were God, the other would not be God; but if *partly so* and *partly not*: then in each there would be something and something: therefore neither would be simple, and thereby neither would be God. — From these things it is argued that the divine *being* cannot be multiplied either *in reality* or *in estimation*.

*To the opposite* it is argued, first against the aforesaid reasons:

1. Because they do not seem to conclude what is *true*. For since that God is one is an article in which pagans, idolaters, unbelievers and heretics have erred, all of whom have the use of reason; it can not only be thought, but also supposed and believed, that there are several gods; or, if the contrary cannot be supposed, it is not an article of faith.

2. Again, they do not seem to conclude *necessarily*: because by the same reasons it can be proved that there are not several persons in the divine *being*, as is plain to one who works it through, whether on the side of the *sublimity of nature*, or of *power*, or of *wisdom*, or of *goodness*, or of *inflowing*, or of *causality*. But if the reasons proceed from the same middle terms to conclude something false, then they do not conclude necessarily.

3. Again, if by the aforesaid reasons it is necessarily established and proved that *God is one*; since "faith has no merit where human reason supplies the proof[^p60-4]": then the merit of faith seems to be voided: therefore either they will be feeble, or useless.

From themselves it is objected against them:

4. And first from *sublimity*. It is of greater sublimity to be borne above things of like nature than of unlike, and above great things than above small: if therefore the creature is not of a nature like God's and is as nothing in comparison with God, God is not understood to be *highest* and *most excellent*, if he is borne above created *being* only. It seems therefore to pertain to the divine excellence to posit several uncreated natures, and again one above the rest.

5. On the side of *omnipotence*, thus. "It belongs to perfect power," as Hilary says[^p60-5], "that the nature of the doer be able to do what the speech of the speaker can signify"; and again, reason dictates that so great a power reaches so far, and a greater to a greater, and the greatest to the greatest; but God is most omnipotent: therefore since we can signify that there are several gods, God can bring it about that there be; and since he can make a lesser thing to be, he can make a greater and a greatest: therefore he can make something other than himself as great as he himself is: it is consonant with omnipotence, therefore, that there be several gods.

6. On the side of *wisdom*, thus: because it belongs to the highest wisdom to know something other than itself, and wisdom is the more perfect the more perfect the knowable is: if therefore nothing is altogether perfect except[^p60-6] the being which is God, and the divine wisdom knows eternally something other than itself, and this by a most perfect cognition; it seems that there was eternally something other than God which nevertheless would be God: therefore the divine wisdom does not exclude, but posits, several divine essences.

7. On the side of *goodness* the argument runs thus. "The good is in the highest degree diffusive of itself," and "more goods are to be chosen than fewer[^p60-7]"; each of these is true of itself; but the highest diffusion of the divine *being* cannot be in a creature: it is therefore into another uncreated nature, and once that is posited the whole divine essence is better, for this reason, that there will be more goods. Therefore if everything better is to be attributed to God by reason of the highest goodness, it seems that the highest goodness does not exclude but requires several divine natures.

8. On the side of the *highest inflowing*, thus. The perfection of inflowing necessarily requires that the inflowing not be

<!-- page 61 -->

without the divine essence, presence and power: therefore by parity of reasoning it cannot be multiplied without a multiplication of essence and of power and of presence: therefore since the divine inflowings are many, the essences too are many.

9. On the side of *causality*, thus. The first cause is most perfect, it is first and immediate, therefore also proper; but when the effect is multiplied, the proximate and proper cause must necessarily be multiplied[^p61-1]: therefore it is necessary that multiplication be posited in the first cause, since multitude is posited and is in the effects caused by it.

10. Again, from all of them it is objected against all: because the most perfect sublimity in *nature* and *power*, in *wisdom* and *goodness*, in *inflowing* and *causality* is the ground for positing multitude in the effects caused: therefore if the same thing is not the cause of opposites[^p61-2], it seems that they lead rather to positing multitude than unity on the side of the efficient cause, and so are rather for the opposite than for the proposition.

**CONCLUSION.**

*That God is one is a truth not only credible, but also intelligible.*

**I answer:** It must be said that this truth, *that God is one*, is a truth not only *credible* but also *intelligible*; because it is necessary and certain not only from the *testimony of Scripture* and the *illumination of grace*, which is found in faith; but it is also certain *from itself* and *from the testimony of the creature*[^p61-3]. — *From itself*, then: because the divine *being*, on account of its singular sublimity and sublime singularity, has unity in every mode. For since God has every perfection in himself, and this in the highest and most excellent degree, he is established to be one not only from the *sublimity of nature* and of *wisdom*, of *power* and of *goodness* and of *inflowing* and of *causality*, but rather from *all his conditions* and noble properties which are attributed to him in the highest degree. Whence all the conditions bear witness to the unity of the highest essence.

From the *testimony* of the *creature* also: because every creature, just as it has natural *goodness*, so also is established to have *unity*. "For nothing can be, unless it be one," as Boethius says[^p61-4] and Augustine, and as sense and understanding teach. As therefore every creature by its *goodness* cries out that in God there is true and highest goodness; so by its *unity* it cries out that the cause of all things is in itself one. — Nor does the *diversity* of things stand against this testimony. For all diversity of things is comprehended within one universe, which in itself is finite and limited and perfect[^p61-5]. But this would not be so unless that plurality were led back to something in which there were a stopping-point; and thereby it is necessary that all things be led back to one last end and one first principle, otherwise there would be a going off to infinity. The very universe of things testifies, therefore, that God is one; whence, just as of one circumference it is impossible that there be, or be reasonably thought out, more than one centre, from which the lines flow and to which they are led back as to their term; so in one universe there is no positing or understanding of more than one and only God.

This, therefore, is necessary and known, and so far known that no one having the use of reason doubts of it, if he knows "what it is that is said by the name[^p61-6]". For if by this name *God* is signified the first and highest principle of all things; since in this the whole universe is enclosed, whoever understands this says consequently that God is one. Whence, just as principles are known of themselves, because they are known at once through knowledge of the terms[^p61-7]; so to everyone who understands the signification of the terms it is indubitably certain that God is one.

Many, nevertheless, have erred about the number of the gods; for ignorance of the signification gave birth to deception about the thing signified, and this introduced the heresy of a plurality of gods. For because men supposed that to be God which exceeds human *power* or human *knowing*, and they saw that demons could do this; therefore they believed them to be gods; and they erred and were blinded and bent down to the worship of idols; which indeed is most absurd, and no wonder, since "a small error in the beginning is a great one in the end[^p61-8]". From this error philosophy rescues us, but much better the Christian faith; and they concur in common in this, that the divine *being* is one in the highest degree. — Whence the reasons which show this are to be granted, because they conclude what is true and through a necessary middle term.

1. To that which is objected about the falsity of the conclusion, on the ground that many have supposed that there are several

<!-- page 62 -->

gods; the answer is already clear: because they use the supposit and signification of this term *God* otherwise than its institution has it; and therefore there is no contradiction there. Whence, just as this name *God* according to its proper institution has no plural, although it has one according as it is taken *by way of naming*[^p62-1]; so it is to be understood in the case before us; and because, through the blinding of the mind, the false was frequently taken for the true, therefore the illumination of faith was necessary. — It could nevertheless be said that *that God is one* can be proved by reason, in so far as his unity is considered absolutely; but the illumination of faith is necessary in so far as it is compared to the plurality of persons.

2. To that which is objected, that they could conclude likewise about the persons; it must be said that it is not alike: because in all the persons the nature is one, and the power and the wisdom and the goodness and the inflowing and the causality; but it would not be so if there were diverse divine natures; and therefore several persons are in no way repugnant to these.

3. To that which is objected, that the merit of faith is voided; it must be said that this would be true if one assented to this truth only on account of the reasons; but as it is, since one does not assent principally on account of the reasons, but on account of the truth itself, therefore he can have merit on account of his faithful assent. For nothing prevents the same thing from being *known* and *believed* according to one respect and consideration and another[^p62-2].

4. To that which is objected about *sublimity* in respect of great and like things; it must be said that this would hold if something accrued to the divine sublimity from the subjection of others; but this has no truth, because God is as sublime in himself and existing alone as if he ruled a thousand worlds. — *Moreover*, it would not make for his sublimity if something of like nature had a servile condition; whence, even though such sublimity may seem in some way to make for exaltation, yet in truth it makes for lowering.

5. To that which is objected about *power*, it must be said that either expression can be understood of that *being-able* which indeed belongs to power. For a certain *being-able* is rather of weakness than of sublimity; for to be able to have a nature equal to oneself and differing from oneself, this is of the dignity of a limited power, and thereby is repugnant to omnipotence; therefore, although it can be said in speech, it cannot be attributed to God; nor is the quantity of the divine power measured according to the quantity of this possible thing, but according to the dignity of its proper act, which is immense, according as the power is immense[^p62-3].

6. To that which is objected about *wisdom*, that it knows something other; it must be said that although God knows eternally something other than himself, and this belongs to the highest wisdom; nevertheless it does not belong to him to know something other *through something other*, but *through himself*; and this cannot be except something other[^p62-4] which is born to proceed from God himself, which is in him as in a causing principle. For even if evil is known by God, this is through the idea of the good opposed to it[^p62-5]. Whence for the perfection of divine cognition it is not required that the knowable be *a being in act*; nor is it required that it be *the highest being*, since he knows earthly things most nobly and best through himself. But that has its place in the science which takes its rise from things, which science God does not have.

7. To that which is objected, that the highest diffusion and a multitude of goods belong to the highest good; it must be said that the highest diffusion into something *outside* does not belong to it, according as *highest* is said *simply*; but *within*, the highest communication does belong to it. For there ought not to be more than one highest good, which indeed cannot be increased from a multitude of goods. Whence, as to what is said, that the highest good is diffusive in the highest degree; it must be understood that it holds true according as that word *in the highest degree* determines "diffusive" by reason of *power*, not by reason of *act*[^p62-6]. And as to what is said, that more goods are more to be chosen; it must be said that this holds true in finite goods, of which one adds to another, and to each of which something of good is lacking, which is supplied by the good adjoined.

8. To that which is objected about inflowing, that it cannot be without God, therefore neither can it be multiplied; it must be said that this does not hold and is not alike: because it is of the perfection of an agent that the effect cannot be without its presence, since it depends wholly on it, and a nobler agent acts by acting immediately rather than through a medium[^p62-7]; but it is not so with number and multitude. For this is not of the perfection of an agent, that it be multiplied according to the multitude of its effects, but rather its contrary, namely that one thing remaining in itself be the cause of diverse things.

9. To that which is objected about a proximate and immediate cause, that it is multiplied; it must be said that this holds true of a cause acting after the manner of nature, whose power moreover is confined, and so is a proper cause, not a universal one. But the first cause acts after the manner of art and of will in the production of creatures; therefore it is a most free cause, confined to nothing, and at once universal and proper, because what-

<!-- page 63 -->

ever of nobility is in the creature must necessarily be posited in the creating essence[^p63-1].

10. To that which is objected, that those are middle terms for proving multitude and diversity; it must be said that they do not prove just any diversity in creatures, but a diversity with order and connection and inclination toward unity[^p63-2], and so a diversity in unity, and by reason of that unity they infer unity in the cause; nor yet are they a way to opposites, because unity in the cause is not repugnant to multitude in the things caused, as is plain in unity, from which proceed the infinite species of numbers. So too the highest wisdom and power and goodness, although they are one, yet because they are not sufficiently manifested through one effect; therefore they infer diversity in the effects caused by it. — And thus the answer to all the objections is plain.

### Register — what was exercised

- ***intellectus* → "understanding", with ZERO bare "intellect" in the English.** The ratified narrow exception is **unreachable in this work** by the pilot's census (*intellectus agens* / *possibilis* occur nowhere in its 71 printed pages), so a bare "intellect" here would be a defect by construction. The sites are argument 4's *hoc est contra intellectum* → "this is even against understanding" and the *Respondeo*'s *et docet sensus et intellectus* → "and as sense and understanding teach". The verb *intelligere* takes "understand" throughout, including in its heavily repeated idiom *immo etiam nec intelligere* → "nay, it is impossible even to understand it".
- ***notitia* → "knowledge" / *cognitio* → "cognition" held apart** (the `bon-qsc-q2` ruling): *per terminorum notitiam* → "through knowledge of the terms" stands a few lines from *cognitione perfectissima* → "by a most perfect cognition" and *ad perfectionem divinae cognitionis* → "for the perfection of divine cognition". *Scientia* takes **"science"** where it is the technical term (*quae ortum habet a rebus* → "the science which takes its rise from things"; p. 61 n. 7's *Principium scientiae*), which is what keeps the three words visibly distinct in one paragraph.
- ***esse* is the substantive throughout and is kept as "being"** — *divinum esse* → "the divine being", *superfertur solum esse creato* → "is borne above created being only". ⚠ **Quaracchi sets it in a reversing font and the plate shows both directions**: italic *esse* inside roman body text, and roman `esse` inside the italic subtitle *Utrum divinum* esse *sit summe unum*. The markup follows the print.
- ***posse* → "being-able"** where it is the substantive noun (argument 2's *a quo est omne posse*, reply 5's *quoddam posse potius est infirmitatis*), against the ordinary verb elsewhere. The reply turns on the distinction and an English that flattens it loses the argument.
- ***influentia* → "inflowing"**, held to one word across ten occurrences (the fifth property, opposing argument 8, reply 8), against *influit* → "flows into".
- ***ratio*** takes its contextual English: *rationibus demonstrare* → "by demonstration from reasons" and *ratio dictat* → "reason dictates" (the faculty and its arguments), against *secundum rationem efficiendi, exemplandi et finiendi* → "according to the account of efficiency, of exemplarity and of finality", and *ratio ponendi multitudinem* → "the ground for positing multitude".
- **⛔ *lux* / *lumen*: neither occurs in these five leaves.** Ruling 2 is not exercised, and **the Psalm 4:7 site does not recur** — the two attestations `bon-qmt-q1-a1` and `q1-a2` handed to the shakedown gate stand at two, and this chunk adds nothing to them either way. **The gate at p. 67 still owns the question.**
- **⛔ *vacatio* / *quies* is NOT tested here** — neither family occurs. The deciding sites remain `q5-a2` (raw L23636, L23645) and `q7-a2` (raw L25824, L25900), of which L25824 holds both in one clause.
