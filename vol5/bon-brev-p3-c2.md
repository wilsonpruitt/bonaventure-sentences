## Latin

### Capitulum II. *De primorum parentum tentatione.*

Ad intelligendum autem, qualiter corruptela peccati introivit in mundum, considerare oportet *lapsum* primi parentis, *traductionem* culpae originalis et *ortum* seu radicem peccati actualis. — Circa *lapsum* igitur primi parentis haec tria nobis consideranda occurrunt, scilicet *tentatio* diabolica, *culpa* commissa et *poena* inflicta[^p231-7].

<!-- page 232 -->

Haec autem de *tentatione* tenenda sunt, quod cum Deus hominem condidisset in felicitate paradisi in duplici sexu, virili scilicet et muliebri; diabolus invidens homini, assumta specie serpentina, aggressus est mulierem, primo *quaerendo: Cur praecepit vobis Deus, ne comederetis*[^p232-1]? secundo *asserendo: Nequaquam moriemini;* tertio *promittendo: Eritis sicut dii, scientes bonum et malum;* volens ista tentatione deiicere mulierem infirmiorem et per illam postea prosternere sexum virilem; quod et fecit, Domino permittente.

Ratio autem ad intelligentiam praedictorum haec est: quia, sicut primum principium est potentissimum in producendo, sic etiam est rectissimum in gubernando; et ideo « res, quas condidit, ita administrat, ut tamen eas agere proprios motus sinat[^p232-2] ». Quoniam ergo homo sic factus erat, ut per victoriam pugnae perveniret ad praemium quietis aeternae; licet Deus sciret, hominem tentationi succumbere, debuit tamen permittere, hominem tentari ab eo qui *sciret, posset* et *vellet*[^p232-3]. — Quoniam igitur diabolus, qui prius erat sciens et rectus, per superbiam cadendo factus est versutus et invidus; et ideo per invidiam tentare *volebat* et per astutiam *sciebat:* ideo tentavit, iuxta quod *potuit*, et Deus permisit. Quod ergo in tentando speciem serpentinam assumsit, hoc fuit dispensationis divinae, ut non solum posset deprehendi eius versutia, verum etiam ex illa effigie versutia diabolica in tentando innotescere posset cunctis filiis Adae.

Rursus, quod tentavit de praecepto *disciplinae*[^p232-4], hoc similiter fuit dispensationis divinae, ut, sive vinceretur, sive vinceret, cunctis innotesceret meritum obedientiae, sive demeritum inobedientiae. — Quod autem incepit a *muliere*, hoc fuit versutiae suae, quia facilius est deiicere minus fortem; unde versutia hostis ex infirmiori parte aggreditur civitatem.

Similiter *modus*, quo in tentatione processit, magnae fuit versutiae, quia *experiendo, impellendo* et *alliciendo* processit. *Experientiam* enim sumsit in interrogatione, *impulit* in assecuratione, *allexit* autem in promissione. Primo enim *interrogavit* de causa mandati, ut rationem duceret in dubium; secundo, dubitatione habita: *Ne forte moriamur*[^p232-5], *assecuravit*, ut irascibilem duceret in contemptum; tertio, *promisit*, ut concupiscibilem duceret in appetitum, et sic his tribus modis libertatem arbitrii traheret ad consensum, quae facultas est *rationis et voluntatis*, complectens nihilominus tres praedictas vires, rationalem, irascibilem et concupiscibilem[^p232-6]; respectu quarum diabolus allexit mulierem per triplex appetibile, scilicet per *scientiam*, quae est appetibilis rationali; per *excellentiam* ad modum Dei, quae est appetibilis irascibili; per *suavitatem ligni*, quae est appetibilis concupiscibili. Et sic tentavit omne, quod erat in muliere tentabile, per omne illud, per quod poterat in tentationem induci, quod est triplex appetibile mundi, scilicet *concupiscentia carnis, concupiscentia oculorum et superbia vitae*[^p232-7]; secundum quae tria attenditur omnis tentationis origo, sive a mundo, sive a carne, sive a diabolo.

## English

### Chapter II. *On the temptation of the first parents.*

Now, in order to understand how the corruption of sin entered into the world, it is necessary to consider the *fall* of the first parent, the *transmission* of original guilt, and the *rise* or root of actual sin. — Concerning the *fall* of the first parent, therefore, these three occur to us as to be considered, namely the diabolical *temptation*, the *guilt* committed, and the *punishment* inflicted[^p231-7].

<!-- page 232 -->

Now these things are to be held concerning the *temptation*: that, when God had established man in the happiness of paradise in a twofold sex, namely the male and the female, the devil, envying man, having assumed the appearance of a serpent, attacked the woman, first by *asking: Why has God commanded you, that you should not eat*[^p232-1]? second by *asserting: You shall by no means die;* third by *promising: You shall be as gods, knowing good and evil;* wishing by this temptation to cast down the woman as the weaker, and afterwards through her to lay low the male sex; which indeed he did, the Lord permitting it.

Now the reason for the understanding of the foresaid is this: since, just as the first principle is most powerful in producing, so also it is most right in governing; and therefore « the things which it has founded it so administers that it nonetheless allows them to perform their own motions[^p232-2] ». Since therefore man had been so made that he should come through the victory of the battle to the reward of eternal rest; although God knew that man would succumb to the temptation, he nevertheless had to permit that man be tempted by one who *would know*, *would be able*, and *would will* it[^p232-3]. — Since therefore the devil, who before was knowing and upright, by falling through pride became crafty and envious; and therefore through envy he *willed* to tempt and through cunning he *knew* how: therefore he tempted, according as he *was able*, and God permitted it. That, then, in tempting he assumed the appearance of a serpent, this was of the divine dispensation, so that not only might his craftiness be detected, but also that from that likeness the diabolical craftiness in tempting might become known to all the sons of Adam.

Again, that he tempted concerning the precept of *discipline*[^p232-4], this likewise was of the divine dispensation, so that, whether he were conquered or should conquer, the merit of obedience, or the demerit of disobedience, might become known to all. — And that he began from the *woman*, this was of his craftiness, because it is easier to cast down the less strong; whence the craftiness of the enemy attacks the city from the weaker side.

Likewise the *manner* in which he proceeded in the temptation was of great craftiness, because he proceeded by *making trial, by impelling* and *by enticing*. For he took his *trial* in the questioning, he *impelled* in the reassuring, and he *enticed* in the promising. For first he *questioned* concerning the cause of the command, that he might lead reason into doubt; second, once doubt was had — *Lest perhaps we die*[^p232-5] — he *reassured*, that he might lead the irascible into contempt; third, he *promised*, that he might lead the concupiscible into appetite, and thus by these three modes might draw the freedom of choice to consent — which faculty is *of reason and of will*, embracing nonetheless the three foresaid powers, the rational, the irascible and the concupiscible[^p232-6]; in respect of which the devil enticed the woman through a threefold desirable object, namely through *knowledge*, which is desirable to the rational; through *excellence* after the manner of God, which is desirable to the irascible; through the *sweetness of the tree*, which is desirable to the concupiscible. And thus he tempted everything that was temptable in the woman, through everything by which she could be led into temptation, which is the threefold desirable object of the world, namely *the concupiscence of the flesh, the concupiscence of the eyes, and the pride of life*[^p232-7]; according to which three the origin of every temptation is reckoned, whether from the world, or from the flesh, or from the devil.
