# Quaestiones disputatae de mysterio Trinitatis — Quaestio VII, Articulus I
## *Utrum esse divinum sit summe necessarium.*

---

## Latin
<!-- page 106 -->

### Quaestio VII. *Utrum possit stare trinitas cum summa necessitate.*

Consequenter quaeritur, utrum possit stare trinitas cum summa necessitate. Et circa hoc quaeruntur duo.

Primum est, utrum *esse* divinum sit summe necessarium.

Secundum est, utrum stent simul trinitas et summa necessitas[^p106-5].

### Articulus I. *Utrum esse divinum sit summe necessarium.*

Quaeritur ergo, utrum *esse* divinum sit summe necessarium. Et quod sic, videtur.

1. Omne immutabile et aeternum est summe necessarium[^p106-6]; sed divinum *esse* est huiusmodi: ergo etc.

2. Item, necesse est, omne, quod est nobilitatis et perfectionis, poni in Deo in summo; sed constans est, quod[^p106-7] necessitas est magis quam scientia: ergo etc.

3. Item, omne, quod ita est, quod non potest non esse nec cogitari non esse, est necessarium in summo; sed divinum *esse* est huiusmodi[^p106-8]: ergo etc.

4. Item, omne, quod habet *esse* a se et per se et secundum se, est omnino necessarium; sed tale est *esse* divinum, cum sit primum: ergo etc.

5. Item, impossibile est, necessitatem esse in effectu nisi per causam: ergo si in Deo non est

<!-- page 107 -->

necessitas, in nullo est necessitas: ergo nihil est necessarium. Sed si nihil est necessarium, nihil est impossibile: ergo omnia sunt possibilia: ergo aliquid simul esse et non esse est possibile. Sed hoc est absurdum[^p107-1]: ergo etc.

6. Item, impossibile est, necessitatem esse in composito nisi per necessitatem existentem in simplici; ergo si simplicissimum caret necessitate, impossibile est, quod in aliqua complexione vel compositione sit ponere necessitatem. Sed hoc est absurdum, quod nulla argumentatio necessaria sit[^p107-2]: ergo etc.

Cᴏɴᴛʀᴀ: 1. Omne necessarium est determinatum ad sic et non aliter se habendum; omne determinatum ad aliquid est finitum[^p107-3]; sed divinum *esse* est infinitum: ergo etc.

2. Item, nihil, quod est indifferens ad opposita, est necessarium; sed divinum *posse*, *velle* et *agere* est indifferens ad opposita: ergo non[^p107-4] est necessarium. Sed divinum *posse*, *velle* et *agere* est divinum *esse:* ergo divinum *esse* non est necessarium.

3. Item, nulli immutabili potest attribui aliquid mutabile, ergo nulli summe necessario potest attribui aliquid contingens; sed Deo attribuitur aliquid contingens: ergo etc.

4. Item, sicut *impossibilitas* repugnat potentiae, ita *necessitas* voluntati; sed divina potentia tanta est, quod omnia sunt ei possibilia, nec ponitur aliquo modo impossibilitas in Deo[^p107-5]: ergo cum *velle* Dei sit perfectissimum, videtur, quod non patiatur necessitatem in *esse* divino.

5. Item, si causa est necessaria, et effectus[^p107-6]; sed Deus est causa omnium prima et summa et immediata: ergo si in Deo est necessitas, omnia sunt necessaria.

6. Item, quanto aliquid contingentius, tanto est indifferentius, et quanto indifferentius, tanto communius, et quanto communius, tanto simplicius[^p107-7]: ergo *a primo*, quanto aliquid contingentius, tanto simplicius: ergo simplicissimum est contingentissimum, non ergo necessarium.

7. Item, omne necessarium scibile, sicut omne probabile opinabile[^p107-8]; sed omne scibile vel est principium demonstrationis, vel demonstratum; divinum autem *esse* neutrum est: ergo etc.

8. Item, quanto aliquid magis est necessarium, tanto est magis certum, et quanto magis certum, tanto magis notum[^p107-9]; sed divinum *esse* est maxime occultum: ergo etc.

9. Item, sicut frustra movetur quis, quia appetit impossibile[^p107-10], ita, quia timet necessarium: ergo si divina Maiestas est necessitas, ergo nullo modo est timenda. Sed hoc est falsum et absurdum: ergo etc.

Cᴏɴᴄʟᴜsɪᴏ.

*Divinum esse est necessarium perfectissima necessitate immutabilitatis.*

Rᴇsᴘᴏɴᴅᴇᴏ: Ad praedictorum intelligentiam est notandum, quod triplex est necessitas: quaedam *omnino extrinseca*, quaedam *partim intrinseca* et *partim extrinseca*, et quaedam *omnino intrinseca*. Necessitas *omnino extrinseca* est illa, quae ortum habet a principio, quod est *extra*, nihil cooperante passo[^p107-11]; et haec dupliciter habet esse: vel respectu operum *naturalium*, vel respectu *voluntariorum;* et sic duplex est necessitas, scilicet *coactionis* et *violentiae*. — Necessitas vero *partim intra*, *partim extra* est quae aliquo modo est a principio intrinseco, respiciente aliquid extra vel per modum *principii moventis*, vel per modum *termini quiescentis;* et sic est necessitas dupliciter dicta, scilicet *inevitabilitatis* et *indigentiae*. — Quaedam vero est necessitas *omnino intrinseca*, quae inest rei ex propria natura; et haec est necessitas *immutabilitatis* et independentiae; et talis necessitas *secundum quid* reperitur in creatura, *simpliciter* autem non potest reperiri nisi in creatrice essentia. Ipsa enim sola est, quae non admittit aliquam dependentiam; cetera vero, cum sint creata, necessario dependent ab ipsa.

Haec autem necessitas necessario ponitur in *esse divino*, quia est *in se ipso* et *a se ipso*. Quia autem *in*[^p107-12] *se ipso*, ideo caret *componente* principio, aut *sustentante;* ideo nec potest mutari nec deficere.

<!-- page 108 -->

Quia autem *a se ipso*, ideo caret *producente* et creante; ideo nec potest *dependere* nec *incipere;* ac per hoc summam et perfectissimam habet necessitatem, quae excludit omnem mutabilitatem et dependentiam, inevitabilitatem et indigentiam, coactionem et violentiam, quae necessitatem dicunt diminutam; et econtra ponit summam permanentiam et stabilitatem, summam sufficientiam et libertatem. Quia enim est *se ipsa*, ideo est permanens et stabilissima; quia vero *sui gratia*, ideo liberalis et sufficientissima; quia vero est *se ipsa* et *sui gratia*, ideo est *alpha et omega*[^p108-1]; et ideo cetera sunt *ab ipsa*, et cetera *propter ipsam*, sive necessaria sive contingentia. Sicut enim omnia *mobilia* manant ab uno immobili primo et ad illud reducuntur; sic omnia *contingentia* a necessario fluunt et in illud reducuntur[^p108-2]. Est igitur necessitas divini *esse* omnis *esse*, *vivere* et *intelligere* origo et complementum; et omnia clamant, primum principium esse necessarium, sive sint necessaria sive contingentia. — Et ideo rationes concedendae sunt ad partem istam.

1. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod omne determinatum ad aliquid est finitum; dicendum, quod est determinatio duplex, scilicet per *arctationem*, cuiusmodi est illa, quae fit per *additionem* unius super alterum, sicut additur dispositio super potentiam materiae incompletam; et est determinatio per *completionem*, sicut summe bonum est ad bonum agendum, quia non potest deficere in faciendo malum. Primo modo determinatio repugnat infinitati secundo vero modo non repugnat[^p108-3]; et haec quidem determinatio convenit necessitati, secundum quod est in Deo, et non primo modo.

2. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod nihil indifferens ad opposita est necessarium; dicendum, quod divinum *posse* et *velle* et *agere* dicitur *indifferens ad opposita* non ratione sui in *se ipso*, sed ratione indifferentiae in *effectu producendo*. Unde indifferentia illa non ponit aliquam vertibilitatem in divino *posse;* sed ponit potestatem unam et simplicissimam, super omnia opposita omnipotentem, ita quod ad nullum illorum restringi potest[^p108-4]; et hoc magis necessitati consonat, quam repugnet.

3. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod nulli summe necessario potest attribui aliquid contingens; dicendum, quod cum aliquid contingenter praedicatur de Deo, ibi in praedicato illo clauditur et *principale significatum* et *connotatum;* et quantum ad *principale significatum* non est aliud quod Deus, quantum autem ad *connotatum* est effectus; et ratione primi, cui competit praedicatio, *necessario* inest; ratione vero secundi dicitur inesse *contingenter*, ac per hoc non ponitur contingentia esse *in Deo*, sed potius *a Deo*. In huiusmodi praedicationibus divinum *esse* intra se nullam praedicationem recipit, quae non includat completissimam necessitatem. — Quod autem dicitur totum illud dictum *contingens*, scilicet Deum salvare Petrum; hoc est, quia talis est natura totius copulati, quod fit in eo denominatio a minus nobili, ut dicatur *falsa* propositio copulativa, cuius altera pars falsa, et *contingens*, cuius altera pars est contingens. Et ratio huius est, quia facilius est rem destrui quam construi[^p108-5]; et *habitus* impermixtus debet esse privationi et eius denominatio; denominatio autem *privationis* se habet contrario modo, quia de necessitate intellectus positionis et habitus aliquo modo in eius clauditur intellectu.

4. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod necessitas repugnat voluntati, ut impossibilitas potestati; dicendum, quod illud veritatem habet de illa necessitate, quae est *ab extra*, cuiusmodi est necessitas *violentiae* et *coactionis;* de illa autem necessitate, quae est *intrinseca*, cuiusmodi est necessitas *immutabilitatis*, veritatem non habet, sicut ex praecedentibus apparet.

5. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod si causa necessaria, et effectus; dicendum, quod, sicut dicit Augustinus quinto de Civitate Dei[^p108-6], causarum aliae sunt tantum *factae;* aliae vero *facientes et factae;* tertiae vero sunt *facientes tantum*. Causas autem *tantum factas* vocat causas naturales, quae determinatae sunt ad unum cursum; causas vero *facientes et factas*, liberum arbitrium et voluntatem; causas vero *factivas tantum*, ipsam divinam providentiam. Dicit autem, causas naturales *factas* esse, non *facientes*, non quia nihil faciant, sed quia sic faciunt, secundum quod est eis inditum, non habendo dominium super actum suum, quemadmodum habet liberum arbitrium, et tales sunt unigeneae suis partibus et conformes; et in huiusmodi praedicta propositio veritatem habet. De causa vero *faciente*, quae quidem potens est efficere et non efficere per eandem vim propter dominium super actionem et

<!-- page 109 -->

effectum consequentem, non habet veritatem. Sicut enim talis causa non mutatur propter effectuum mutabilitatem et differentiam, sic etiam nec contingens esse ponitur propter contingentiam in suis effectibus.

6. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod quanto aliquid indifferentius, tanto communius; dicendum, quod est *communitas* duplex, scilicet per *abstractionem*, et per *indenominationem*. Cum ergo dicitur, quod quanto aliquid indifferentius, tanto communius; hoc intelligitur de communitate per *indenominationem*, per quem modum dicimus, quod contrariorum unicum est subiectum; quod maxime verum est in his quorum alterum non habet circa subiectum determinatam causam[^p109-1]. Cum autem adiungit, quod quanto aliquid communius, tanto simplicius; illud intelligitur de communitate per *abstractionem;* unde ex hoc non concluditur, quod contingentia ponant simplicitatem; quin potius includunt mutabilitatem, quae excludit summam simplicitatem; propter quod summa simplicitas non potest nisi in summe necessario reperiri. Quia ergo divinum *esse* est simplicissimum; ideo necesse est, ipsum esse non contingens, sed necessarium.

7. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod omne necessarium est scibile, et omne scibile est principium demonstrationis, vel per demonstrationem conclusum; dicendum, quod est necessarium *in genere*, et est necessarium *extra genus;* et hoc quidem est dupliciter: vel extra *genus determinatum*, vel extra *omne genus simpliciter*[^p109-2]. Necessarium ergo *extra omne genus simpliciter*, hoc est in solo Deo, qui est supra et extra omne genus entis, quia in genere esse non potest, nec *per se* nec *per appropriationem;* et tale necessarium omnem demonstrationem antecedit et supergreditur, quia nullam definitionem prorsus habere potest[^p109-3]. — Quod ergo dicitur: omne necessarium est principium demonstrationis, vel demonstratum; dicendum, quod illud intelligitur de necessario, quod est *determinati generis*, vel quod ad *determinatum genus potest appropriari*, sicut sunt communes animi conceptiones, quae, in quantum appropriantur, demonstrationem ingrediuntur. Unde ratio illa non concludit, quod divinum *esse* est necessarium ad genus determinatum coarctatum.

8. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod quanto magis necessarium, tanto magis certum; dicendum, quod aliquid dicitur esse certius altero seu notius dupliciter: vel *simpliciter*, vel *quoad nos*[^p109-4]. Prima igitur propositio veritatem habet, secundum quod intelligitur de maiori certitudine *simpliciter*, non autem, secundum quod intelligitur de certitudine *quoad nos*, pro eo quod illa quae sunt certissima et luculentissima in se ipsis obscura nobis videntur propter defectum ex parte intellectus creati; et ideo Philosophus[^p109-5] dicit, quod « sicut se habet oculus noctuae ad lucem, sic oculus noster ad manifestissima naturae ». Nihilominus tamen, licet divinum *esse* secundum plenitudinem suae claritatis sit nobis occultum, quamdiu sumus in statu viatorum, certissimum nobis est et indubitabile, Deum esse, secundum quod ex praecedentibus quaestionibus[^p109-6] manifeste apparet.

9. Ad illud quod obiicitur, quod necessarium non est timendum, sicut nec impossibile appetendum; dicendum, quod necessarium in *eventu* vel in effectu formidandum non est, quia nihil prodest formido, ubi necesse est sic evenire; necessarium autem secundum *statum intrinsecum*, quod potest sine sui mutatione retribuere sempiternam gloriam, vel sempiternum supplicium; hoc, inquam, est maxime formidandum et propter acquirendam gratiam et propter declinandam poenam, quia tale necessarium voluntatis liberalitatem et aequitatis iustitiam non excludit, secundum quod in sequenti quaestione melius apparebit.

## English

### Question VII. *Whether trinity can stand with supreme necessity.*

Next it is asked whether trinity can stand with supreme necessity. And concerning this two things are asked.

The first is whether the divine *being* is supremely necessary.

The second is whether trinity and supreme necessity stand together[^p106-5].

### Article I. *Whether the divine being is supremely necessary.*

It is asked, then, whether the divine *being* is supremely necessary. And that it is, it seems.

1. Everything immutable and eternal is supremely necessary[^p106-6]; but the divine *being* is of this kind: therefore etc.

2. Likewise, it is necessary that everything which belongs to nobility and perfection be posited in God in the highest degree; but it is certain that[^p106-7] necessity is more than knowledge: therefore etc.

3. Likewise, everything which is such that it cannot not be, nor be thought not to be, is necessary in the highest degree; but the divine *being* is of this kind[^p106-8]: therefore etc.

4. Likewise, everything which has *being* from itself and through itself and according to itself is altogether necessary; but such is the divine *being*, since it is first: therefore etc.

5. Likewise, it is impossible that necessity be in an effect except through a cause: therefore if in God there is not

<!-- page 107 -->

necessity, in nothing is there necessity: therefore nothing is necessary. But if nothing is necessary, nothing is impossible: therefore all things are possible: therefore it is possible for something at once to be and not to be. But this is absurd[^p107-1]: therefore etc.

6. Likewise, it is impossible that there be necessity in the composite except through a necessity existing in the simple; therefore if the most simple lacks necessity, it is impossible that necessity be posited in any combination or composition. But this is absurd, that no argumentation should be necessary[^p107-2]: therefore etc.

Cᴏɴᴛʀᴀ: 1. Everything necessary is determined to being disposed thus and not otherwise; everything determined to something is finite[^p107-3]; but the divine *being* is infinite: therefore etc.

2. Likewise, nothing that is indifferent to opposites is necessary; but the divine *being able*, *willing* and *acting* is indifferent to opposites: therefore it is not[^p107-4] necessary. But the divine *being able*, *willing* and *acting* is the divine *being:* therefore the divine *being* is not necessary.

3. Likewise, to nothing immutable can anything mutable be attributed, therefore to nothing supremely necessary can anything contingent be attributed; but something contingent is attributed to God: therefore etc.

4. Likewise, just as *impossibility* is repugnant to power, so *necessity* is to will; but the divine power is so great that all things are possible to it, nor is impossibility posited in God in any mode[^p107-5]: therefore since the *willing* of God is most perfect, it seems that it does not suffer necessity in the divine *being*.

5. Likewise, if the cause is necessary, the effect is also[^p107-6]; but God is the first and highest and immediate cause of all things: therefore if there is necessity in God, all things are necessary.

6. Likewise, the more contingent a thing is, the more indifferent it is, and the more indifferent, the more common, and the more common, the more simple[^p107-7]: therefore *from the first*, the more contingent a thing is, the more simple: therefore the most simple is the most contingent, and therefore not necessary.

7. Likewise, everything necessary is knowable, just as everything probable is matter of opinion[^p107-8]; but everything knowable is either a principle of demonstration or something demonstrated; but the divine *being* is neither: therefore etc.

8. Likewise, the more necessary a thing is, the more certain it is, and the more certain, the more known[^p107-9]; but the divine *being* is supremely hidden: therefore etc.

9. Likewise, just as one is moved in vain because he desires the impossible[^p107-10], so too because he fears the necessary: therefore if the divine Majesty is necessity, then it is in no way to be feared. But this is false and absurd: therefore etc.

Cᴏɴᴄʟᴜsɪᴏ.

*The divine being is necessary with the most perfect necessity of immutability.*

Rᴇsᴘᴏɴᴅᴇᴏ: For the understanding of what has been said it must be noted that necessity is threefold: one *wholly extrinsic*, one *partly intrinsic* and *partly extrinsic*, and one *wholly intrinsic*. Necessity *wholly extrinsic* is that which has its origin from a principle which is *outside*, the patient cooperating in nothing[^p107-11]; and this has being in two ways: either with respect to *natural* works, or with respect to *voluntary* ones; and thus necessity is twofold, namely of *coercion* and of *violence*. — But necessity *partly within*, *partly without* is that which is in some way from an intrinsic principle regarding something outside, either by way of a *moving principle*, or by way of a *resting term;* and thus necessity is spoken of in two ways, namely of *inevitability* and of *need*. — But there is a necessity *wholly intrinsic*, which is in the thing from its own nature; and this is the necessity of *immutability* and of independence; and such necessity is found *in a certain respect* in the creature, but *simply* it cannot be found except in the creating essence. For it alone is that which admits no dependence; but the rest, since they are created, necessarily depend on it.

Now this necessity is necessarily posited in the *divine being*, because it is *in itself* and *from itself*. And because it is *in*[^p107-12] *itself*, it therefore lacks a *composing* principle, or a *sustaining* one; therefore it can neither be changed nor fail.

<!-- page 108 -->

And because it is *from itself*, it therefore lacks anything *producing* and creating it; therefore it can neither *depend* nor *begin;* and on this account it has the highest and most perfect necessity, which excludes all mutability and dependence, inevitability and need, coercion and violence, which speak of a diminished necessity; and on the contrary it posits the highest permanence and stability, the highest sufficiency and freedom. For because it is *itself*, it is therefore permanent and most stable; but because it is *for its own sake*, it is therefore liberal and most sufficient; and because it is *itself* and *for its own sake*, it is therefore the *alpha and omega*[^p108-1]; and therefore the rest are *from it*, and the rest are *for its sake*, whether necessary or contingent. For just as all *mobile* things flow forth from one immobile first and are led back to it; so all *contingent* things flow from the necessary and are led back to it[^p108-2]. The necessity of the divine *being*, therefore, is the origin and completion of all *being*, *living* and *understanding;* and all things cry out that the first principle is necessary, whether they be necessary or contingent. — And therefore the reasons for this side are to be conceded.

1. To the objection that everything determined to something is finite, it must be said that determination is twofold, namely by *restriction*, of which kind is that which comes about by the *addition* of one thing upon another, as a disposition is added upon the incomplete potency of matter; and there is determination by *completion*, as the supreme good is determined to doing good, because it cannot fail by doing evil. In the first mode determination is repugnant to infinity but in the second mode it is not repugnant[^p108-3]; and it is this determination that befits necessity as it is in God, and not the first mode.

2. To the objection that nothing indifferent to opposites is necessary, it must be said that the divine *being able* and *willing* and *acting* is called *indifferent to opposites* not by reason of itself in *itself*, but by reason of an indifference in the *effect to be produced*. Whence that indifference does not posit any vertibility in the divine *being able;* but it posits one most simple power, omnipotent over all opposites, such that it cannot be restricted to any one of them[^p108-4]; and this is more consonant with necessity than repugnant to it.

3. To the objection that nothing contingent can be attributed to what is supremely necessary, it must be said that when something is predicated contingently of God, there is enclosed in that predicate both the *principal significate* and the *connoted;* and as regards the *principal significate* it is nothing other than God, but as regards the *connoted* it is an effect; and by reason of the first, to which the predication belongs, it is in Him *necessarily;* but by reason of the second it is said to be in Him *contingently*, and on this account contingency is not posited to be *in God*, but rather *from God*. In predications of this kind the divine *being* receives within itself no predication that does not include the most complete necessity. — But that the whole dictum is called *contingent*, namely that God saves Peter, this is because the nature of the whole conjunction is such that the denomination in it comes from the less noble part, so that a copulative proposition is called *false* when one of its parts is false, and *contingent* when one of its parts is contingent. And the reason for this is that it is easier for a thing to be destroyed than to be constructed[^p108-5]; and a *habit* ought to be unmixed with privation, and so ought its denomination; but the denomination of *privation* holds in the contrary way, because of necessity the understanding of position and of habit is in some way enclosed in its understanding.

4. To the objection that necessity is repugnant to will, as impossibility is to power, it must be said that this holds true of that necessity which is *from outside*, of which kind is the necessity of *violence* and of *coercion;* but of that necessity which is *intrinsic*, of which kind is the necessity of *immutability*, it does not hold true, as is clear from what has gone before.

5. To the objection that if the cause is necessary, the effect is also, it must be said that, as Augustine says in the fifth book of *The City of God*[^p108-6], of causes some are only *made;* others are *making and made;* and the third are *making only*. The causes *only made* he calls natural causes, which are determined to a single course; the causes *making and made*, free choice and will; the causes *productive only*, the divine providence itself. And he says that natural causes are *made*, not *making*, not because they do nothing, but because they so act, according to what is implanted in them, not having dominion over their act, as free choice has, and such causes are of one kind with their parts and conformed to them; and in these the aforesaid proposition holds true. But of a *making* cause, which is able to effect and not to effect by the same power on account of its dominion over its action and

<!-- page 109 -->

the consequent effect, it does not hold true. For just as such a cause is not changed on account of the mutability and difference of its effects, so neither is it posited to be contingent on account of the contingency in its effects.

6. To the objection that the more indifferent a thing is, the more common it is, it must be said that *commonness* is twofold, namely by *abstraction*, and by *non-denomination*. When therefore it is said that the more indifferent a thing is, the more common it is, this is understood of commonness by *non-denomination*, in which mode we say that there is a single subject of contraries; which is most true in those of which neither has a determinate cause in regard to the subject[^p109-1]. But when it adds that the more common a thing is, the more simple it is, that is understood of commonness by *abstraction;* whence from this it is not concluded that contingent things posit simplicity; rather they include mutability, which excludes supreme simplicity; on account of which supreme simplicity cannot be found except in what is supremely necessary. Since therefore the divine *being* is most simple, it is therefore necessary that it be not contingent, but necessary.

7. To the objection that everything necessary is knowable, and everything knowable is a principle of demonstration or concluded through demonstration, it must be said that there is the necessary *in a genus*, and there is the necessary *outside a genus;* and this in two ways: either outside a *determinate genus*, or outside *every genus simply*[^p109-2]. The necessary *outside every genus simply*, then, is in God alone, who is above and outside every genus of being, because He cannot be in a genus, neither *through Himself* nor *by appropriation;* and such a necessary precedes and surpasses all demonstration, because it can have no definition whatever[^p109-3]. — As to what is said, then, that everything necessary is a principle of demonstration or something demonstrated, it must be said that this is understood of the necessary which is *of a determinate genus*, or which *can be appropriated* to a *determinate genus*, as are the common conceptions of the mind, which, inasmuch as they are appropriated, enter into demonstration. Whence that reasoning does not conclude that the divine *being* is necessary as restricted to a determinate genus.

8. To the objection that the more necessary a thing is, the more certain it is, it must be said that a thing is said to be more certain or more known than another in two ways: either *simply*, or *as regards us*[^p109-4]. The first proposition, then, holds true inasmuch as it is understood of greater certitude *simply*, but not inasmuch as it is understood of certitude *as regards us*, for the reason that the things which are most certain and most lucid in themselves seem obscure to us on account of a defect on the part of the created understanding; and therefore the Philosopher[^p109-5] says that "as the eye of the owl is to the light, so is our eye to the most manifest things of nature." Nevertheless, although the divine *being* in the fullness of its clarity is hidden from us so long as we are in the state of wayfarers, it is most certain and indubitable to us that God is, as is manifestly clear from the preceding questions[^p109-6].

9. To the objection that the necessary is not to be feared, just as neither is the impossible to be desired, it must be said that the necessary in the *event* or in the effect is not to be dreaded, because dread profits nothing where it is necessary that things so come about; but the necessary according to an *intrinsic state*, which can without change of itself render everlasting glory or everlasting punishment — this, I say, is most to be dreaded, both for acquiring grace and for avoiding punishment, because such a necessary does not exclude the liberality of will and the justice of equity, as will appear better in the following question.
