### Capitulum IX. *De productione hominis quantum ad spiritum.*

Post naturam corpoream et incorpoream dicenda sunt aliqua de natura ex utrisque[^p226-6] composita, primo ex parte *mentis*, secundo ex parte *carnis*, tertio ex parte *totius hominis*.

De *anima* igitur *rationali* haec in summa tenenda sunt secundum sacram doctrinam, scilicet quod ipsa est forma *ens*, *vivens*, *intelligens* et *libertate utens*. Forma quidem *ens* non a se ipsa nec de divina natura, sed a Deo de nihilo per creationem in *esse* deducta. — Forma autem *vivens*; non ex natura extrinseca, sed se ipsa; non vita mortali, sed vita perpetua. — Forma vero *intelligens* non tantum creatam, sed etiam « creatricem essentiam[^p226-7] », ad cuius imaginem facta est per memoriam, intelligentiam et voluntatem. — Forma quoque *libertate utens*, quia semper est libera a *coactione*; a *miseria* vero et *culpa* libera fuit in statu innocentiae, licet non in statu naturae lapsae; haec autem libertas a *coactione* nihil aliud est quam facultas voluntatis et rationis, quae sunt potentiae animae principales[^p226-8].

Ratio autem ad intelligentiam praedictorum haec est: quia, cum primum principium sit *beatissimum* et *benevolentissimum*; ideo sua summa benevolentia beatitudinem suam communicat creaturae, non tantum *spirituali* et proximae, sed etiam *corporali* et longinquae. *Corporali* tamen et longinquae communicat *mediate*, quia « lex divinitatis haec est, ut infima per media reducantur ad summa[^p226-9] ». Et ideo non tantum spiritum angelicum et separatum fecit beatificabilem, sed etiam spiritum coniunctum, sci-

<!-- page 227 -->

licet humanum. Est igitur anima rationalis *forma beatificabilis*. Et quia ad beatitudinis praemium pervenire non est gloriosum nisi per *meritum*; nec mereri *contingit*, nisi in eo quod voluntarie et libere fit: ideo oportuit, animae rationali dari *libertatem arbitrii* per remotionem omnis *coactionis*; quia hoc est de natura voluntatis, ut nullatenus possit *cogi*, licet per culpam *misera* efficiatur et serva peccati[^p227-1].

Rursus, quia forma beatificabilis est capax Dei per memoriam, intelligentiam et voluntatem; et hoc est esse ad imaginem Trinitatis propter unitatem in essentia et trinitatem in potentiis: ideo animam necesse fuit esse *intelligentem* Deum et omnia, ac per hoc Dei imagine insignitam. — Et quia nihil beatum potest beatitudinem amittere, nihil poterat esse beatificabile, nisi esset incorruptibile et immortale; necesse fuit, animam rationalem immortali vita de sui natura esse viventem.

Postremo, quia omne, quod ab alio beatificabile est et immortale, est mutabile secundum *bene esse* et incorruptibile secundum *esse*; ideo anima nec *a se* est nec *de divina natura*, quia *mutabilis*; nec *producta de aliquo* nec *per naturam generata*, quia est *immortalis* et incorruptibilis; et ita haec forma non potest per generationem in *esse* introduci, quia omne naturaliter generabile est naturaliter corruptibile[^p227-2]. — Ex his apparet, qualiter finis beatitudinis necessitatem imponit praedictarum conditionum ipsi animae ad beatitudinem ordinatae.

Quoniam autem ut beatificabilis est immortalis; ideo, cum unitur mortali corpori, potest ab eo separari; ac per hoc non tantum *forma* est, verum etiam *hoc aliquid*[^p227-3]; et ideo non tantum unitur corpori ut *perfectio*, verum etiam ut *motor*; et sic *perficit* per essentiam, quod *movet* pariter per potentiam. Et quoniam ipsa non tantum dat *esse*, verum etiam *vivere* et *sentire* et *intelligere*; ideo potentiam habet *vegetativam*, *sensitivam* et *intellectivam*, ita quod per potentiam *vegetativam* generat, nutrit et augmentat: generat ut *quid*, nutrit ut *quale*, augmentat ut *quantum*[^p227-4]. — Per *sensitivam* apprehendit sensibilia, retinet apprehensa, componit et dividit retenta: *apprehendit* quidem per sensitivam exteriorem quinquepartitam secundum correspondentiam ad quinque mundi corpora principalia[^p227-5]; *retinet* per memoriam, *componit* et *dividit* per phantasiam, quae est prima virtus collativa. — Per *intellectivam* autem *discernit* verum, *refugit* malum et *appetit* bonum: verum quidem *discernit* per rationalem, malum *repellit* per irascibilem, bonum *appetit* per concupiscibilem[^p227-6].

Rursus, quia *discretio veri* est cognitio, *fuga* et *appetitus* est affectio; ideo tota anima dividitur in *cognitivam* et *affectivam*.

Amplius, quoniam *cognitio* veri est duplex: vel veri ut *veri*, vel veri ut *boni*; et hoc vel *aeterni*, quod est supra animam, vel *temporalis*, quod est infra: hinc est, quod potentia cognitiva, utpote intellectus et ratio, dividitur ita, quod *intellectus* in speculativum et practicum, *ratio* in superiorem portionem et inferiorem; quae potius nominant diversa *officia* quam diversas *potentias*[^p227-7].

Postremo, quoniam *appetitus* dupliciter potest ad aliquid ferri, scilicet secundum *naturalem instinctum*, vel secundum *deliberationem* et arbitrium; hinc est, quod potentia *affectiva* dividitur in voluntatem *naturalem* et voluntatem *electivam*, quae proprie *voluntas* dicitur[^p227-8]. Et quoniam talis electio indifferens est ad utramque partem, ideo est a *libero arbitrio*. — Et quia haec indifferentia consurgit ex deliberatione praeambula et voluntate adiuncta; hinc est, quod *liberum arbitrium* est facultas rationis et voluntatis; ita quod, sicut dicit Augustinus, omnes praedictas rationales potentias comprehendit. Ait enim[^p227-9]: « Cum de libero arbitrio loquimur, non de parte animae loquimur, sed certe de tota ». Ex concursu enim illarum potentiarum, rationis supra se ipsam redeuntis et voluntatis concomitantis, consurgit integritas libertatis, quae est principium meriti, vel demeriti, secundum electionem boni, vel mali.

## English

### Chapter IX. *On the production of man as regards the spirit.*

After the corporeal and the incorporeal nature, some things are to be said about the nature composed of both[^p226-6] — first on the side of the *mind*, second on the side of the *flesh*, third on the side of *the whole man*.

Concerning the *rational soul*, then, these things are to be held in sum according to sacred doctrine: namely, that it is a form *being*, *living*, *understanding* and *using freedom*. A form indeed *being*, not from itself nor of the divine nature, but brought into *being* by God from nothing through creation. — A form, moreover, *living*; not from an extrinsic nature, but by itself; not with a mortal life, but with a perpetual life. — A form indeed *understanding* not only a created but also « a creating essence[^p226-7] », to whose image it was made through memory, intelligence and will. — A form likewise *using freedom*, because it is always free from *coercion*; from *misery* and *guilt*, however, it was free in the state of innocence, though not in the state of fallen nature; and this freedom from *coercion* is nothing other than the faculty of will and of reason, which are the principal powers of the soul[^p226-8].

Now the reason for the understanding of the foresaid is this: that since the first principle is *most blessed* and *most benevolent*, therefore by its supreme benevolence it communicates its own beatitude to the creature — not only to the *spiritual* and near creature, but also to the *corporeal* and far. To the *corporeal* and far, however, it communicates *mediately*, because « this is the law of the Godhead, that the lowest be led back to the highest through the middle[^p226-9] ». And therefore it made beatifiable not only the angelic and separated spirit, but also the conjoined spirit, that

<!-- page 227 -->

is, the human. The rational soul, therefore, is a *beatifiable form*. And because to arrive at the reward of beatitude is not glorious except through *merit*, and meriting does not *befall* except in that which is done voluntarily and freely: therefore it was fitting that *freedom of choice* be given to the rational soul by the removal of all *coercion*; for this belongs to the nature of the will, that it can in no way be *coerced*, although through guilt it be made *wretched* and a slave of sin[^p227-1].

Again, because a beatifiable form is capable of God through memory, intelligence and will — and this is to be to the image of the Trinity, on account of the unity in essence and the trinity in powers: therefore it was necessary that the soul be *understanding* God and all things, and through this stamped with the image of God. — And because nothing blessed can lose beatitude, nothing could be beatifiable unless it were incorruptible and immortal; it was necessary that the rational soul be living, of its own nature, with an immortal life.

Lastly, because everything that is beatifiable and immortal from another is mutable as regards *well-being* and incorruptible as regards *being*: therefore the soul neither is *from itself* nor is *of the divine nature*, because it is *mutable*; nor is it *produced from something* nor *generated through nature*, because it is *immortal* and incorruptible; and thus this form cannot be introduced into *being* through generation, because everything naturally generable is naturally corruptible[^p227-2]. — From these things it appears how the end of beatitude imposes the necessity of the foresaid conditions upon the soul itself, which is ordered to beatitude.

But since, as beatifiable, it is immortal, therefore, when it is united to a mortal body, it can be separated from it; and through this it is not only a *form*, but also *a this-something*[^p227-3]; and therefore it is united to the body not only as its *perfection*, but also as its *mover*; and so it perfects through essence that which it likewise moves through power. And since it gives not only *being*, but also *living* and *sensing* and *understanding*, therefore it has a *vegetative*, a *sensitive* and an *intellective* power — in such a way that through the *vegetative* power it generates, nourishes and increases: it generates as to *what*, nourishes as to *of what sort*, increases as to *how much*[^p227-4]. — Through the *sensitive* it apprehends sensible things, retains the apprehended, composes and divides the retained: it *apprehends* indeed through the exterior sensitive power, which is fivefold according to a correspondence to the five principal bodies of the world[^p227-5]; it *retains* through memory, it *composes* and *divides* through the imagination, which is the first collative power. — Through the *intellective*, however, it *discerns* the true, *flees* the evil and *seeks* the good: the true indeed it *discerns* through the rational [power], the evil it *repels* through the irascible, the good it *seeks* through the concupiscible[^p227-6].

Again, because the *discernment of the true* is cognition, and *flight* and *appetite* are affection: therefore the whole soul is divided into the *cognitive* and the *affective*.

Further, since the *cognition* of the true is twofold — either of the true as *true*, or of the true as *good*; and this either of the *eternal*, which is above the soul, or of the *temporal*, which is below — hence it is that the cognitive power, namely intellect and reason, is divided thus: that the *intellect* [is divided] into speculative and practical, and *reason* into a higher portion and a lower; which rather name diverse *offices* than diverse *powers*[^p227-7].

Lastly, since *appetite* can be borne toward something in two ways, namely according to *natural instinct*, or according to *deliberation* and choice: hence it is that the *affective* power is divided into the *natural* will and the *elective* will, which is properly called *will*[^p227-8]. And since such an election is indifferent to either side, therefore it is from *free choice*. — And because this indifference arises from a preambular deliberation and a will adjoined, hence it is that *free choice* is a faculty of reason and of will; in such a way that, as Augustine says, it comprehends all the foresaid rational powers. For he says[^p227-9]: « When we speak of free choice, we do not speak of a part of the soul, but assuredly of the whole ». For from the concurrence of those powers — of reason returning upon itself and of will accompanying it — there arises the integrity of freedom, which is the principle of merit, or of demerit, according to the election of good, or of evil.
