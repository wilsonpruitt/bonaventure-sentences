## Latin
<!-- page 94 -->

## DUBIA CIRCA LITTERAM MAGISTRI.

### Dub. I.

In parte ista sunt dubitationes circa litteram, et primo quaeritur de hoc quod dicitur, quod caro illa, antequam esset Verbo unita, fuit peccato obnoxia in Maria. Videtur enim falsum, quia non est peccatum nisi in anima, vel ratione animae; sed caro illa non fuit ante animata quam Verbo unita: ergo non fuit peccato obnoxia. — Item, beata Virgo ante conceptionem Filii Dei fuit sanctificata[^1]: ergo caro eius non fuit peccato obnoxia.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod, sicut tactum fuit in praecedentibus[^2], aliquando peccatum dicitur ipsa poena vel sequela. Dico ergo, quod caro non dicitur obnoxia peccato ratione culpae, sed ratione foeditatis, quae est culpae causa, et ratione passibilitatis, quae est culpae poena; et utroque modo fuit peccato obnoxia in Maria ante sanctificationem; secundo modo postea[^3]. Et sic patent illa duo.

### Dub. II.

Item quaeritur de hoc quod dicitur, quod primitiam nostrae massae dicitur Christus assumsisse. Videtur hoc esse falsum, quia, cum incarnatus sit in fine saeculorum, potius videtur assumsisse reliquias nostrae massae quam primitiam.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod primitiam nostrae massae dicitur Christus assumsisse triplici ratione. *(Triplex ratio.)* Nam primitia dicitur ab eo quod est primum; primum autem dicitur aliquid et secundum ordinem temporis et secundum ordinem dignitatis[^4]. *(Distinctio.)* — *(Ratio 1.)* Quantum ad ordinem dignitatis non est dubium, quin Christus assumserit nostrae carnis primitiam, pro <!-- page 95 --> eo quod eius caro excedit omnem dignitatem carnis humanae[^5]. — Secundum autem quod primitia dicitur a prioritate secundum tempus, dicitur Christus assumsisse primitiam nostrae massae non per prioritatem, sed per conformitatem et similitudinem. *(Duplex distinctio.)* Duplex est autem ratio conformitatis ibi: una quantum ad qualitatem, alia quantum ad virtutem. — *(Ratio 2.)* Quantum ad qualitatem, quia, sicut caro, quae in principio fuit creata, caruit omnis vitii corruptela; sic et caro a Verbo assumta[^6]. — *(Ratio 3.)* Quantum vero ad virtutem est similitudo, quia in his quae primo nascuntur, intensior est naturae vigor, ut ad maturitatem pertingant; et talia dicuntur primitia, id est primi fructus, qui debebant offerri Domino[^7]; talis autem fuit caro Christi. — Et sic patet, pro quanto dicatur primitia, tum propter dignitatem, tum propter munditiae qualitatem, tum propter virtutis vigorem, propter quem ceteros praecellit, prius pertingens ad maturitatem perfectionis et dulcedinis gloriam; et pro tanto dicitur[^8] assumsisse primitiam nostrae massae. — Et per hoc patet determinatio rationis praedictae; procedit enim de primitia, secundum quod dicitur a prioritate originis.

### Dub. III.

Item quaeritur de hoc quod dicit: *Sola illius non est caro peccati, quia non eum mater concupiscentia, sed gratia concepit.* Si enim concupiscentia est vitium et defectus, non ergo est generationis principium. Nihil ergo est dictum, cum ait: non eum mater concupiscentia concepit.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod littera ista dupliciter potest intelligi: *(Interpretatio 1.)* uno modo, quod concupiscentia sit nominativi casus, et sic[^9] constructio appositiva; et tunc negat Magister, quod Christum non concepit concupiscentia, sed gratia tanquam mater, pro eo quod concupiscentia locum tenet matris in generatione filiorum peccati, secundum illud Ezechielis[^10]: *Pater tuus Amorrhaeus, et mater tua Cethaea,* ut per Amorrhaeum intelligamus diabolum, et per Cethaeam concupiscentiam. — Sed gratia econtra est mater in generatione spirituali; et quia Christus non habuit peccatum in generatione, sed gratiam, ideo potius dicitur mater gratia quam concupiscentia.

*(Interpretatio 2. et melior.)* Sed licet isto modo[^11] posset intelligi, tamen est alius modus magis consonus veritati, ut hoc quod est concupiscentia et gratia sit ablativi casus et construatur cum hoc verbo *concepit* non secundum habitudinem causae, sed secundum habitudinem concomitantiae; et hoc quod dicit *mater,* supponat pro Virgine Maria, et est sensus: *Mater,* id est Virgo Maria, non concepit, concupiscentia concomitante, sed concomitante gratia; et tunc non notatur, quod concupiscentia sit alicuius conceptionis principium, sed vitium concomitans. — Et patet responsio ad obiecta[^12].

### Dub. IV.

Item quaeritur de hoc quod dicit: *Totum Verbum incarnatum est.* Si enim totum est quod habet partem et partem[^13], et Verbum simplicissimum est, male dicit, quod totum incarnatum est.

**Respondeo:** Dicendum, quod *totum* potest accipi privative, vel positive. *(Distinctio.)* Si privative; tunc Verbum dicitur incarnatum totum, quia non habet partes, secundum quas incarnari habeat. Alio modo dicitur positive; et tunc dicitur dupliciter: *(Subdistinctio.)* quia totum idem est quod ex suis partibus constitutum[^14]; alio modo idem est quod perfectum. Et secundum quod totum dicit integritatis totalitatem, sic praedictus sermo non intelligitur, sed secundum quod dicit totalitatem perfectionis, et secundum quod dicit privationem partis et partis; et sic patet, quod illa obiectio non procedit recte propter diversam acceptionem huius vocabuli, quod est *totum.* Sed hoc melius in primo libro, distinctione decima nona[^15] invenitur determinatum.

## English

## DOUBTS CONCERNING THE TEXT OF THE MASTER.

### Doubt I.

In this part there are doubts concerning the text, and first it is asked about what is said: that that flesh, before it was united to the Word, was subject to sin in Mary. For it seems false, because there is no sin except in the soul, or by reason of the soul; but that flesh was not animated before it was united to the Word: therefore it was not subject to sin. — Likewise, the blessed Virgin was sanctified[^1] before the conception of the Son of God: therefore her flesh was not subject to sin.

**I respond:** It must be said that, as was touched upon in what precedes[^2], sometimes sin is said [to be] the penalty or consequence itself. I say, therefore, that the flesh is not called subject to sin by reason of fault, but by reason of foulness, which is the cause of fault, and by reason of passibility, which is the penalty of fault; and in both ways it was subject to sin in Mary before her sanctification; in the second way afterward[^3]. And thus those two things are clear.

### Doubt II.

Likewise it is asked about what is said: that Christ is said to have assumed the first-fruits of our mass. This seems to be false, because, since he was incarnate in the end of the ages, he seems rather to have assumed the remnants of our mass than the first-fruits.

**I respond:** It must be said that Christ is said to have assumed the first-fruits of our mass for a threefold reason. *(Threefold reason.)* For "first-fruits" (*primitia*) is so called from that which is first; but a thing is said to be first both according to the order of time and according to the order of dignity[^4]. *(A distinction.)* — *(First reason.)* As to the order of dignity, there is no doubt that Christ assumed the first-fruits of our flesh, on account of the fact that his flesh exceeds every dignity of human flesh[^5]. — But according as "first-fruits" is said from priority according to time, Christ is said to have assumed the first-fruits of our mass not by priority, but by conformity and likeness. *(A twofold distinction.)* And there is here a twofold ground of conformity: one as to quality, the other as to power. — *(Second reason.)* As to quality, because, just as the flesh which was created in the beginning lacked all corruption of vice, so also the flesh assumed by the Word[^6]. — *(Third reason.)* But as to power there is a likeness, because in those things which are first born the vigor of nature is more intense, so that they attain to maturity; and such are called first-fruits, that is, the first fruits which were to be offered to the Lord[^7]; and such was the flesh of Christ. — And thus it is clear in what respect it is called first-fruits: both on account of dignity, and on account of the quality of purity, and on account of the vigor of power, on account of which it excels the rest, attaining first to the maturity of perfection and to the glory of sweetness; and to that extent it is said[^8] to have assumed the first-fruits of our mass. — And by this the determination of the aforesaid argument is clear; for it proceeds from "first-fruits" according as it is said from priority of origin.

### Doubt III.

Likewise it is asked about what he says: *Of her alone is the flesh not [the flesh] of sin, because concupiscence did not conceive him as mother, but grace.* For if concupiscence is a vice and a defect, it is therefore not the principle of generation. Nothing therefore has been said, when he says: concupiscence did not conceive him as mother.

**I respond:** It must be said that this text can be understood in two ways: *(First interpretation.)* in one way, that *concupiscence* is in the nominative case, and thus[^9] the construction is appositional; and then the Master denies that concupiscence, as mother, did not conceive Christ, but grace [did], for the reason that concupiscence holds the place of mother in the generation of the children of sin, according to that saying of Ezechiel[^10]: *Thy father was an Amorrhite, and thy mother a Cethite,* so that by the Amorrhite we understand the devil, and by the Cethite concupiscence. — But grace, on the contrary, is mother in spiritual generation; and because Christ had not sin in his generation, but grace, therefore grace is rather called mother than concupiscence.

*(Second and better interpretation.)* But although it can be understood in this way[^11], yet there is another way more in harmony with the truth: that this [phrase], "concupiscence" and "grace," be in the ablative case and be construed with this verb *conceived* not according to the relation of cause, but according to the relation of concomitance; and that what he says, *mother,* stand for the Virgin Mary, and the sense is: *Mother,* that is, the Virgin Mary, did not conceive with concupiscence accompanying, but with grace accompanying; and then it is not signified that concupiscence is the principle of any conception, but a vice accompanying [it]. — And the response to the objections is clear[^12].

### Doubt IV.

Likewise it is asked about what he says: *The whole Word was made flesh.* For if a whole is that which has part and part[^13], and the Word is most simple, he speaks ill in saying that the whole was made flesh.

**I respond:** It must be said that "whole" can be taken privatively, or positively. *(A distinction.)* If privatively, then the Word is said to be incarnate as a whole, because it does not have parts according to which it would be capable of being made flesh. In another way it is said positively; and then it is said in two ways: *(A subdistinction.)* because a whole is the same as that which is constituted of its parts[^14]; in another way it is the same as the perfect. And according as "whole" signifies the totality of integrity, the aforesaid statement is not understood [thus], but according as it signifies the totality of perfection, and according as it signifies the privation of part and part; and thus it is clear that that objection does not proceed rightly, on account of the diverse acceptation of this word "whole." But this is found determined better in the first book, distinction nineteen[^15].
