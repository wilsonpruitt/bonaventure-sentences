## English

DIST. VIII. P. I. ART. I. QUAEST. I.

2. Likewise, the true is equally common as the good, and in a certain way more so. But goodness is not a property of God [alone]; therefore, by parity of reasoning, neither is truth.

3. Likewise, truth is the ground for discerning the Creator from creatures and one creature from another. But what is the ground for discerning and distinguishing is diverse in diverse things. Therefore truth in God is other than in creatures, and in one creature other than in another — therefore it is not proper to God alone.

4. Likewise, Augustine in the *Soliloquia*: "The true is that which is." Therefore truth is entity itself; therefore it is not a property of the [divine] essence, because if so, then by the same reasoning by which it is said "truth is a property of the essence," the converse could be said — since they are entirely the same.

CONCLUSION.

*Truth, insofar as it is opposed to falsity, is found also in creatures according to its threefold comparison: to the subject that it informs, to the principle that it represents, and to the intellect that it arouses. But insofar as it is opposed to admixture, it is a property of God alone.*

I respond: It must be said that truth has a threefold comparison. For it can be compared to the subject that it informs, to the principle that it represents, and to the intellect that it arouses. In comparison to its subject, truth is called the indivision of act and potency. In comparison to its principle, truth is called the representation or imitation of the supreme unity and the first [principle]. In comparison to the intellect, truth is called the ground of discernment.

And in all these comparisons, truth can be taken in two ways: in one way, insofar as it is distinguished against falsity; in another way, insofar as it is distinguished against admixture — according to which the true is said to be pure and unmixed.

Insofar as truth is divided against falsity — which is the privation of indivision, of imitation, and of expression — then, since in a creature there is found in some way both indivision, imitation, and expression, truth is not only in the Creator but also in creatures; and thus it is not assigned as a property of God.

But in the other way, insofar as truth is divided against admixture or impurity, it exists in God alone. For in God alone is there pure indivision not mixed with any diversity; in God alone is there pure imitation and likeness not mixed with any dissimilitude; and in God alone is there an expression of light not mixed with obscurity. But in a creature there is indivision with diversity of act and potency, and imitation with dissimilitude; furthermore, there is in it expression with obscurity. And therefore in this way truth is a property of divine being; and this is how the Master and Augustine and Jerome take it. For they call *true being* that which has nothing of possibility, nothing of vanity, nothing of non-entity. And therefore in God there falls neither past nor future, which are in some way non-beings.

1, 2, 5, 6. And thus the first two authorities and arguments proceed [correctly].

3. To the objection that there is no truth other than the eternal, because that alone causes understanding: it must be said that just as color is the object of sight and what moves sight — yet not without the act of light — and differs from light itself, so it must be said that created truth, although it cannot move [the intellect] without uncreated truth, is nonetheless motive in its own way and is a truth other than that [uncreated truth].

4 and 7. To the objection from Anselm, that all things are true by the first truth: it must be said that *true*, by its primary imposition, designates a comparison to the exemplary cause, just as *good* [designates a comparison] to the final cause. For just as a thing is said to be good by reason of order, so it is said to be true by reason of expression; and the ground of expressing belongs to the exemplar itself. Just as, therefore, when it is said of created goods that they are good by the uncreated goodness, *goodness* in the ablative predicates the end, not the form — because the goodness of God is not the form of any creature — similarly, when it is said that all things are true by the uncreated truth, the ablative designates the formal exemplary cause. For all things are true and are apt to express themselves through the expression of that supreme light; and if it were to cease influencing, the rest would cease to be true. Therefore no created truth is true through its essence, but through participation; and through this the last [objection] is clear.

To the objection to the contrary — that [truth] is not a property — the response is clear: because

[p. 152]

the objection concerns truth insofar as it is opposed to the defect of falsity, not to the admixture of possibility; for in the former way it belongs not only to the Creator but also to the creature.

4. To the objection that truth is the same as the divine essence: it must be said that in creatures, the account of a *property* includes these three things: first, that it belongs to [something] alone; second, that it is a ground of making known; third, that it differs from that of which it is a property. The first two belong to perfection; the last, however, belongs to imperfection, because it excludes simplicity. And therefore truth is said to be a property of the divine essence not because it differs from or inheres in the divine essence as an accident in a subject, but because it belongs to God alone and is a ground of knowing him. And this is the reason why, conversely, essence is not a property of truth — because it is not a ground of making truth known, as it is the other way around.

But if you ask, since *essence* and *truth* signify the same thing, why one is the ground of knowing the other and not conversely: to this some have said that although essence and truth signify the same thing, one — namely, truth — is more a ground of knowing by reason of what it connotes. But this cannot stand, because truth connotes nothing. It must be said, therefore, that this is by reason of the mode of signifying and understanding.

And it should be noted that these names can be spoken of in two ways: in one way, by reason of what they name or signify; in another way, by reason of that in which what they name is signified as posited. Hence it is different to say "sense" and "the sense of a human being," because in the first way it is said commonly of the sense of a human being and of a brute, but in the second way it is proper to a human being. If, therefore, we take names of this kind according to themselves, the account of *essence* is other than that of *truth* — since essence designates *what [something is]*, and truth a condition of being. But when we draw essence and truth to God, although they are the same, nevertheless by reason of their general significations, one is taken as a property of the other. And there is no synonymy there, nor are they synonymous names; rather, the relation of subject and property remains according to the mode of signifying, and remains also according to the mode of understanding — because through essence in a creature we understand essence in the Creator, and through the truth of a creature we understand uncreated truth. Hence, just as created truth is a property and ground of knowing created essence, so uncreated truth, according to the mode of signifying and understanding, is a ground of knowing and understanding uncreated essence.

## Notes

- The chunk begins mid-argument (opening arguments 1 and the beginning of 2 lost to OCR damage at top of page)
- This is a central text for Bonaventure's metaphysics of truth — truth defined through three relations (to subject, principle, intellect) and two oppositions (falsity, admixture)
- Key principle: truth *as opposed to admixture* (*permixtio*) belongs to God alone because only in God is there pure actuality with no admixture of potentiality, pure likeness with no dissimilitude, pure light with no obscurity
- The distinction between truth *contra falsitatem* (also in creatures) and truth *contra permixtionem* (God alone) is fundamental
- The lengthy discussion of whether "essence" and "truth" are synonymous in God deploys the scholastic distinction between *modus significandi* (mode of signifying) and the thing signified — they name the same reality but differ in their mode of signification
- "No created truth is true through its essence, but through participation" — classic Bonaventurean exemplarism/participation metaphysics
- OCR artifacts: `\RT.` for ART., `Respondko` for *Respondeo*, `sental` for *sentat*, `essa` for *esse*, broken marginal glosses inline (`conciusio 2.`, `Deargg.`, `Qnaestio in-`)
