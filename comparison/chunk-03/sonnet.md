2. Likewise, the true is equally common as the good, and in a certain way more so; but goodness is not a property of God: therefore by the same reasoning neither is truth.

3. Likewise, truth is the ground of discerning the Creator from the creature and one creature from another; but what is the ground of discerning and distinguishing is different in different things: therefore the truth in God is other than that in the creature, and the truth in one creature is other than that in another: therefore it is not proper to God alone.

4. Likewise, Augustine in the *Soliloquies*: "The true is that which is"; therefore truth is being itself: therefore it is not proper to the essence, because if it were, by the same reasoning that one says: truth is a property of essence, one could say the converse, since they are entirely the same.

CONCLUSIO.

Truth, insofar as it is opposed to falsity, is found also in creatures according to its threefold relation — to the subject it informs, to the principle it represents, and to the intellect it arouses; but insofar as it is opposed to admixture, it is a property of God alone.

I respond: It must be said that truth has a threefold relation. For it can be related to the subject it informs, to the principle it represents, and to the intellect it arouses. In relation to the subject, truth is called the indivision of act and potency. In relation to the principle, truth is called the representation or imitation of the highest unity. In relation to the intellect, truth is called the ground of discernment.

And in all these relations truth can be taken in two ways: in one way, insofar as it is distinguished against falsity; in another way, insofar as it is distinguished against admixture, according to which the true is called pure and unmixed.

Insofar as truth is divided against falsity — which is the privation of indivision, imitation, and expression — since in the creature there is to be found in some manner both indivision and imitation and expression, truth exists not only in the Creator but also in the creature; and in this way it is not assigned as a property of God.

In the other way, insofar as truth is divided against admixture or impurity, it exists in God alone. For in God alone is there pure indivision, unmixed with any diversity; in God alone is there pure imitation and likeness unmixed with any dissimilitude; and in God alone is there expression of light unmixed with darkness. In the creature, however, there is indivision together with the diversity of act and potency, and imitation together with dissimilitude; and further, in the creature there is expression together with obscurity. And therefore in this way truth is a property of the divine being; and in this sense the Master and Augustine and Jerome take it. For they call true being that which has nothing of potency, nothing of vanity, nothing of non-being. And therefore in God there is no past and no future, which are in some manner non-beings.

1, 2, 3, 6. And thus the two first authorities and arguments proceed from the affirmative side.

3. To what is objected — that there is no truth other than the eternal, because only it causes understanding — it must be said that just as color is the object of sight and what moves sight — yet not without the act of light — and differs from light itself; so it must be said that created truth, although it cannot move without uncreated truth, is nonetheless motive in its own way and is a truth other than that one.

4. and 7. To what is objected from Anselm, that all things are true by the first truth, it must be said that the true, by its original imposition, expresses a relation to the exemplar cause, just as the good expresses a relation to the final cause. For just as something is called good by reason of order, so it is called true by reason of expression; and the ground of expressing belongs to the exemplar itself. Just as therefore, when it is said of created goods that they are good by uncreated goodness, goodness predicates the end in the ablative and not the form — since God's goodness is the form of no created thing — so similarly, when it is said that all things are true by uncreated truth, the ablative expresses the exemplar formal cause. For all things are true and are naturally disposed to express themselves through the expression of that highest light; and if it were to cease to flow in, the rest would cease to be true. Therefore no created truth is true through essence, but through participation; and through this the last point is clear.

To what is objected on the contrary side — that it is not a property — the response is clear: for the objection concerns truth insofar as it has opposition to the defect of falsity, not to the admixture of potency; for in the aforesaid way it belongs not only to the Creator but also to the creature.

4. To what is objected — that truth is identical with the divine essence — it must be said that of the account of a property in the creature there are three things: the first is that it belongs to it alone; the second is that it is the ground of being known; the third is that it differs from that of which it is the property. The first two pertain to perfection, but the last pertains to imperfection, because it excludes simplicity. And therefore truth is called a property of the divine essence, not because it differs from or inheres in the divine essence as an accident in a subject, but because it belongs to it alone and is the ground of knowing it. And this is why conversely essence is not a property of truth, because it is not the ground of being known by it, as truth is by essence.

But if you ask, since essence and truth signify the same thing, why one is the ground of knowing the other and not conversely — to this some have said that although essence and truth signify the same thing, nonetheless one, namely truth, is more the ground of knowing by reason of what it connotes. But this cannot stand, because truth connotes nothing. It must therefore be said that this is by reason of the mode of signifying and understanding.

And it is to be noted that there are two ways of speaking about these names: in one way by reason of what they name or signify; in another way by reason of that in which what they name is signified as placed. Whence it makes a difference to say sense and the sense of man; because in the first case what is said is common to the sense of man and of beast, in the second it is proper to man. If therefore we take names of this kind according to themselves, the account of essence is one thing and the account of truth is another, since essence says what a thing is, and truth says a condition of being. But when we draw essence and truth toward God, although they are the same, nonetheless by reason of their general significations one is taken as a property of the other; and there is no synonymy there, nor are they synonymous names, but the account of subject and property remains according to the mode of signifying, and it remains also according to the mode of understanding; because through essence in the creature we understand essence in the Creator, and through the truth of the creature we understand uncreated truth. Whence just as created truth is the property and ground of knowing created essence, so uncreated truth according to the account of signifying and understanding is the ground of knowing and understanding uncreated essence.

QUESTION II.
Whether divine being is so true that it cannot be thought not to be.

Second, the question is raised whether this property belongs to God in the highest degree, that is, whether divine being is so true that it cannot be thought not to be.

1. And that it is, seems to be shown by Anselm, who says that God, according to the common conception of the mind, is that than which nothing greater can be thought; but that which cannot be thought not to be is greater than that which can: therefore since nothing greater than God can be thought, divine being is such that it cannot be thought not to be.

2. Likewise, Damascus says that knowledge of God's being is naturally impressed upon us; but natural impressions do not release or accustom themselves to the contrary: therefore the truth of God impressed upon the human mind is inseparable from it: therefore it cannot be thought not to be.

3. Likewise, there is greater truth in divine being than in any axiom; but a certain axiom is so true that there is no contradicting it in the heart, as for instance: every whole is greater than its part, and similar propositions; whence it cannot be thought not to be: therefore it will be much more true to say this of the first truth.

4. Likewise, our intellect understands nothing except through the first light and truth; therefore every act of the intellect, which consists in thinking something not to be, occurs through the first light; but through the first light it is not possible to think that the first light or truth is not: therefore in no way is it possible to think that the first truth is not.

5. Likewise, what can be thought can be stated; but it is not possible to state that the first truth is not: therefore neither can it be thought. Proof of the minor: every assertoric statement asserts itself to be true; whence it follows: if man is a donkey, then that man is a donkey is true; but everything that posits truth posits the first truth, because every truth entails it: therefore every statement asserts that the first truth is: therefore, etc.

6. Likewise, every assertoric statement is either affirmative or negative; but an affirmative one affirms this of this; but it follows: if there is a being that is this, there is a being that is not this, because when I say a being that is this, I say a being that is limited, finite, and constrained; and given such a being, a highest being is posited: therefore every affirmative statement about a creature entails God.

7. Likewise, similarly for a negative one, because no statement is more negative than this one: there is no truth. But Augustine in the *Soliloquies* proves that this statement posits that some truth exists; because if there is no truth, it is true that there is no truth; and if this is true, something is true; and if something is true, there is some truth: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. Damascus: "So greatly did the pernicious malice of men prevail, that some say God does not exist, according to that word of the Psalm: The fool said, etc."

2. Likewise, an idolater says that there is no God other than the idol, and believes and thinks this; but the idol manifestly is not God: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, whatever, once understood not to be, still leaves something able to be understood, can be thought not to be. But Boethius in the book *On the Hebdomads* says that, once it is understood by way of impossibility that the highest good is not, something can still be understood to be round and white: therefore similarly regarding the highest truth, and thus it will be possible to think it not to be.

4. Likewise, what most escapes our awareness can easily be thought not to be; but the truth of divine being is of this kind, since God dwells in unapproachable light [1 Tim. 6:16]: therefore, etc.

5. Likewise, I ask: what does it mean to say that God cannot be thought not to be? If it is because he cannot be thought in any way, whether truly or falsely, this is manifestly false; if it is because he cannot be thought truly, then similarly neither can the soul nor the heavens and things of that kind.

6. Likewise, what can be expressed can also be thought; but it can be expressed that divine being is not: therefore it can also be thought. That this is possible is evident when it is said in the particular: God is not, and in the general: nothing is; and that neither of these entails that God is, is evident, because the opposite does not entail the opposite, and what posits nothing entails nothing; but each of these posits nothing.

CONCLUSIO.

The truth of divine being is so great that it cannot be thought not to be with assent, except by reason of a defect on the part of the one understanding, who does not know what God is; but on the part of the intelligible itself there can be no defect, neither of presence nor of evidence, whether in itself or in being proved.

I respond: It must be said that to think something not to be is twofold. Either in the manner of the false, as when I think this proposition: man is a donkey; and to think in this way is nothing other than to understand what is said. In this way it is possible to think that divine being is not true.

In the other way, thinking occurs with assent, as when I think something not to be and believe it not to be; and in this way to think something not to be, which is, can arise either from a defect on the part of the one understanding or from a defect on the part of the intelligible.

The defect of the one understanding is blindness or ignorance, by reason of which, because one is ignorant of a thing, one thinks it not to be. Now there can be thought of a being in two ways: whether it is, and what it is.

Our intellect, however, fails in its cognition of divine truth with respect to knowledge of what it is, yet does not fail with respect to knowledge of whether it is. Whence Hugo: "God so tempered knowledge of himself in man from the beginning, that just as what he is could never be comprehended, so because he is could never be ignored." Because therefore our intellect never fails in knowledge of God as to whether he is, it therefore cannot be ignorant that he simply is, nor think that he is not.

But because it fails in knowledge of what he is, it therefore frequently thinks God to be what he is not — as an idol — or not to be what he is — as God who is just. And because one who thinks God not to be what he is — namely, just — consequently thinks him not to be, therefore by reason of the defect of the intellect God can be thought not to be, or the highest truth can; yet not simply or in a general way, but as a consequence, as one who denies that blessedness is in God thereby denies that he is.

Now in this way proceed the arguments proving that some intellect thinks, or can think, that divine being is not.

In another way something can be thought not to be by reason of a defect on the part of the intelligible, and a defect of this kind can be twofold: either a defect of presence or a defect of evidence. A defect of presence — for instance, because something does not exist always, or everywhere, or everywhere totally. What does not exist always, exists at some times and not others: and therefore it can sometimes truly be thought not to be. Similarly regarding what is not everywhere, because by the same reasoning by which it can be thought not to be here, it can be thought not to be elsewhere. Similarly regarding that which is present in part and absent in part.

But God is always and everywhere, and wholly always and everywhere: therefore he cannot be thought not to be. This argument is assigned by Anselm in the book against the fool.

### Notes

- **Chunk opening.** The text begins mid-quaestio (mid-sentence with objection 2 of Q. I). The preceding objection 1 and the argumenta in favor of the affirmative position are not included in this chunk; the translation begins where the Latin does.

- ***Dignitas*** (Q. II, arg. 3). A technical term meaning a self-evident or immediately known proposition — equivalent to the Greek *axioma*. The Quaracchi note cites Aristotle and Boethius on this sense. Translated as "axiom" throughout.

- **"Ablative of exemplar formal cause"** (*ablativus dicit causam formalem exemplarem*). Bonaventure's grammatical-metaphysical analysis of the phrase "all things are true by uncreated truth" — the ablative does not express the inherent form of creatures but the exemplar cause after which they are fashioned. The technical phrasing is preserved in translation.

- ***Hugo*** (Q. II, *Respondeo*). The citation is from Hugh of St. Victor, *De Sacramentis* I, p. III, c. 1. The Latin tag is translated as given; no source is added per the instructions.

- **"Whether it is / what it is"** (*si est / quid est*). A standard Aristotelian distinction from *Posterior Analytics* II.1, between existence-knowledge and quidditative knowledge. Preserved with the standard English rendering.

- ***Non esse quod est, ut iustum*** (near end of *Respondeo*). The example is that one who denies God is just thereby, in a derived sense, denies God exists — since God's justice is not an attribute separable from his being. The phrase *iustum* (just) is retained against the Quaracchi note flagging the Vat. reading *non iustum*, since the sense is equivalent and the manuscript tradition favors *iustum*.

- **Boethius, *De Hebdomadibus*** (obj. 3, *contra*). The argument is that even hypothetically understanding the highest good not to exist, one can still conceive of round or white things — implying the highest good's non-existence does not collapse all conceptual content, and therefore it is not inconceivable. Bonaventure's *Respondeo* will address this via the distinction between thinking-as-false and thinking-with-assent.
