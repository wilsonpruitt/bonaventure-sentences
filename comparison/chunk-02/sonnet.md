DIST. III. P. II. ART. II. QUAEST. II.

QUESTION II.
Whether mind, knowledge, and love have order, equality, and consubstantiality.

Second, the question is raised concerning these three in relation to one another, according to the threefold comparison that Augustine sets forth — namely, of order, equality, and consubstantiality — which the Master recites in the text. There is order among these, because mind is the parent, knowledge is the offspring, and love is the third, proceeding from both. There is also equality, because the mind knows itself as much as it is, and loves itself as much as it knows itself. There is also consubstantiality; whence Augustine in the ninth book of *On the Trinity*: "We are admonished, if we can see it in any way, that these exist substantially in the soul, not as in a subject, as color is in a body, or any quality or quantity; for whatever is such does not exceed the substance in which it is. But the mind, by the love with which it loves itself, can also love another"; and thus he holds that love is consubstantial with the mind.

1. But objections are raised against this. First, it seems that there is no order or origin among these. For either they are taken as innate habits or as acquired ones. If as innate, there is no order, because they exist simultaneously with the soul itself; if as acquired, then love precedes knowledge, for no one acquires or studies to learn something unless he loves to know. Whence Augustine at the end of the ninth book of *On the Trinity*: "An appetite precedes the birth of the mind, by which, through seeking and finding what we wish to know, there is born the offspring, which is knowledge itself." Therefore either there is no order, or it is not such an order.

2. Likewise, it seems that there is no equality there. For either knowledge and love are taken in comparison to lower things, or in comparison to the soul. If in comparison to lower things, it is manifest that there is no equality, for we know many things that we do not love; if in comparison to the soul, then either there is equality with respect to intensity or with respect to extension. With respect to extension, there is not — that is certain, since there is only one object there: therefore with respect to intensity; but that this is false seems evident, because since we know the soul to be less than God and greater than the body, it happens sometimes that we love it more than God and less than the body; and thus the quantity of love does not follow the quantity of knowledge.

3. Likewise, it seems that there is no consubstantiality there, because love and knowledge are habits and are qualities; therefore they seem to differ essentially from the mind itself.

4. Likewise, Augustine's argument is that they are not in the soul as accidents, because they extend outward; but this amounts to nothing, because accidents also extend outward, as heat does by heating and color by affecting vision. Furthermore, a man knows certain knowable things through acquired science, which is an accident, and it too extends outward.

CONCLUSIO.

Mind, knowledge, and love have order, equality, and consubstantiality.

I respond: It must be said that, as Augustine assigns, there is order, equality, and consubstantiality among these.

Now order is attended to in these connnatural habits of the soul in comparison to acts, just as order is posited in faith, hope, and charity, even though they are infused simultaneously.

1. And thus the answer to the objection is clear: there is no order in the habits themselves considered absolutely, but in relation to their acts.

Similarly, there is equality there according to the soul's conversion upon itself and the perfection of the said habits. Whence Augustine says in the ninth book of *On the Trinity* that there is no equality in these habits except insofar as they are perfect.

2. And thus the solution to what is objected concerning love is clear, because that love is not perfect love but libidinous and disordered. — Or it must be said that they are equal insofar as they are connnatural; for to whatever degree one is apt or ready to know oneself, to that same degree one is ready to love oneself; but this is not true of acquired habits, whether bad or good, and it is not meant of those.

Similarly, the third is present there, namely consubstantiality, because, as has been said above, love and knowledge are connnatural to the soul insofar as it turns upon itself; and thus they add absolutely nothing over and above the powers themselves. For by the fact that the soul is present to itself, it has knowledge; by the fact that it is one with itself, it has the habit of love: and therefore, just as the powers are consubstantial with the soul, as was seen above, so also are habits of this kind. Whence even if they seem to express the mode of a habit or quality, they nonetheless add nothing real over and above the powers.

3. And thus the response to the objection is clear: they are not qualities in that sense.

4. To what is objected concerning Augustine's argument, it must be said that this argument does not conclude principally that love or knowledge exist substantially in the soul — for if it did, by the same reasoning the same could be said and objected of every love — but it concludes consequentially, which is made clear as follows. For when love extends outside its subject by loving another, this occurs by virtue of substance, just as it does not exist through itself except through substance. If therefore love and knowledge extend by substantial virtue, and these are understanding and will; and the love by which the soul loves itself is identical with the will itself; and similarly the knowledge by which it knows is nothing other than understanding: it follows therefore that love and knowledge with respect to themselves are consubstantial with the mind itself.

QUESTION III.
Whether the trinity of the image, which consists in mind, knowledge, and love, necessarily leads to knowledge of the three divine persons.

Third and last, the question is raised whether this trinity of the image — namely of mind, knowledge, and love — necessarily leads to knowledge of the Trinity with respect to the persons. And it seems that it does:

1. Because in this trinity of the image there is relation; but there is no relation except with respect to persons: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, in this trinity there is distinction, because knowledge is not love; but in God there is no distinction except of persons: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, in this trinity there is the origin of one born from one and of a third from both: therefore, since these are proper to the persons, it is evident, etc.

4. Likewise, in this trinity there is love in the third place, which is proper to the Holy Spirit and which is toward another: therefore it seems that it necessarily leads to the Trinity of persons.

On the contrary: 1. This trinity is understood in the creature without personal distinction: therefore it can be understood in God as well; but this is false: therefore, etc.

2. Likewise, knowledge and love are in each of the persons; but through what is in all, one does not arrive at knowledge of personal distinction: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, even if it were understood that there were only one person, that person would still know and love itself: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, the philosophers knew this trinity, and yet did not know the Trinity of persons: therefore this does not necessarily lead to that.

CONCLUSIO.

Reason alone does not ascend from this trinity of mind, knowledge, and love to knowledge of the Trinity.

I respond: It must be said that through this trinity one arrives at knowledge of the most high Trinity by attributing to that highest Trinity what is in this trinity. But this can occur in two ways. For either these three can be attributed to God according to substance — so that by mind we understand mind in God, and by knowledge in the soul we understand knowledge in God, and so on for the third — and in this way it leads to knowledge of the Trinity only with respect to appropriations; and in this sense the philosophers understood it.

Or these three can be drawn toward God by reason of the properties, which are order and origin, distinction and relation; and in this way they lead to knowledge of the Trinity with respect to what is proper.

But to posit or understand these in God can be done by faith, but not by reason; and thus perfect knowledge of the image is had only through faith. Whence it must be granted that the image, perfectly known as image, leads to knowledge of the Trinity, but not absolutely. And through this both parts are evident.

1. 2. To what is objected concerning love, it must be said that love can express complacency, and in this sense it is common; or it can express connection or communion or gift, and in this sense it has the character of a person.

DUBIA CONCERNING THE TEXT OF PART II.

DUB. I.

In this part a question arises concerning what he says: *The image of God remains*. Against this: Psalm: *Lord, in the city you will reduce their image to nothing* [Ps. 72:20].

I respond: Image is said in two ways: with respect to substantial being — and this regards the trinity of the powers, and order and equality, and in this sense it always remains; and in another way insofar as it adds upon being a well-being, such as beauty and honor — and this can be lost, because "man, when he was in honor, did not understand" [Ps. 48:21].

DUB. II.

Likewise, a question arises concerning what he says: *Memory is said in relation to something*, etc.; therefore memory, understanding, and will are in the category of relation.

Likewise, the question is raised why mind is said to be more toward itself than memory or understanding.

I respond: It must be said that to be said in relation to something is twofold: either properly and through itself, as father and son, or by reason of something annexed, because it has an annexed respect and inclination; and in this way memory is said to be in relation to something, because it has an innate respect for what is memorable, similarly understanding toward what is intelligible and will toward what is willed; and these objects have a mutual relation. And thus the objection is clear.

To what is asked concerning mind, it must be said that mind is named from an essential act. Therefore it is to be understood that what gives being to the soul in the most general sense — and in this way it is called essence; or insofar as it gives generic being — and in this way it is called life, because the soul is in the genus of living things; or insofar as it gives spiritual being — and in this way it is called mind. For mind is called only what lives by intellectual life. — Or: the soul in itself is called essence, as act of the body is called life, as perfectible by God is called mind.

DUB. III.

Likewise, a question arises concerning what he says, that the three persons are not of one God; because this seems to say something false, for if they are of one essence, and essence is God, therefore they are of one God. If you say this does not follow, I ask why it is not granted that the three persons are of one God. If it is because the genitive denotes diversity, then since essence is not diverse from person, it cannot be said: the three persons are of one essence.

I respond: It must be said that the genitive is sometimes construed in the sense of possession, as when one says: the ox of Peter or of John; sometimes by the force of declaration of essence, as a woman of outstanding beauty; sometimes intransitively, as a creature of salt. Intransitively, a general term is construed with a specific one; and in this way one can say: substance or person of God. By the force of declaration of essence, a name importing form in the manner of form; and in this way the three persons are said to be of one essence. Because, therefore, when it is said: the three persons are of one God, *God* signifies neither in the manner of form nor in the manner of specifying, it is understood in the manner of possessor or originator: and therefore it is simply false.

DUB. IV.

Likewise, a question arises concerning what he says, that the image is for the most part dissimilar. It seems — since an image is an express likeness — that if it is for the most part dissimilar, it is not an image.

I respond: It must be said that expression is either simple or in a genus. If therefore we speak of expression simply, then I say that the rational soul is not greatly similar to God; but if we speak in the genus of creature, because it approaches as much as created nature can, in this way it is said to be greatly similar and an express likeness of God.

DUB. V.

Likewise, a question arises concerning what he says: *If there were two, either each would be insufficient or one would be superfluous*; because by this line of reasoning it could be shown that there is only one person.

I respond: It must be said that among the persons there cannot be superfluity, because in them there is one sufficiency; whence if one were superfluous, all would be. But there cannot be any insufficiency there, because three have no more than one. But if there were two essences, there would be two sufficiencies if each were sufficient through itself; but if one required the other, each would be insufficient: and thus it is clear that the case of two essences is not like that of two persons.

DUB. VI.

Likewise, a question arises concerning what he says, that the philosopher understood that God has a wisdom that is begotten from him, because he understood that God is not a foolish thing; therefore it seems, according to this, that the Father is wise by begotten wisdom.

I respond: It must be said that this inference is not to be understood immediately, namely: because he is not a foolish thing, therefore he has begotten wisdom. But it is to be understood in this way: God is a spiritual substance: therefore he is by nature apt to know: therefore if he does not have wisdom, he is a foolish thing; but he is not a foolish thing: therefore he has wisdom; but there is no wisdom without the Word, and there is no Word unless it proceeds from a mind and is thus generated: therefore, from the first, if he has wisdom, it is necessary that wisdom be begotten. And all these inferences must be understood as immediate.

DISTINCTIO IV.

Whether God the Father begot himself as God.

Here a sufficiently necessary question arises. For it is established and irrefragably true that God the Father begot the Son. Therefore the question is raised whether it should be granted that God begot God. For if God begot God, it seems that he either begot himself as God or begot another. If he begot another God, then there is not only one God; but if he begot himself, then some thing begot itself.

In response to this we say that it is sound and catholic to grant that one begot one, and that God begot God, because God the Father begot God the Son. For it is also written in the Creed: "Light from Light, true God from true God." But as for the addition — therefore he begot himself as God or another God — we say that neither is to be granted. That he did not beget another God is manifest, because there is only one God. But that he did not beget himself, Augustine shows in the first book of *On the Trinity*, saying: "Those who think it within God's power to beget himself err all the more, in that not only is God not like this, but neither is any spiritual or bodily creature. For there is no thing that begets itself so as to be"; and therefore it is not to be believed or said that God begot himself.

But garrulous reasoners still object, saying: if God the Father begot God, he either begot the God who is God the Father, or the God who is not God the Father. If he begot the God who is not God the Father: therefore there is a God who is not God the Father: therefore there is not only one God. But if he begot the God who is God the Father: therefore he begot himself.

To this we respond by determining this proposition as they put it: if God the Father begot God, either the God who is God the Father or the God who is not God the Father. For this can be understood soundly or perversely; and therefore the answer must be as follows: that God the Father begot the God who is the Father himself — this we say is false; and we grant the other, namely that he begot the God who is not the Father; nor did he therefore beget another God, nor is the one who was begotten another God than the Father, but one God with the Father. But if it is added: he begot the God who is not God the Father — here we draw a distinction, because this can be understood in two ways: he begot the God who is not God the Father, namely God the Son, who as Son is not the Father, who is God; this sense is true. But if it is understood thus: he begot the God who is not God the Father, that is, who is not the God who is the Father; this sense is false. For one and the same God is Father and Son and Holy Spirit; and conversely, the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit is one God.

Cap. II.

Whether the Trinity is predicated of one God just as one God is predicated of the three persons.

Certain adversaries of the truth, however, grant that the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit, or the three persons, are one God, one substance, but are unwilling to grant that one God or one substance is three persons, saying that the divine substance is predicated of the three persons, but not the three persons of the divine substance. But the catholic faith holds and proclaims both that the three persons are one God, one substance or essence or divine nature, and that one God or the divine essence is three persons. Whence Augustine in the first book of *On the Trinity* says: "Rightly is God himself understood to be the Trinity, blessed and alone powerful." See how expressly he said "God himself is the Trinity," so as to show that God himself is the Trinity and the Trinity is God himself. Likewise in the same work: "In those words of the Apostle, in which, treating of the coming of Christ, he says: whom at the proper times he will show, the blessed and alone powerful, King of kings and Lord of lords, who alone has immortality, etc., neither the Father is properly named, nor the Son, nor the Holy Spirit, but the blessed and alone powerful, that is, the one and only true God, who is the Trinity itself." See, he says openly here as well that the one only true God is the Trinity itself; and if one God is the Trinity, therefore one God is three persons. Likewise in the fifth book of *On the Trinity*: "We say that not three gods but one God is the Trinity itself, most excellent." Likewise in the book called the *Enchiridion to Laurence*, chapter nine: "It is enough for a Christian to believe that the cause of created things, visible or invisible, is none other than the goodness of the Creator, who is the one true God, and that there is no nature that either is not himself or is not from him, and that he is the Trinity, namely the Father and the Son and the Holy Spirit." Likewise Augustine in the sermon on Faith: "We believe that one God is one Trinity of the divine name." The same in the sixth book of *On the Trinity*: "We say that God alone is the Trinity itself." See, by these and many other authorities it is clearly shown that it must be said and believed that one God is the Trinity, and one substance is three persons; just as conversely the Trinity is said to be one God, and three persons are said to be one substance.

Now let us return to the foregoing question, where it was asked whether God the Father begot himself as God or another God. To this we say that neither is to be granted. Yet Augustine says in the *Letter to Maximus* that God the Father begot himself as another, in these words: "The Father, in order to have a Son from himself, did not diminish himself, but so begot from himself another himself, that he remained whole in himself and was in the Son as great as he is alone." This can be understood thus: that is, he begot from himself another who is other than himself, not indeed another God, but another person; or he begot himself as another, that is, he begot another who is what he himself is. For even if the Father is other than the Son, he is not however other in nature than the Son, but one.

COMMENTARY ON DISTINCTION IV.

Questions from the comparison of generation to the essential concrete term, namely the name *God*.

*Here a sufficiently necessary question arises*, etc.

DIVISION OF THE TEXT.

In the preceding distinction the Master proved the Trinity and Unity through fitting likenesses and arguments. In the present distinction the second part is set forth, in which he resolves incidental doubts. And a doubt arises from the fact that in divine things there is trinity and unity, and thus something distinguishing and something distinguished, something undistinguished, such as substantial terms. A doubt therefore arises from the comparison of the distinguishing property to the substantial term. Now this part has two sections. In the first he raises a doubt from the comparison of the distinguishing property to the substance or essence; in the second, to its power, below at Distinction Six: *Furthermore, a question is usually raised*.

Likewise the first part has two sections, because substance can be signified in the concrete, as by the name *God*, or in the abstract, as by the name *essence*. He therefore first raises the question from the comparison of generation to the name *God*, and second, to the name *essence*, below at Distinction Five: *After these things it is asked whether it is to be granted*, etc.

Now this distinction has four parts, corresponding to the four matters touched upon therein. In the first, supposing that this is true — God begot God — the question is raised concerning: he begot himself or another; and he resolves it by refutation. In the second he asks concerning: he begot the God who is God the Father, or who is not God the Father; and he resolves this by making a distinction on the part of the predicate, at: *But garrulous reasoners still object*. In the third he asks concerning: God is the Trinity, and proves by many authorities that this is true, on the occasion of the foregoing, at: *Certain adversaries of the truth, however*. In the fourth he returns to his original proposal, namely to the first question raised; he adds to the first solution, namely, that although it is not to be granted — he begot himself, or another — separately, yet it can be granted conjointly, at: *Now let us return to the foregoing question*.

TREATMENT OF THE QUESTIONS.

For the understanding of what the Master treats in the present distinction, four things are asked.

First, whether this expression is to be granted in divine things: God begot God.

Second, whether the unity of essence admits this expression: God begot another God, or God is other than God.

Third, the question is raised concerning the consignification of the name *God*, namely whether grammatically we can say *several gods*.

Fourth and last, the question is raised concerning the supposition of the name *God*, whether it supposits for a person or for the essence.

### Notes

- **Chunk scope.** The text spans three distinct literary units: (1) the body of I Sent., d. 3, p. 2, a. 2, q. 2 (with its *Dubia*); (2) the text of Distinction IV as Bonaventure quotes/recites Peter Lombard; and (3) Bonaventure's own *Commentarius* on Distinction IV (the *Divisio textus* and *Tractatio quaestionum* outline). These are translated in sequence without reordering.

- ***Fundameata.invicem*** (opening). OCR artifact: "Fundameata" is a marginal gloss ("Fundamenta") bleeding into the body text. Silently omitted; the sentence reads naturally without it.

- **"Quantity of love" / "quantity of knowledge"** (obj. 2). The Latin *quantitas amoris… quantitatem notitiae* uses *quantitas* in the sense of degree or intensity rather than physical magnitude; translated accordingly.

- **"Connnatural" (*connaturales*).** Bonaventure's technical term for habits that co-arise with the soul's nature rather than being externally acquired. Rendered "connnatural" throughout to preserve the precision.

- **Dub. II, *quo est* passage.** The phrase *quo est dat animae esse generalissimum* is syntactically compressed and the manuscripts diverge (see apparatus). I have read *quo est* as an ablative absolute or instrumental phrase referring to the soul's essential act, translating "what gives being to the soul in the most general sense." This is genuinely ambiguous; some manuscripts supply *potest intelligi in quantum* after *quo est*.

- **Dub. III, genitive discussion.** Bonaventure analyzes three uses of the genitive (*possessionis*, *declarationis essentiae*, *intransitive*) to explain why "three persons of one God" is false while "three persons of one essence" is true. The grammatical register of this discussion is preserved in the translation.

- **Dist. IV, *garruli ratiocinatores*.** Literally "garrulous reasoners" — a polemical term for dialectical objectors who press logical consequences without theological discretion. Translated literally, as Bonaventure's rhetorical register is part of the text.

- **Augustine, *Letter to Maximus*, *de se alterum*.** The phrase *genuit de se alterum se* is deliberately ambiguous and Bonaventure's two-part gloss (*alterum a se* / *alterum qui hoc est quod ipse*) unpacks this. Both interpretations are rendered as Bonaventure presents them.

- **Scholion and critical apparatus sections** are marked [Scholion omitted] and [Critical apparatus omitted] per instructions.
