# I Sent., d. 3, p. 2, a. 2, q. 2 — Opus Translation

## English

DIST. III. P. II. ART. II. QUAEST. II.

QUAESTIO II.
*Whether mind, knowledge, and love have order, equality, and consubstantiality.*

Second, the question is asked about these in comparison to one another, according to the threefold comparison that Augustine posits — namely, of order, equality, and consubstantiality — and which the Master recounts in the text. There is order among these, because the mind is the parent, knowledge is the offspring, and the third is love proceeding from both. Equality is also present there, because the mind knows itself as much as it is, and loves itself as much as it knows itself. Consubstantiality likewise; whence Augustine in the ninth book of *On the Trinity*: "We are reminded, if we can see it in any way, that these exist in the soul substantially — not as in a subject, as color in a body, or any quality or quantity. For whatever is such does not exceed the substance in which it is. But the mind, by the love with which it loves itself, can love also something else." And thus he holds that love is consubstantial with the mind.

1. But against this it is objected: First, it seems that in these there is no order, nor origin. For either they are taken as innate habits or as acquired. If as innate, there is no order, because they exist simultaneously with the soul itself. If as acquired, then love precedes knowledge — for no one acquires or strives to learn anything unless he loves knowing. Whence Augustine at the end of the ninth book of *On the Trinity*: "The offspring of the mind is preceded by an appetite, by which, through seeking and finding what we wish to know, the offspring is born, which is knowledge itself." Therefore either there is no order, or it is not such an order.

2. Likewise, it seems that there is no equality there. For either knowledge and love are taken in comparison to lower things or to the soul. If to lower things, it is manifest that there is no equality there, for we know many things that we do not love. If in comparison to the soul, there is either equality with respect to intensity or with respect to extension. Not with respect to extension — that much is clear, because there is only one [object] there. Therefore with respect to intensity. But that this is false seems apparent, because since we know the soul to be less than God and greater than the body, it sometimes happens that we love it more than God and less than the body; and thus the quantity of love does not follow the quantity of knowledge.

3. Likewise, that there is no consubstantiality there seems apparent, because love and knowledge are habits and are qualities. Therefore it seems that they differ essentially from the mind itself.

4. Likewise, Augustine's argument is that they are not in the soul as accidents, because they extend beyond it. But this amounts to nothing, because accidents extend beyond [their subject] — as heat by heating and color by altering sight. Moreover, a person knows certain knowable things by acquired knowledge, which is an accident, and thus it extends beyond [its subject].

CONCLUSION.

*Mind, knowledge, and love have order, equality, and consubstantiality.*

I respond: It must be said that, as Augustine establishes, in these there is order, equality, and consubstantiality.

Now order is attended to in these connatural habits of the soul in comparison to their acts, just as order is posited in faith, hope, and charity, even though they are infused simultaneously.

1. And thus what is objected to the contrary is clear, because there is no order in the habits themselves considered absolutely, but through their relation to acts.

Similarly, there is equality there according to the soul's turning back upon itself and the perfection of the aforesaid habits. Whence Augustine says in the ninth book of *On the Trinity* that there is no equality in these habits except insofar as they are perfect.

2. And thus the solution to the objection about love is clear, because that love is not perfect love, but libidinous and disordered. — Or it may be said that they are equal insofar as they are connatural; for as much as one is capable of or disposed to knowing oneself, so much to loving oneself. But regarding acquired habits, whether bad or good, this is not true; and the discussion does not concern these.

Similarly, the third [property] is present there — namely, consubstantiality — because, as was said above, the love and knowledge connatural to the soul are [of the soul] insofar as it turns back upon itself; and thus they add nothing at all beyond the powers themselves. For by the fact that the soul is present to itself, it has knowledge; by the fact that it is one with itself, it has the habit of love. And therefore, just as the powers are consubstantial with the soul, as was seen above, so too are habits of this kind. Hence, even though they may seem to designate the mode of a habit or quality, in reality they add nothing beyond the powers.

3. And thus the response to the objection is clear — that they are not qualities in that way.

4. To the objection about Augustine's argument: it must be said that his argument does not principally conclude that love or knowledge exist substantially in the soul — and this is because by parity of reasoning the same could be said and objected about every love. Rather, it concludes as a consequence, which is clear as follows: When love extends beyond its subject by loving another, this occurs through the power of substance — just as [love] does not exist per se except through substance. If, therefore, love and knowledge are extended through substantial power, and these [substantial powers] are intelligence and will; and the love by which the soul loves itself is identical with the will itself; and similarly, the knowledge by which it knows itself is nothing other than intelligence — it remains, therefore, that love and knowledge with respect to the self are consubstantial with the mind itself.

## Notes

- This is the Augustinian *mens–notitia–amor* triad as vestige of the Trinity (from *De Trinitate* IX)
- The three properties mirror the Trinity: order (Father begets Son, Spirit proceeds from both), equality (co-equal persons), consubstantiality (one substance)
- Key distinction: the *connatural* habits of self-knowledge and self-love are consubstantial with the soul (they add nothing beyond the powers of intelligence and will), unlike *acquired* habits which are accidental
- The objection about love preceding knowledge (you must love knowing before you can know) is resolved by distinguishing the order of habits from the order of acts
- The equality holds only for *perfect* habits — disordered love (loving body more than God) breaks the proportion, but this is acquired/disordered love, not the connatural self-love
- OCR artifacts: `haheant` for *habeant*, `aeqmlitatem` for *aequalitatem*, `consuhstantialitalem` for *consubstantialitatem*, `islis` for *istis*, `secuudum` for *secundum*, `acqidsitis` for *acquisitis*
