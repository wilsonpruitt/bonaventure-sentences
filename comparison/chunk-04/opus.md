# I Sent., d. 13, a. un., q. 1–2 — Opus Translation

## English

DIST. XIII. ART. UNICUS QUAEST. I.

[p. 231]

...improper; therefore truly and properly there is procession in the divine persons.

2. Likewise, in the divine persons there is most truly an origin, and therefore also a production. But to every active production there corresponds a passive production; and passive production is procession. Therefore, from the first premise, in the divine persons there is truly and properly [procession].

3. Likewise, our love both is love and is from another, and its going forth from another is truly and properly expressed by the word *proceeding*. Since, therefore, the divine love, which is the Holy Spirit, both truly and properly is love and is from another, just as the name of love befits him most properly, so too procession seems to befit him.

4. Likewise, for the complete account of procession, these two things are required: that it be from another and that it tend toward another. But the love that is the Holy Spirit does not proceed from the Father insofar as he loves himself, nor from the Son insofar as he loves himself, but insofar as the one loves the other — because he is the bond. Therefore the Holy Spirit is the love by which the one loving tends toward another. Therefore he is love both from another and toward another, and these two things comprise the account of perfect procession. Therefore there is procession in the divine persons.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. *Procession*, from the very meaning of its name, implies distancing and withdrawal — whence *processio* is, as it were, *procul cessio* [a going far off], just as *praecessio* [precedence] implies going before. But just as in the divine persons there is no precedence on account of the supreme simultaneity, so there is no distancing on account of the supreme unity. Therefore, just as the name of *precedence* is not applied in the divine persons, neither should the name of *procession* be.

2. Likewise, in creatures, *procession* by the nature of its genus implies motion, and therefore indigence and imperfection. But no such name ought to be transferred to God; therefore neither should *procession*.

3. Likewise, *creation* designates a special difference of procession, and a difference that adds nobility, because it designates the going forth of a thing from the Creator, who is the most noble cause. But passive creation is in no way found in the divine persons — for no person is said to be created. Therefore, by parity of reasoning, neither is procession.

4. Likewise, procession in creatures is said only in two ways — namely, local and causal. Local procession is that which occurs in progressive motion; causal is that of an effect from a cause. But local procession cannot be transferred to the divine, because no change according to place, whether in general or in particular, falls upon God. Moreover, since such procession is from one [place] into another, it would be necessary to posit that the Holy Spirit would not eternally proceed from the Father and the Son. Therefore local procession is not found [in God]. But local procession is nobler than causal, because it belongs to a complete being and proceeds from an intrinsic principle. Therefore, by argument *a maiori*, causal procession is not found in God either — and thus no mode of procession is found [in God].

CONCLUSION.

*The account of procession is truly, properly, and perfectly found in the divine persons.*

I respond: It must be said that procession in creatures designates a relation to the principle from which [something proceeds] — as a ray is said to proceed from the sun, or a flower from a tree — and that it implies change is accidental to it. Similarly, by the meaning of the name, it designates a relation to the terminus toward which; and that it implies distancing is accidental to it.

Since, therefore, both relations are found in the divine persons — for the proceeding person has a relation to the principle from which, and the proceeding love has a relation to the beloved (namely, that love which is the bond and charity) — therefore truly and properly and perfectly the account of procession is found in the divine persons. Hence the arguments adduced for this position are to be conceded.

1. To the first objection to the contrary — that procession implies distancing — it must be said that this is true in creatures, in which, through approach to the terminus toward which, there occurs a distancing from the terminus from which, on account of the creature's finitude, circumscription, and distance. But it is not so in God.

2. To the objection that it implies motion in creatures: it must be said that it is not transferred by reason of the motion it implies, but by reason of the twofold relation, which — although it cannot exist in creatures without motion on account of imperfection — nevertheless exists in the Creator.

[p. 232]

3. To the objection about creation: it must be said that *creation*, by its primary imposition, designates a going forth from nothing. Therefore its signification can in no way be preserved in God, neither properly nor by transference. But this is not the case with *generation* and *procession*.

4. To the objection that neither causal nor local [procession is found in God]: it must be said that rather, causal procession in one way has a likeness to local procession. For causal procession in one way terminates in the one proceeding, such that it regards nothing further — as when one says, "A son proceeds from a father." In another way, the effect regards something as a terminus — and thus love proceeds from the lover into the beloved, and in a certain way it agrees with local procession (because it regards a terminus toward which), yet in a certain way it differs (because it does not regard it as though received in it, but as an object). And since both the relation and the emanation are truly found in the divine persons, hence that emanation is said to be according to the likeness of causal emanation. But the likeness of local procession falls there only remotely — and therefore the Greeks were deceived.

And to the point that local procession is more perfect: it must be said that local procession always has imperfection conjoined with it by the very meaning of its name, but originary [procession] does not. And it is true that [local procession] is more perfect among motions, but procession in the divine persons does not designate motion, but origin without motion and change — as was said above regarding generation.

QUAESTIO II.
*Whether the procession of the Holy Spirit is generation.*

Second, the question is whether the procession of the Holy Spirit is generation. And that it is not, appears as follows:

1. Augustine, *On the Trinity* XV: "Just as generation — without any beginning of time and without any change — bestows essence upon the Son, so does procession upon the Holy Spirit." Therefore, if what bestows essence upon the two persons [is not the same], etc.

2. Likewise, this same point appears by reason: for generation is an emanation according to the mode of the fecundity of nature. But, as was proved above, the Holy Spirit proceeds by the mode of liberality and love. Therefore he is not generated; therefore generation is not [his] procession.

3. Likewise, nothing one goes forth from two similar [principles] by way of generation unless one is as father, the other as mother — one as active principle, the other as passive principle. But the Holy Spirit proceeds from two similar [principles]. Therefore, if by way of generation, one is to him as father, the other as mother — which is altogether absurd.

4. Likewise, the Son is his generation and the Holy Spirit is his procession. Therefore, if to proceed were to be generated, the Holy Spirit would be the Son. But the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Son and is called the Spirit of the Son, just as the Son is called [the Son] of the Father. Therefore, if the Son is not the Father, neither is the Holy Spirit the Son; therefore neither is procession generation.

On the contrary: 1. In lower things, generation is a motion toward substance — whence generation is the production of substance. But the procession of the Holy Spirit is the production of substance — or rather, of a hypostasis. Therefore it is generation.

2. Likewise, *to generate* is defined by Damascene as follows: [Generation consists in this, that from the substance of the one generating, offspring of the same substance as the one generating is produced.]

## Notes

- The chunk begins mid-argument (the opening arguments *pro* for Q. 1 are on the preceding page)
- Key distinction: procession has two essential *respectus* (relations) — to the principle *a quo* and to the terminus *ad quem*. The imperfections of motion and distancing are accidental, not essential, to procession.
- "The Greeks were deceived" (*Graeci decepti sunt*) — refers to the *Filioque* controversy. The Greeks (Eastern Orthodox) held that the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father alone, partly because they modeled procession too closely on local motion. Bonaventure argues that divine procession follows causal, not local, analogy.
- Q. 2 asks whether the Holy Spirit's procession can be called *generatio* — Bonaventure will distinguish procession *per modum naturae* (= generation, producing the Son) from procession *per modum voluntatis/amoris* (= spiration, producing the Holy Spirit)
- Q. 2 text breaks off before the *respondeo*
- OCR artifacts: *amof-* for *amor*, *Contba* for *Contra*, *Ilem* for *Item*, *esl* for *est*, *araans* for *amans*, *corapetil* for *competit*
