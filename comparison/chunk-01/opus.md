# I Sent., d. 1, a. 1, q. 1 — Opus Translation

## English

DIST. I. ART. I. QUAEST. I.

[p. 31]

...to which each thing is ordered, and in this sense the Philosopher says: "That whose use is good," etc.

In the second way, *use* is said commonly, and thus *use* is distinguished from *disuse*. In this way it is defined by Victorinus: "Use is an act frequently elicited by a power," and in this way it can be said that it is the act of every power, and adds nothing to the act beyond frequency.

In the third way, *to use* is taken properly, and thus it is distinguished from the habits of memory and intelligence — that is, from natural talent and learning. And thus it is defined by Augustine in the tenth book of *On the Trinity*: "To use is to take up something into the faculty of the will," and thus it designates an act of the will properly.

In the fourth way, it is taken more properly, and thus it is distinguished from the quieting act — that is, from *to enjoy*. And thus it is defined by Augustine: "To use is to take up something into the faculty of the will for the sake of something else," and thus it is an act of the will as referred to something else.

In the fifth way, it is taken most properly, and thus it is distinguished from the disordered act — that is, from *to misuse*. And thus it is defined by Augustine in *On Christian Doctrine*: "To use is to refer what has come into use to that which is to be enjoyed," and thus *to use* designates an act of the will that is both referred and ordered to its end.

The reason, then, for the diverse definitions is the manifold senses of *to use*; and the reason for the manifold senses is its opposition to diverse things.

If, therefore, one asks to which power this act belongs, it must be said that, speaking most commonly and commonly, it is the act of every power — and Augustine does not speak in this way. But properly, more properly, and most properly, it is the act of the will itself.

1. And thus the first objection to the contrary — namely, that it belongs to every power — is clear.

2. To the second objection, that every power is referred to its end through its own act: it must be said that the power which is said *to use* its own act is the one that is master of its own act; and it belongs to such a power to refer its own act, not merely to be referred. And since the will alone is master of its own act and alone is self-moving, it alone is that to which *to use* actively belongs. To the other powers it happens to use materially and passively, because they are referred but do not refer. Hence Augustine says: "To use is to take up something into the faculty of the will" — for *faculty* designates the mastery by which the will can readily exercise its own act as its ruler.

3. To the objection that it belongs to reason to refer: it must be said that there are two ways of referring — namely, by comparison and inference, as premises are referred to a conclusion (and this belongs to reason, and this is not *to use*); and by inclination and ordering to something else (and this properly belongs to the will, because it can take up something either by resting in it or by intending something else — and this latter is *to refer*, by which *to use* is defined).

4. To the objection about art: it must be said that art designates both what pertains to operation and what pertains to speculation. Insofar as it designates what pertains to speculation, it does not involve *use*, and thus belongs to reason. But insofar as it designates what pertains to operation, it extends to the will and governs it in using.

QUAESTIO II.
*Whether every creature is to be used.*

Second, having seen what *to use* is in its essence, the question is asked about *what is to be used* — namely, whether every creature is to be used. And that it is so appears in this way:

1. Proverbs 16[:4]: *The Lord has made all things for himself*. Therefore all things are orderable to God; but everything orderable to God can be ordered to him; but by ordering what is orderable to God, we rightly use it. Therefore, if all things can be ordered, all things are to be used.

2. Likewise, being and good are convertible, as Dionysius holds. But all things are beings; therefore all things are good. But every good thing is to be loved; therefore every creature is to be loved — but not for its own sake; therefore for the sake of another. But this is *to use*; therefore every creature can be used.

3. Likewise, every virtue can be rightly used. Therefore the work of every virtue is right use. But the use of some virtue pertains to the evil of punishment — as patience — and of some virtue to the evil of fault — as penance. Therefore all evils, both of fault and punishment, can be rightly used; therefore much more so all goods; therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, "Opposites are apt to occur in the same subject." But all things can be misused; therefore all things can be rightly used; therefore every creature is to be used.

ON THE CONTRARY: 1. Charity is that by which we rightly use, because all things that are rightly ordered are ordered to their end through charity. But as Augustine says in *On Christian Doctrine*: "Only four things are to be loved from charity, and only three created things — namely, ourselves, our neighbor, and our own body." Therefore only three things are referred or ordered to their end through charity. Since there is no right use except through charity, therefore only three things are to be used — and therefore not every creature.

2. Likewise, all things that we use are subject to our will. But some things are not subject to our will, such as necessary and perpetual things. Therefore these are not to be used.

3. Likewise, everything that we rightly use, we do with a good end. But some things can be done with no good end — such as lying and similar things "which, once named, are conjoined with evil." Therefore such things are not to be used.

4. Likewise, virtue cannot be badly used, as is clear from its definition. Therefore, by the argument from opposites, the evil of fault — that is, vices — cannot be rightly used. Therefore not all things other than God are to be used.
