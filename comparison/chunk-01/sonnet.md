# I Sent., d. 1, a. 1, q. 2

## English Translation

**DISTINCTION I. ARTICLE I. QUESTION II.**

---

**Whether Every Created Thing Is to Be Used**

Second, having seen what *use* (*uti*) is in its essence, the question is raised concerning *using*, namely, whether every created thing is to be used. And that it is, seems to be shown in the following way.

**In favor of the position:**

1\. Proverbs 16[:4]: *The Lord has made all things for himself* [Prov. 16:4]; therefore all things are capable of being ordered to God; but whatever is capable of being ordered to God can in fact be ordered; but in ordering what is orderable to God we rightly use it: therefore, if all things can be ordered, all things are to be used.

2\. Likewise, being and the good are convertible, as Dionysius holds [*De Divinis Nominibus*, c. 5]; but all things are beings: therefore all things are good. But everything good is to be loved: therefore every created thing is to be loved; but it is not to be loved for its own sake: therefore for the sake of another; but this is to use: therefore it is possible to use every created thing.

3\. Likewise, every virtue can be rightly used [cf. Augustine, *De Libero Arbitrio* II.18–19]; therefore the work of every virtue is right use; but the use of certain virtues is with respect to the evil of punishment, as patience, and of certain others with respect to the evil of fault, as penance: therefore it is possible to rightly use all evils — both evils of fault and evils of punishment — and much more, then, goods: therefore, etc.

4\. Likewise, "opposites are naturally concerned with the same subject" [Aristotle, *Categories*, on Opposites]; but it is possible to misuse all things: therefore it is possible to rightly use all things: therefore every created thing is to be used.

---

**On the contrary:**

1\. Charity is that by which we rightly use things, for all things that are rightly ordered are ordered to their end through charity; but as Augustine says in *On Christian Doctrine* [I.23.22]: "Only four things are to be loved out of charity, and only three of these are created: ourselves, our neighbor, and our own body": therefore only three things are referred or ordered to their end through charity — since there is no right use except through charity — and therefore only three things are to be used; therefore not every created thing.

2\. Likewise, all things that we use are subject to our will; but there are certain things that are not subject to our will, such as necessary and eternal things: therefore those things are not to be used.

3\. Likewise, everything that we rightly use we do for a good end; but there are certain things that cannot be done for any good end — such as lying and things of that kind, which, "as soon as they are named, are bound up with evil" [Aristotle, *Ethics* II.6]: therefore such things are not to be used.

4\. Likewise, virtue cannot be badly used, as is clear from its definition [Augustine, *De Libero Arbitrio* II.18–19]: therefore, by contrast, things contrary to virtue — evil of fault, or vices — cannot be rightly used: therefore not all things besides God are to be used.

---

**CONCLUSION.**

*We cannot use every created thing as an instrument, nor as a habit, nor as an act; yet we can use every thing as an object, but in four ways: either by doing, by accepting, by enduring, or by rejecting.*

I respond: It must be said that to use something is said in four ways. Either as an instrument by which we operate — as someone uses a tool or organ — and in this way it is not possible to use all things; for instance, purely natural powers cannot be used in this way, taking *use* in the proper sense as Augustine takes it. Or as a habit by which we are regulated — for instance, a virtue — and in this way it is not possible to rightly use all things, as for instance a habit of vice, by which one is rather prone to deviate. Or as an act by which we are moved, and in this way it is not possible to rightly use every act, as for instance those acts which…

[*The Latin text as provided breaks off at this point.*]

## Notes

**On the structure:** This is Question II of Article I of Distinction I. The text provided begins mid-sentence (opening with *ad quam unaquaeque res ordinatur*), which belongs to the tail end of Question I's resolution — specifically the section on the five modes of *uti*. The main body of Question II begins at *Secundo, viso quid sit uti per essentiam*. I have translated from that point as the full quaestio.

**"For himself" vs. "for itself" (Prov. 16:4):** The Vulgate reads *propter semetipsum operatus est Dominus* ("the Lord worked all things for himself"). Bonaventure's text reads *Deus* where the Vulgate has *Dominus*; the Quaracchi note flags this variant. I have followed Bonaventure's text.

**"Being and the good are convertible" (Dionysius):** This is a standard scholastic citation of Pseudo-Dionysius, *De Divinis Nominibus* c. 5, which Bonaventure uses to ground the transcendental convertibility of *ens* and *bonum*. The Quaracchi note cites the Greek text: "It does not say that the good is one thing and being another."

**"Quae mox nominata coniuncta sunt malo":** This is a citation of Aristotle's *Nicomachean Ethics* II.6 (in the medieval Latin translation), referring to acts intrinsically disordered by their species. I have translated the sense rather than giving a fully literal rendering of this embedded quotation.

**"Quatuor diligenda":** Bonaventure cites Augustine's *De Doctrina Christiana* I.23 and I.26, where Augustine enumerates four objects of love: (1) what is above us [God], (2) what we ourselves are [our soul], (3) what is beside us [our neighbor], (4) what is below us [our body]. Of these, only three are created. Bonaventure uses this to constrain the scope of *uti*.

**Truncation:** The text as supplied ends mid-sentence within the *Respondeo* section, at the discussion of the third mode of use (acting by an act). The fourth mode — using something as an *object* — including the fourfold subdivision (*operando, acceptando, tolerando, respuendo*) stated in the Conclusion, would appear in the continuation of the *Respondeo* not included here.

**Q. I tail (opening lines):** The passage that opens the supplied Latin (*ad quam unaquaeque res ordinatur… uti est id quod in usum venerit referre…*) belongs to the resolution of Question I, covering the five modes of *uti* and their corresponding definitions (from Victorinus, Augustine's *De Trinitate*, and *De Doctrina Christiana*). I have translated this section as a preface since it appears in the supplied text, though it is technically the conclusion of Q. I rather than the body of Q. II.
