### English

DIST. XIX. P. I. ART. UNICUS. QUAEST. I.

And for the understanding of this it is to be noted that equal and unequal is the proper passion consequent upon quantity. Now quantity is said in two ways: properly, namely quantity of mass, and by transference, quantity of power. And because equal and unequal is the proper passion of quantity, it follows upon both kinds of quantity: wherever therefore quantity of power is to be posited, there equality or inequality is to be posited. Now this quantity of power is posited in spiritual things and is found in the highest degree in divine things, because this quantity is not repugnant to simplicity but consonant with it; and similarly neither is the equality consequent upon this quantity. And because the more complete part of a contradiction is to be attributed to God, and equality is prior to inequality: therefore equality is to be posited in divine things; and the arguments to this effect are to be granted.

1. To what is therefore objected on the contrary — that where there is equality, there is divisibility — it must be said that this is true of the equality that follows upon quantity of mass.

2. To what is objected second — that where there is equality, there is quantity — it must be said that this is true either according to genus or according to species; and although the name of quantity is not transferred, nonetheless the name of the species is transferred, as magnitude.

3. To what is objected — that where there is equality, there is finitude — it must be said that equality in creatures imports two things, namely commensuration and privation of excess; the first pertains to incompleteness by reason of limitation, the second to perfection: therefore it is transferred by reason of the second, not the first.

Yet it could also be said that the infinite, which is incomprehensible to a finite intellect, is according to truth comprehensible by an infinite intellect, and therefore commensurable to the infinite and is finite with respect to it. And from this it does not follow: it is commensurable to the infinite, therefore it is simply finite; rather, it is all the more infinite: and there is there a fallacy of qualified and unqualified.

4. To what is objected — that where there is equality, there is diversity — it must be said that there is diversity of supposits and diversity of form. For equality and likeness require diversity of supposits, but not diversity of form; rather, they require unity of form. But the fact that in lower things a numerical multiplication of form or quantity follows upon diversity of supposits — this pertains to imperfection; and therefore everything that pertains to perfection is transferred to God. Yet properly speaking there is not diversity among the supposits, but distinction.

QUESTION II.
Whether there is supreme equality in divine things.

Second, the question is raised whether there is supreme equality in divine things. And that there is, seems to be shown in the following way.

1. As is said by Augustine in the book *On Faith to Peter*: "One is not said to be greater than another unless either because he precedes in age, or because he exceeds in magnitude, or because he surpasses in power"; but none of these is found in divine things, as Augustine and the Master prove: therefore there is no inequality there: therefore there is supreme equality.

2. Likewise, no greater equality can be thought than where one is equal to one and one to many and one to all; but in divine things the Son is as great as the Father, and the Son is as great as the Father and the Holy Spirit, and also as great as all three: therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, unity in quantity, as has been said, produces equality: therefore where there is supreme unity, there is supreme equality; but this is the case in divine things: therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, rest is only at the summit; but rest is found in that equality through the reduction of all things to it: therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. Augustine in the book of the *Eighty-three Questions*: "If all things were equal, all things would not be": therefore equality in every respect removes perfection. If therefore nothing is to be posited in God that is repugnant to perfection, there is no equality in every respect among the divine persons.

2. Likewise, the equality that is attended to according to both continuous and discrete quantity is greater than that which is attended to according to continuous quantity alone; but in divine things there is no equality according to discrete quantity, because there is trinity and thus oddness of number: therefore in divine things there is not equality in every respect.

3. Likewise, the equality that is attended to according to power and wisdom and goodness is greater than that which is attended to according to wisdom and power alone; but in divine things there is no equality according to goodness: therefore there is not supreme equality there. Proof of the minor: the good is diffusive of itself; but the Son diffuses himself more than the Holy Spirit, because he produces one equal to himself, and likewise the Father more than the Son: therefore, etc.

The question is therefore raised: why does Augustine not show equality in wisdom and goodness? And again, why not from the side of place, as he does from that of magnitude and also of the other differences of quantity? Since he does not do this, he seems to assign not equality in every respect, but only in part; or if he shows equality in every respect, he proceeds insufficiently.

CONCLUSIO.

The equality of the divine persons is supreme in eternity with respect to origin, in magnitude with respect to all that they have in themselves, and in power with respect to effects.

### Notes

- **Chunk scope.** The text spans the conclusion of Q. I (responses to objections) and the full body of Q. II up to its *Respondeo*, which breaks off mid-sentence at "assigned by Augu—" (Augustine). The translation follows the Latin as given.

- ***Passio*** (*propria passio*). A technical Aristotelian term for a property that necessarily follows from and belongs to a subject, analogous to "proper accident" or "necessary concomitant." Rendered "proper passion" to preserve the scholastic register.

- **"Fallacy of qualified and unqualified"** (*fallacia secundum quid et simpliciter*). Bonaventure invokes the Aristotelian fallacy (*Sophistical Refutations* c. 4–5) of inferring an unqualified predicate from a qualified one: being commensurable to an infinite intellect does not entail being simply finite. The Quaracchi apparatus notes cod. X makes this explicit with *fallacia secundum quid et simpliciter*; Bonaventure's text signals it with *et est ibi quid et simpliciter*, translated accordingly.

- ***Suppositum* / *distinctio*.** Bonaventure carefully distinguishes *diversitas suppositorum* (diversity of supposits, appropriate to creatures) from *distinctio* (distinction, proper to divine persons). The note at the end of Q. I — "properly speaking there is not diversity among the supposits, but distinction" — is a precise terminological correction preserved in the translation.

- ***Imparitas*** (Q. II, obj. 2). Literally "oddness" or "unevenness"; here it refers to the fact that three is an odd number, used to argue that discrete numerical equality does not hold among the Trinity. Translated "oddness of number."

- **"The good is diffusive of itself"** (*bonum est diffusivum sui*). A standard Dionysian axiom in scholastic theology, drawn from Pseudo-Dionysius. Bonaventure uses it to press a difficulty: if diffusiveness measures goodness, the Father and Son exceed the Spirit, undermining equality in goodness. The axiom is translated as a standard formula.

- ***Omnimoda aequalitas*.** Literally "equality in every respect" or "total equality." Rendered consistently as "equality in every respect" to preserve the force of Bonaventure's terminological distinction between *summa aequalitas* and *omnimoda aequalitas*.
