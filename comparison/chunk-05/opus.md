# I Sent., d. 19, p. 1, a. un., q. 1–2 — Opus Translation

## English

DIST. XIX. P. I. ART. UNICUS QUAEST. I.

And for understanding this, it should be noted that *equal* and *unequal* are proper attributes consequent upon quantity. Now *quantity* is said in two ways: properly — namely, quantity of mass — and by transference, quantity of power. And because [equality] is a proper attribute of quantity, it follows upon both kinds of quantity. Therefore, wherever quantity of power is to be posited, there equality or inequality is to be posited. Now this quantity of power is posited in spiritual things and is found supremely in the divine persons, because this quantity does not conflict with simplicity but is consonant with it; and likewise, neither does the equality consequent upon this quantity. And because the more complete side of a contradiction is to be attributed to God, and equality is prior to inequality, therefore equality is to be posited in the divine persons; and the arguments for this are to be conceded.

1. To the first objection to the contrary — that where there is equality, there is divisibility — it must be said that this is true of the equality that follows upon quantity of mass.

2. To the second objection — that where there is equality, there is quantity — it must be said that this is true either according to genus or according to species; and although the name *quantity* is not transferred [to God], the name of its species is transferred, such as *magnitude*.

3. To the objection that where there is equality, there is finitude: it must be said that equality in creatures implies two things — namely, co-termination and the privation of excess. The first belongs to incompleteness by reason of limitation; the second belongs to perfection. Therefore it is transferred by reason of the second, not the first.

It could also be said, however, that the infinite which is incomprehensible is, according to truth, comprehensible by the infinite, and therefore commensurable with the infinite and is finite to it. And from this it does not follow: "It is commensurable with the infinite, therefore finite" — rather, the more so infinite. And here there is [a fallacy of] *secundum quid* and *simpliciter*.

4. To the objection that where there is equality, there is diversity: it must be said that there is diversity of supposits and diversity of form. For equality and likeness, diversity of supposits is required, but not diversity of form — rather, unity [of form]. Now, that in lower things the numeration of form or quantity follows upon the diversity of supposits belongs to imperfection; and therefore everything that belongs to perfection is transferred to God. Nevertheless, in the [divine] supposits there is not properly speaking diversity, but distinction.

---

QUAESTIO II.
*Whether in the divine persons there is supreme equality.*

Second, the question is whether in the divine persons there is supreme equality. And that there is, appears in this way:

1. As Augustine says in the book *On Faith to Peter*: "No one is said to be greater than another except either because he precedes in age, or exceeds in magnitude, or surpasses in power." But none of these obtains in the divine persons, as Augustine and the Master prove. Therefore there is nothing of inequality there; therefore there is supreme equality.

2. Likewise, no greater equality can be conceived than where one is equal to one, and one to many, and one to all. But in the divine persons, the Son is as great as the Father, and the Son is as great as the Father and the Holy Spirit, and [the Son] is even as great as all three. Therefore, etc.

3. Likewise, unity in quantity, as has been said, implies equality. Therefore, where there is supreme unity, there is supreme equality. But this obtains in the divine persons; therefore, etc.

4. Likewise, there is no ultimate point except in what is supreme. But there is an ultimate point in that equality, through the reduction of all things to it. Therefore, etc.

On the contrary: 1. Augustine, in the book of *Eighty-Three Questions*: "If all things were equal, not all things would exist." Therefore, total equality removes perfection. If, therefore, nothing is to be posited in God that conflicts with perfection, in the divine persons there is not total equality.

2. Likewise, greater is the equality that is attended to according to both continuous and discrete quantity than that which is according to continuous quantity alone. But in the divine persons there is no equality according to discrete quantity, because there is a trinity there — and thus an imparity. Therefore in the divine persons there is not total equality.

3. Likewise, greater is the equality that is attended to according to power, wisdom, and goodness than that which is according to wisdom and power alone. But in the divine persons there is no equality according to goodness; therefore there is not supreme equality there. Proof of the minor: the good is self-diffusive. But the Son diffuses himself more than the Holy Spirit, because he produces one equal to himself; and likewise the Father [diffuses himself] more than the Son. Therefore, etc.

The question is therefore asked: Why does Augustine not demonstrate equality in wisdom and goodness? And again, why not from the standpoint of place, as with magnitude and the other differences of quantity? Since he does not do this, he does not seem to establish total equality, but only in part — or if he does establish total equality, he proceeds insufficiently.

[CONCLUSION.]

*The equality of the divine persons is supreme: in eternity with respect to origin, in magnitude with respect to all that they have in themselves, and in power with respect to effects.*

I respond: It must be said that in the divine persons there is supreme equality, and [this] supreme [equality] is also established by Augustine...

## Notes

- The chunk begins mid-argument (the opening objections and foundation for Q. 1 are on the previous page)
- Heavy OCR damage throughout: inline marginal notes garbled into the text (e.g., `Acquaiiias at <imM-` = marginal gloss "Aequalitas at..." broken into body), `■ioioiio op-` = marginal marker, `Aequai.ias` = broken "Aequalitas"

- Key theological content: equality in God is grounded in *quantitas virtutis* (quantity of power/perfection), not *quantitas molis* (quantity of mass/extension). The divine persons are equal because they share the same infinite magnitude.
- The fallacy of *secundum quid et simpliciter* (from qualified to unqualified) is invoked to block the inference from "commensurable with the infinite" to "finite"
- "Diversity" vs. "distinction" — Bonaventure insists the divine persons are not *diverse* (implying different forms) but *distinct* (different supposits sharing one form)
- Q. 2 respondeo breaks off before completion
- The self-diffusiveness of the good (*bonum est diffusivum sui*) from Pseudo-Dionysius is a key Bonaventurean principle
